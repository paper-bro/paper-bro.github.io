001Lesson 1 · The problem~6 min

Why even a smart AI can be a bad tutor

A good human tutor does three things most AI chatbots don't. They notice how you got it wrong, they explain at your level, and they choose your next practice to attack exactly that weak spot. A correct final answer alone isn't tutoring — it's just an answer key.

The paper shows why even smart chatbots fail at this. They are built as two separate jobs that never talk to each other. Job (a) is problem-tutoring: you ask a question, the tutor diagnoses your mistake and explains. But that diagnosis is used only in that one turn and then forgotten. Job (b) is question-generation: the system makes new practice problems, but from a generic goal like "make a medium calculus question," not from what you just got wrong.

The root cause: no memory of you. Older systems keep only a rough score like "70% at algebra" instead of your actual reasoning traces — e.g., "keeps forgetting chain rule when trig is inside." Without that detail, every session starts over.

DeepTutor fixes this by adding what the authors call a Hybrid Personalization Engine. Think of it as two memories working together. SKG (Static Knowledge Grounding) remembers what the course teaches — it can search your textbook. DPM (Dynamic Personal Memory) remembers how you learn — it builds a growing learner profile D = (Ds, Dw, Dr) from every interaction.

Those two together close the loop: a tutoring chat leaves traces → traces update your profile Dw (your active weak concepts) → Dw shapes which practice questions are generated next → how you do on those questions refines your profile again → your next explanation is better. Every chat makes the next chat smarter about you.

Try it below: run 5 tutoring sessions in Isolated mode where the tutor has no memory, then switch to Closed-Loop mode where it remembers. Watch which one wastes time repeating what you already know, and which one actually shrinks your gaps.

002Lesson 2 · Knowing the course~8 min

Teaching with the textbook open

Imagine you ask a tutor: “What’s the chain rule for trig?” A normal chatbot tries to answer from memory. It might be right, or it might hallucinate a formula. DeepTutor does something duller and smarter: it opens your actual textbook first.

That “open book” part is called Static Knowledge Grounding, or SKG. Think of it as the tutor’s library card for your course. The paper says DeepTutor starts by cutting each PDF into atomic content units — the smallest useful pieces, and it keeps tables, equations, and images intact instead of smashing them into plain text. So a graph of sin(x²) stays a graph, not a garbled sentence.

Then it builds two different indexes over the exact same pieces. Both index the same knowledge K, but they are good at different things:

🕸️

Knowledge graph G

For explicit links. It knows “this definition page links to that formula page” and “this example uses that theorem.” Great when you ask something factual like “show me the formula.”

🧠

Embedding index B

For meaning. It turns every chunk into numbers (an embedding) so it can find chunks that mean something similar, even if they don’t share keywords. Great for “why does this work?” questions.

At question time the tutor searches both. Graph traversal pulls candidates from G, dense search pulls candidates from B. Now it has two ranked lists that often disagree. How to merge them?

Reciprocal rank fusion, in plain words: give each chunk points for how high it ranked. Rank 1 = lots of points, rank 2 = fewer, rank 10 = crumbs. The recipe uses 1 / (k + rank) so being #1 matters much more than being #5. Add the points from both lists (weighted by how much you trust each list), sort, remove duplicates, and cut off when you hit your token budget. That final merged text is C_rag — the course grounding for this turn.
$$\text{score}(d) = \frac{w_G}{k + \text{rank}_G(d)} + \frac{w_D}{k + \text{rank}_D(d)}$$

Here d is one textbook chunk, rank_G and rank_D are where it placed in each list, k is a small cushion (the paper uses a standard value like 60; we use 3 in the lab so you can see the math), and w_G, w_D balance which search brain to trust more.

One last detail: the tutor never gets unlimited reading time. Before each step it splits a token budget between C_rag (what the course says) and C_mem (what it knows about you, which you’ll meet in Lesson 3). A factual lookup gets more C_rag; a follow-up on your past mistake gets more C_mem.

Ready to feel the two brains disagree? In the lab, flip between a factual and a conceptual question, drag the blend slider between Graph and Dense, and watch the fused C_rag ranking flip. Notice which chunk neither brain alone would have put on top.

003Lesson 3 · Knowing you~9 min

A memory that grows with you

Last lesson we gave the tutor the textbook. Now we give it a memory — not of the textbook, but of you.

Most chatbots treat each message like a fresh start. You make the same algebra slip on Monday and Friday, and the bot explains it the same generic way both times. That's because it has no evolving model of you — at best a score like “70% at trig.” DeepTutor replaces that score with a Trace Forest, called $\mathcal{F}$ in the paper.

Here's the picture: every complete chat you have becomes one tree in the forest. Each tree has three levels, from blurry to sharp:

🌲

Level 1 — Session

The cover page: your question plus a short global summary. “Asked about derivative of $\sin(2x)$, confused on inner derivative.”

🪜

Level 2 — Planning

The middle branches: the sub-goals the tutor made — e.g., “decompose problem, plan: review chain rule in trig.”

🔍

Level 3 — Execution

The leaves: the tiny receipts — tool outputs, which textbook page was cited, whether the check passed or failed.

Why three levels? Because sometimes the tutor needs the big picture (“we've done chain rule twice”), and sometimes it needs the exact receipt (“you dropped the factor $2$ on p.42”). Every node — at every level — gets turned into numbers (an embedding), so the whole forest is searchable by meaning.

How does the tutor search it? With a tiny toolkit the paper calls TraceToolkit:

  • SearchTrace — semantic search: “find moments like chain rule mistake anywhere in the forest.”
  • ListTraces — filter by time, topic, or task type: “show last week's trig sessions.”
  • ReadNodes — open a hit and also pull its ancestors, so you get context, not just a leaf.

A searchable forest is still just storage. The clever part is how it becomes a learner profile $\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_s, \mathcal{D}_w, \mathcal{D}_r)$ — three views that update after each tree is added:

  • $\mathcal{D}_s$ session history: what topics you covered, which paths you took, how you performed over time.
  • $\mathcal{D}_w$ weakness inventory: the evidence-backed list of recurring confusions and active gaps — e.g., “drops inner derivative in $\sin(2x)$ and $\cos(3x)$.”
  • $\mathcal{D}_r$ teaching reflection: notes to its future self about how to teach you — “show $u=2x$ step-by-step, not a one-line answer.”
Key insight: This profile isn't made by asking an LLM to “summarize the chat.” Three specialist memory agents actively query the forest with the toolkit, compare this session to older ones, and only then update $\mathcal{D}_s$, $\mathcal{D}_w$, $\mathcal{D}_r$. That grounds personalization in real trace evidence, not a vague summary.

Finally, not every part of the brain should see everything. Before each step, DeepTutor builds a small personal context $\mathcal{C}_{\text{mem}}$ in two ways: it retrieves the most relevant nodes across levels, and it gives each agent only the slice it needs — the planner gets $\mathcal{D}_s+\mathcal{D}_w$ to make a specific plan, the writer gets $\mathcal{D}_r$ to pick the right depth and tone, the question-maker gets $\mathcal{D}_w$ plus your past difficulty trends.

The same substrate — one forest, one profile, same toolkit — is then reused everywhere else the paper sketches: research, visualizing, writing surfaces, mastery paths, even multi-channel Partners. But even for tutoring alone, this is what closes the loop: your mistakes today shape what the tutor remembers and how it helps tomorrow.

Try it below: grow a tiny forest of 3 sessions, type a query like “chain rule mistake,” and watch hits light up across L1/L2/L3. Then switch the agent on duty — Planner vs Writer vs Question Generator — and see that they literally receive different pieces of your profile.

004Lesson 4 · The teaching move~9 min

Don't just answer — scaffold

Imagine you ask for help with sin(x²) and you wrote the derivative as cos(x²). A one-shot chatbot might just drop the answer — 2x·cos(x²) — and move on. You still don't see why you missed the 2x, so next time you miss it again.

DeepTutor does something different. Instead of one big “think and answer” loop, it splits problem tutoring into three jobs that take turns. Why split? If one loop tries to both investigate deeply and write beautifully, both goals fight for the same limited context window — the tutor either forgets evidence or rambles.

Stage ① Investigate — make a plan just for you. Given your question $q$, the planner first breaks it into meta-questions and gathers two kinds of context: $\mathcal{C}_{\text{rag}}$ from the textbook and $\mathcal{C}_{\text{mem}}$ from your memory (past mistakes, gaps). Together they produce an annotated plan $\mathcal{P} = \langle s_1, \dots, s_K \rangle$ — a short list of sub-goals. Because it sees your actual gap, the plan is specific: “review chain rule in trig — inner $x^2$” not vague “review calculus.” Think of it as a doctor reading your chart before choosing tests.

Stage ② Guided solving — work step by step and save context. A solver tackles each $s_k$ with tools, conditioned on both $\mathcal{C}_{\text{rag}}$ and $\mathcal{C}_{\text{mem}}$. Two tricks keep the growing history from overflowing. Self-notes distill each step into one tiny takeaway that later steps can reuse instead of the full output. Hierarchical compression progressively summarizes finished sub-goals into compact digests, freeing space for the next ones. If the plan turns out wrong, adaptive replanning revises the remaining steps while keeping what’s already done.

The key saving: Without notes and compression, the tutor’s context meter hits 100% and it starts forgetting earlier evidence. With them, it carries only the essence forward — like keeping lecture notes, not every word.

Stage ③ Iterative writing — explain at your level, with receipts. The writer pulls structured evidence from the scratchpad and refines the answer in passes, reconciling conflicts. It adapts depth to your Zone of Proximal Development: beginners get scaffolded, step-by-step derivations; advanced learners get concise summaries that foreground the insight. And every factual claim outside general reasoning carries a traceable citation to retrieved evidence — usually from $\mathcal{K}$, sometimes from the web if enabled.

In the lab, run the three stages in order. Watch the plan $\mathcal{P}$ appear, see self-notes accumulate and the context bar rise, then toggle compression on and off to feel the overflow. Switch the learner level to compare the same solution delivered two ways — both grounded in the same evidence and citations.

005Lesson 5 · The practice move~8 min

The right question at the right difficulty

Imagine you are a quizmaster. Your job is not just to ask any hard question, but the right question for one specific learner. Too easy and they learn nothing. Too off-topic and you waste their time. Too wrong and you teach a mistake.

DeepTutor splits that job into two clear halves: what to ask and how to verify it. This is the practice move, and it mirrors how the teaching move is split into investigate-solve-write.

The key idea: separate what to ask from how to ask and check. That separation makes practice personal and trustworthy.

Stage 4 — Personalized Idea Generation: Before writing any full question, an idea agent looks at both contexts you already met. C_rag is the course context — the textbook pages found by the knowledge graph and dense search. C_mem is the learner context — past mistakes, difficulty trends, and related problems pulled from the Trace Forest, plus the weakness list Dw.

From that joint view it proposes a pool of candidate ideas. Each idea is small and structured: a target concept, a format — MCQ, written-response, or coding — and a personalized rationale like “student dropped the inner derivative in sin(x²), so target chain rule with trig.” An evaluator then filters the pool on clarity, relevance, and diversity. Vague ideas and near-duplicates are pruned. What survives becomes a set of clean templates {Ti} for the next stage.

Stage 5 — Critic-Guided Generation: For each template Ti, a generator builds the real triple (qi, ai, ei) — the question, the answer, and the explanation. It uses C_rag to stay factually grounded and C_mem to calibrate difficulty: a beginner gets a scaffolded one-step check with a hint, an advanced learner gets a concise multi-step transfer item.

Then a structurally separated validator checks it. “Structurally separated” means the validator shares no reasoning chain with the generator. It must independently verify template alignment, factual correctness, and pedagogical soundness. For coding questions it also runs the code in a sandbox. If it fails, the validator returns diagnostic feedback and the item is regenerated until it passes. This avoids the common trap where a model just agrees with itself and misses its own error.

Go deeper: the closed loop. Weaknesses diagnosed during problem tutoring flow into Dw and shape which templates are chosen next. Results on those new questions then update Ds and Dr, so future explanations adapt too.

006Lesson 6 · Building the test~7 min

How to test 'personal' fairly

How do you give a tutor a fair exam when every student is different? Most older tests dodge the question: they check if the tutor’s answer is correct or sounds nice, and they treat the student as one generic “struggler.” That can’t tell you if a tutor truly personalized — adapted to your textbook and your actual confusions.

DeepTutor’s creators built their own test for this, called TutorBench. Think of it like casting actors for a play and then judging how well the tutor teaches each actor. First, they indexed 30 knowledge bases: real university materials. For textbooks (from OpenStax) they split each book into three chapter chunks as separate PDFs; for frontier research they used 6 papers (on topics like DeepSeek-R1 and YOLOv9). Together these cover five domains: humanities, sciences, engineering, business, and frontier research.

For each knowledge base they created 3 learner profiles — beginner, intermediate, advanced — but only as starting points. Each profile is grounded in fine per-topic states: for every topic you are known well, partially known, or unknown, plus your background and purpose for learning. 30 KBs × 3 = 90 profiles in total.

A single gap isn’t just “got it wrong.” TutorBench samples 3 gaps per entry in three flavors, each tied to real source pages:
1. Misconception — believes a false rule.
2. Incomplete understanding — knows the idea but misses a key condition.
3. Missing knowledge — never encountered it. Each stores both a manifestation (how you’d act) and the correct understanding (hidden from the tutor, used only for judging).

Finally they make the actual task you’ll chat about. An LLM proposes 5 candidate tasks of four kinds (about 30% concept understanding, 30% problem solving, 20% application, 20% comparison). Then a strict rejection sampling filter keeps only good ones. It checks three criteria — when in doubt, it rejects:

  • Gap coherence: do the targeted gaps form one sensible topic, not random jumps?
  • Task–gap fit: does doing the task naturally reveal those gaps without forced steering?
  • Conversational naturalness: does it sound like a real request from that learner, with no contradictions?

The sampler regenerates batches until at least 3 tasks pass; human reviewers then check every entry for factual correctness and plausibility. The math is simple: 90 profiles × 3 accepted tasks = 270 tasks overall. During the live evaluation every tutor system sees the same KB pages and task. The profile and gap manifestations become the simulator’s first-person beliefs, while the correct understandings stay hidden for scoring — so the test is fair, source-grounded, and truly student-centric.

007Lesson 7 · Judging fairly~8 min

Let the student simulator decide

How do you test if a tutor is truly personal? You cannot just check if the answer is right. You need to see if it adapts to this specific student over many turns. DeepTutor’s team built a whole acting troupe to do that.

The loop looks like this: a benchmark entry gives a learner profile and a few hidden knowledge gaps. A student simulator turns those gaps into first-person beliefs like “I think input costs shift demand.” It then chats with the tutor for up to 30 turns. It defends its misconceptions, never shows knowledge marked “unknown,” and only says it is done when it really understands. After tutoring, it asks for 5 custom multiple-choice practice questions. All of that — the chat and the questions — is saved as a trace.

A separate LLM judge scores the trace. In the paper this is Claude Sonnet 4.6 at temperature 0, run 3 times and averaged. Gemini 3 Flash powers both the simulator and all the tutor baselines so no tutor gets a smarter brain. The judge uses 10 rubrics, each 1 to 5:

💬

Tutoring side (every turn)

SF Source Faithfulness: is it true to the textbook and cited?
PER Personalization: does it target your confusion?
APP Applicability: can you act on it now?
VID Vividness: does it use tables, steps, or visuals well?
LD Logical Depth: does it show the why, step by step?

✅

Practice side (the 5 questions)

FIT Fitness: does the question hit your diagnosed gap at the right difficulty?
GND Groundedness: are stem, answer, and explanation anchored in the source?
DIV Diversity: is it novel, not rote recall?
ANS Answer Quality: is the key correct and distractors plausible?
CC Cross Concept: does it connect multiple ideas from the session?

Is the robot judge trustworthy? The team checked. They sampled 45 sessions — 9 from each of 5 domains — and had 10 human university annotators do blind A/B comparisons. If the two annotators for a session disagreed, it counted as a tie. Humans and the LLM judge agreed strongly: across the 10 metrics, their DeepTutor win-rates correlated at Pearson r = 0.82, p = 0.0038 and Spearman rho = 0.83, p = 0.0027. Both gave DeepTutor the largest share on all 10 metrics.

A 1-to-5 score is not about being nice. A 1 means generic, uncited, or wrong. A 5 means “this could only have been written for this student from this textbook page.” That is what PER and GND reward.

008Lesson 8 · What happened~7 min

The payoff — and what's still to prove

So did closing the loop actually help? The paper tests DeepTutor on TutorBench — 270 tasks where a simulator pretends to be you, with your exact gaps and textbook pages. Every answer is scored 1–5 on 10 metrics by an LLM judge (checked against human raters).

The headline: Overall Quality 3.91 vs 3.53 for a plain Naive Tutor — +10.76%. That may look small, but the baselines barely moved: adding chain-of-thought was −0.28%, self-refine +1.13%, ReAct −0.28%. Prompting tricks alone didn't make a tutor personal.

Where were the wins biggest? On the tutoring side, Vividness 4.81 vs 3.89 (richer diagrams and tables, not just prose), Personalization 4.59 vs 4.24, and Logical Depth 4.61 vs 3.99. On the practice side, Groundedness 2.96 vs 2.46, Diversity 3.44 vs 2.83, and Cross-Concept 3.38 vs 3.12 — meaning the follow-up questions were more often tied to real source pages and pulled ideas together. This held across all five domains; OQ varied by only 0.16 points.

Ablation = remove a part, see what breaks. DeepTutor has two engines: SKG (knows the textbook) and DPM (remembers you). Remove SKG and grounding collapses — Groundedness drops most, then Source Faithfulness and Cross-Concept. Remove DPM and adaptation collapses — Personalization and Fitness (gap-matched difficulty) drop most. Remove both and the drop is largest. The lesson: SKG anchors what is true; DPM shapes how to teach it to you. They are complementary, not interchangeable.

What about outside TutorBench? The authors turned off both SKG and DPM — so no textbook and no memory of you — and kept only the teaching scaffold: investigate → guided solving → iterative writing. They tried it on five public hard tests (HLE, GPQA-Diamond, LiveBench, GAIA, AA-LCR) with five different backbone models.

A bit deeper: how the 29.4% is computed. For each backbone m, the paper averages the relative lift across benchmark groups:
$$ \mathrm{Avg}\,\Delta_{m}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{s_{m,b}^{\text{DeepTutor}}-s_{m,b}^{\text{base}}}{s_{m,b}^{\text{base}}}\times 100\% $$
In words: for each test b, measure (new − old) / old as a percent, then average. Example given in the paper: Gemini-3-Flash on HLE goes 19.4 → 30.8. Across all five models the average lift was 25.69% to 32.03%, mean about 29.4%. So the scaffold helps general reasoning too, not just personal tutoring.

What's still to prove? The paper is clear about limits: the student is still a simulator, not real learners in a classroom; the multi-step pipeline costs more compute; and the bigger visions — interactive books, proactive Partners that nudge you on Slack or WhatsApp, the Book Engine — are built as reusable parts of the same system but not quantitatively evaluated yet. They need long-term human studies on retention and motivation.

Use the lab to see it yourself: toggle SKG and DPM off and on to watch which bars fall, check that no single domain drove the win, then switch to the Generalization tab to see the same scaffold lift scores on completely different exams — even with personalization turned off.
