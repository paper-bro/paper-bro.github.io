001Lesson 1 • The bottleneck~6 min

Why calculating electrons is so slow — and why it matters

Most useful materials are messy. The alloys that store data on DVDs and in phase-change memory — Ge-Sb-Te, or GST for short — switch between a tidy crystal and a glassy, disordered state. To predict the glass, you can't use a tiny cell with 10 atoms. You need a supercell with 180 to 216 atoms that freezes in the disorder, like a snapshot of a liquid. Do that for 7 different compositions, and for amorphous silicon too, and you quickly need thousands of atoms.

Finding new materials is even bigger. The authors of UniTB screened 16,195 public crystal structures for five jobs — chip wires, lenses, solar absorbers, fast transistors, thermoelectrics — each needing its own property. If each calculation takes hours, a screen takes months.

That cost comes from how DFT, the usual quantum method, works. DFT guesses the electron cloud, builds a Hamiltonian (a big table of energies and hoppings) and overlap, solves for electron states, then updates the cloud and repeats to self-consistency. The expensive step is that solve: turning the tables into bands means diagonalizing matrices, which grows roughly like $N^3$ for $N$ atoms. Double the atoms, about 8× the time. Triple them, about 27×.

A machine-learned Hamiltonian skips the loop. It looks at the positions and elements and predicts the finished tables $H$ and $S$ directly. One prediction, no iteration. If you can make the cost grow like $N$ instead of $N^3$, large cells and large screens become possible.

The dream in this paper is a universal model: train once on small crystals (the authors used crystals with at most about 20 to 60 atoms, covering 68 elements from H to Bi), then use the same model for new element combinations and for glasses you never showed it. That's hard because chemistry changes, glasses have no repeating pattern, and you still need the answers to be physical and cheap.

The paper says a useful universal model must do three things at once: 1) generalize across chemistry without a new model per material, 2) stay physical so its bands don't sprout fake "ghost" states from tiny errors, and 3) stay cheap for big cells. UniTB is built around those three, which you'll meet in the next lessons.

Try it below: drag the number of atoms $N$ and watch how a toy DFT $N^3$ curve explodes while the learned models — anchored to the paper's real timings at 3,456 atoms (UniTB 5.11 s, UniTB-Flash 1.65 s on one H200 GPU) — rise gently. The check boxes let you compare. Look for the 180–216 atom zone for one amorphous cell, then hit "Screen 1,000 materials" to feel what a search costs. Remember the DFT line is illustrative; the UniTB and Flash curves are simplified models tuned to match the paper at 3,456 atoms.

002Lesson 2 • The object we learn~7 min

What a Hamiltonian actually is (in pictures)

Think of a guitar string fixed between two posts. Each post alone would vibrate at its own note, but linked together they make two new notes: one where they move together (lower pitch) and one where they move opposite (higher pitch). A material's electrons do the same trick. Atoms are the posts, and the Hamiltonian is the table that says how strongly they are linked.

In the paper this table is called $H$ and it lives in a basis of atomic orbitals. Each atom gets cloud-shaped functions: round $s$, dumbbell $p$, clover $d$, and more complicated $f$. Real chemistry uses different numbers of these, but to make one model for 68 elements the authors pad every element to the same size: $27$ spatial functions written $4s2p2d1f$. That is just $4$ kinds of $s$ + $2$ kinds of $p$ + $2$ kinds of $d$ + $1$ kind of $f$, counting all the orientations, so $H$ always has a predictable shape.

🧩

Onsite block $H_{ii}$

Diagonal block: atom $i$ with itself. Holds its own energy levels plus the electric field of all neighbors — the crystal field. In the toy below: $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$.

🔗

Hopping block $H_{ij}$

Off-diagonal: atom $i$ to atom $j$ (including its copies in the next cell). Depends mainly on the two elements and distance. In the toy: $t$. Far apart $t\approx 0$, close $t$ big and negative.

There is a second table you cannot ignore: the overlap $S$. Basis functions on neighboring atoms overlap in space, so $S_{ij}$ is about $0.3$ to $0.6$ when atoms touch and $0$ when far. $S_{ii}=1$ on the diagonal. Bands — the energies you actually measure — solve the generalized problem

$$ H(\mathbf{k})\,c = \varepsilon\, S(\mathbf{k})\,c $$

Here $H(\mathbf{k})$ and $S(\mathbf{k})$ are $H$ and $S$ summed over all periodic images with a phase for wavevector $\mathbf{k}$, $c$ is the electron wave, and $\varepsilon$ is its band energy. Skip $S$ and you get the wrong $ε$, because the basis is not orthogonal — like measuring distance on skewed graph paper. Every property the paper later screens — band gap, effective mass, refractive index $n$, conductivity $\sigma$ — comes from these $\varepsilon$ and $c$.

Go deeper: spin In the paper most of $H$ is spin-independent (same for spin up and down). Spin-orbit coupling adds tiny extra spin-flip blocks $H^{\uparrow\downarrow}$ built from the pseudopotential projectors. They matter for heavy elements but do not change the onsite vs hopping picture above. The full model leaves the harder multi-element, spin-orbit details to later lessons.

Try it: Use the 2-atom lab. Keep $\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_2$ and slide hopping $t$ — watch one level dive (bonding) and one rise (anti-bonding). Now make $\varepsilon_1 \neq \varepsilon_2$ and notice hopping has to fight the onsite mismatch. Finally crank up overlap $s$ and see the splitting become lopsided — that asymmetry is why $S$ must stay in $Hc=\varepsilon Sc$.

003Lesson 3 • Learn the difference~8 min

Don't learn from scratch — learn the correction

Imagine you have to guess a number like 1,023. That's hard. Now imagine someone tells you "it's 1,000 plus a little bit." You only have to guess the 23. Same answer, much easier job.

UniTB does exactly that for the Hamiltonian — the big table of numbers that sets a material's bands. It doesn't ask a neural network to invent the whole table. It starts from a cheap physics guess, called the prior Pij, and learns only the correction.

$$\widehat{H}_{ij} = \mathcal{P}_{ij} + \Delta_{\theta,ij} + \Delta^{\rm pot}_{ij}$$

In plain words: \(\widehat{H}_{ij}\) is the predicted Hamiltonian block between orbital i and j. \(\mathcal{P}_{ij}\) is the prior. \(\Delta_{\theta,ij}\) is what the network learns — the residual H - \mathcal{P}. \(\Delta^{\rm pot}_{ij}\) is an extra shift from charge that moved between atoms (lesson 5 will unpack it).

Why bother? In a simplified version of the paper's test, learning H directly gave a mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.692 meV. Learning the residual H - H^{(0)} with the same tiny network dropped it to 0.813 meV — about seven times better. The paper's full UniTB model pushes this to 0.722 meV after about 100 epochs on 11,953 crystals, and 0.493 meV after about 300 epochs on 29,242 crystals.

A meV is a milli-electron-volt, a thousandth of the energy that moves an electron in chemistry. Small MAE means the predicted table is close entry-by-entry.

Two ways to build the prior — same idea, different bill:
H(0) adds up neutral-atom densities, then builds Hartree and exchange-correlation potentials on a grid that fills the whole cell. Accurate, but the grid grows with cell volume and costs O(N log N).
HGF (grid-free) skips the grid: it looks up tabulated two-center integrals for each pair of elements and rotates them into place with Slater–Koster rules. Like a tight-binding cookbook. It costs only O(N) and doesn't care how much vacuum is in the cell.

There's a trade-off, also from the paper's controlled test where three identical dense networks differed only in the prior. Onsite blocks (atom with itself) are similar: 8.49 meV for H^{(0)} vs 8.90 meV for HGF. But hopping blocks (atom to neighbor) get harder without the grid: 0.726 meV vs 3.01 meV. That's why UniTB (with H^{(0)}) is the more accurate model, while UniTB-Flash (with HGF) is the faster one.

Try the lab below: pick how good the prior is — none, HGF-like, or H(0)-like — and set the network capacity low. Watch which learner collapses and which still succeeds, and compare the toy MAE drop to the paper's 5.69 → 0.81 meV.

004Lesson 4 • One model, many elements~7 min

One brain for 68 elements: experts that pick their jobs

Imagine you run a repair shop for every car brand at once. You can't afford a separate mechanic for each of 68 brands and thousands of model pairs, but a single generalist can't know every quirk either. The old tight-binding workaround was literal: keep a different table for every element pair — Fe-Fe, Ga-As, Si-O, and so on.

UniTB copies that idea, but with learned parts that share most of their brain. It trains two networks, one for onsite blocks (an atom with itself, which feels the whole neighbourhood) and one for hopping blocks (one atom to another). Inside each layer, every update can look different for different chemistry because of element-dependent tensor products.

Then comes the mixture of experts. For each bond, UniTB does not use one giant matrix. It uses a shared, always-on map plus a pick of specialists:

$$W_{ij} = W_s + P\left(\sum_e g_{ij,e} D_e\right) Q^{\mathrm{T}}$$

In plain words: W_s is the generalist who is always working. P and Q are shared bases — like a common language everyone projects into. Each expert owns a small core D_e that acts only inside that language. For every bond, the router computes weights g_{ij,e} and uses just the top 2 of 4 experts, mixing their cores before the activation is applied.

The router's decision is not from distance alone. It sees the ordered bond type — "Ga→As" is different from "As→Ga" — plus a fingerprint of the prior block itself, d(P_{ij}). That fingerprint is made from the signed scalar channels of P_{ij} and the Gram overlaps of its higher-order channels, passed through a signed logarithm. Together they go into cosine-similarity scores (scale 10), the top-2 are kept, and their softmax gives the gates. No extra load-balancing loss is needed — training nudges biases to balance experts.

Why bother? At the 12,000-structure budget, the PDQ-MoE alone cut combined matrix error by 3.49% versus the dense baseline, while just making the dense model wider with the same parameter count cut only 1.15%. Using only bond type to route raised combined error by 2.74%, and for the sensitive onsite blocks by +7.17% (hopping only +2.30%). A router that used bond type plus distance did almost as well as the full one for hopping (−1.63%) but still hurt onsite (+8.82%). In short: hopping mostly cares about the pair and distance, onsite really needs the neighbourhood described by the prior.

The specialization is real but frugal. About 1 in 10 edges gets a non-modal expert pair — not the usual pair for its bond type. If you shuffle gate vectors among edges of the same type, error jumps by a factor of about 3.29× onsite and 2.13× for hopping. Averaging over experts or keeping only the shared map is even worse. The paper's message: let the bond's prior tell you which two specialists to call, and keep a shared backbone so you don't pay for thousands of separate tables.

Try it below. Pick a bond type, then wiggle the two prior invariants and distance. See which two experts light up, when you leave the modal pair, and why distance moves hopping but invariants move onsite — exactly the ablation the paper reported.

005Lesson 5 • Charge talks far~8 min

When charge moves across the whole crystal

Your network only sees nearby atoms — say, inside a small sphere. That's fast, but real charge doesn't respect the sphere. Put an oxygen next to silicon and electrons shift a little from Si to O. That shift changes the electric push felt by atoms far away. The paper's cheap starting guess, the neutral-atom prior, knows nothing about this transfer. If you only let the local network fix it, you're missing the long-range chat.

UniTB fixes it the way textbook tight-binding (called SCC-DFTB) does. Every site gets a learned potential $v_i$ and it enters the Hamiltonian only through the overlap — how much the orbitals touch:

$$\Delta^{\mathrm{pot}}_{ii}=v^{\mathrm{on}}_{i}S_{ii},\qquad \Delta^{\mathrm{pot}}_{ij}=\tfrac12\,(v^{\mathrm{hop}}_{i}+v^{\mathrm{hop}}_{j})\,S_{ij}$$

$S_{ij}$ is the overlap matrix from Lesson 2. Scaling by $S_{ij}$ guarantees the physics stays in the right metric — a distant pair with tiny overlap gets a tiny shift, even if $v_i$ is large.

Where do the $v_i$ come from? From latent charges $q_i$ that talk across the whole crystal. The model predicts an electronegativity $\chi_i = \chi_{Z_i}+f_\chi(h_i)$ and a hardness $J_i$ from local features $h_i$, then solves a tiny global energy problem:
$$q = \arg\min_{\;\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}q=0}\;\Big[\,\chi^{\mathsf T}q + \tfrac12\,q^{\mathsf T}A\,q\,\Big],\qquad A = \mathrm{diag}(J)+\Gamma$$

with total charge locked to zero, $\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}q=0$. $\Gamma$ is the Coulomb kernel of smeared charges in the periodic cell:

$$\Gamma_{ij}= \frac{4\pi k_{e}}{\Omega}\sum_{0< |\mathbf G|\le G_{c}}\frac{e^{-\sigma^{2}|\mathbf G|^{2}}}{|\mathbf G|^{2}}\cos\big[\mathbf G\cdot(\mathbf r_{i}-\mathbf r_{j})\big]$$

That sum over reciprocal vectors $\mathbf G$ is just Coulomb's law made periodic — every atom feels every other atom. Solving $A q + \chi + \lambda \mathbf{1}=0$ gives $q = -x + y(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}x / \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}y)$ where $x=A^{-1}\chi$, $y=A^{-1}\mathbf{1}$. Then $\tilde v_i = s[f_{\mathrm{loc}}(h_i) + \kappa(\Gamma q)_i]$ is centered to $v_i$ by overlap weights $d_i$. In short: local readouts propose charges, the whole cell negotiates them.

Why bother? Without this head, the leftover error that looks like a site potential had RMS 6.4 meV on training crystals but 28.3 meV where the formula was seen before, 59.4 meV for brand-new formulas, and 97.2 meV for the most foreign structures. Add the head — error drops 2–4% on the same dense backbone, and up to 6.98% for cells with more than 22 atoms but only 1.33% for 1–5 atoms. A local-only head without the $\Gamma q$ term is 1.97% worse than the full QEq: the global chat matters, and it matters more when there are more atoms to chat across.

In the lab below, you are the electronegativities. Set $\chi$ for atoms on a ring, hit Balance charges, and watch the globally solved $q_i$ and resulting $v_i$ and $\Delta^{\mathrm{pot}}$ heatmap. Toggle between Full QEq and Local-only — especially try the O–Si polar preset and grow the ring to 7–8 atoms — to see how much signal you miss without long-range equilibration.

Real model uses $\sigma=1.2$ Å, $G_c=4$ Å$^{-1}$ with Ewald evaluation, shared by onsite/hopping nets, costing $O(N^2)$ for $\Gamma$ plus $O(N^3)$ Cholesky — still 0.04 s at 3,456 atoms. Lab is simplified: you dial $\chi$ directly instead of learning it from $h_i$.

006Lesson 6 • Keep it physical~8 min

Hunting ghost bands

A tiny error in a matrix can make a completely fake band appear in the plot you actually use. That's a ghost, or spurious state. Even if every entry of your predicted Hamiltonian $\hat H$ is off by less than 1 meV on average — excellent by normal standards — one bad eigenvalue can land right in the band gap and ruin optics, conductivity and screening.

Why? Bands are not just eigenvalues of $H$. Because atomic orbitals overlap, you must solve the generalized problem:

$$H(\mathbf{k})\,c_a = \varepsilon_a\,S(\mathbf{k})\,c_a$$

$S$ is the overlap matrix — how much one orbital spills onto another. When $S$ is almost singular, small errors in $H$ get amplified. A model trained only to match matrix entries does not know this.

Key insight: the real correction is bounded. The true $H-H^{(0)}$ is just a change in local potential from moving charge, $\Delta V_{\rm Hxc}(\mathbf{r})$. Its generalized eigenvalues are weighted averages of that potential, so they must stay inside $[\min \Delta V, \max \Delta V]$. For 3,063 training crystals that interval never exceeds about 8.5 eV. We can force the AI to obey the same rule.

UniTB does it by whitening and clipping. Write the predicted residual $\hat\Delta = \hat H - H^{(0)}$, factor $S = L L^{\dagger}$ and set $X = L^{-\dagger}$ so $X^{\dagger} S X = I$. In this whitened metric form

$$\hat A = X^{\dagger}\,\hat\Delta\,X = U\,{\rm diag}(\mu)\,U^{\dagger}$$

$\mu$ are the generalized eigenvalues of the residual. Clip them at $b=10$ eV, just above the 8.5 eV seen in real data:

$$\mu'_a = {\rm clip}(\mu_a, -b, b)$$

then rebuild $\hat\Delta_b = \hat\Delta + \sum_{a:|\mu_a|>b}(\mu'_a-\mu_a) S x_a x_a^{\dagger} S$ with $x_a = XU_{:a}$, and $\hat H_b = H^{(0)}+\hat\Delta_b$. By construction $-b I \preceq f_b(\hat A) \preceq b I$, so every projected band obeys

$$\varepsilon_a(H^{(0)},S)-b \le \varepsilon_a(\hat H_b,S) \le \varepsilon_a(H^{(0)},S)+b$$

Directions already inside $[-b,b]$ are untouched; only outliers are pulled to the edge. If $S$ itself is near-singular (overlap eigenvalue below $10^{-5}$), that direction is dropped first.

The result: on the 200-structure band test, mean $\rm fw_{10}$ — average error of levels in a $\pm10$ eV window — drops by 15 to 39 meV after projection. UniTB improves from 102.4 to 63.3 meV in the head-to-head test and to 63.1 meV with the final recipe. Most importantly, zero of the 200 structures show a strict ghost after projection — a level at least 0.5 eV from every DFT level for more than 10% of $k$-points — versus 6 to 13 before. The paper's example is LiZrPd$_2$: a spurious level cuts through the gap before projection; after clipping it vanishes and $\rm fw_{10}$ falls from 156.2 to 78.2 meV.

Why not project UniTB-Flash? Flash learns $H-H_{\rm GF}$. The difference $H_{\rm GF}-H^{(0)}$ comes mostly from the approximate multicenter exchange-correlation in hopping blocks, which is not a local potential and has no such bound. Clipping it would clip real physics. Flash therefore only removes tiny overlap eigenvalues below $2\times10^{-3}$.

Now trap the ghost yourself. Start with projection OFF and push the outlier past the box — watch an orange dot jump outside $[-b,b]$ and a red level appear alone in the gap. Turn projection ON and try $b=5, 7, 10, 20$ eV. Notice how the shaded corridor $H^{(0)}\pm b$ swallows the ghost at $b=10$ eV while leaving the good bands almost unchanged.

007Lesson 7 • Glass from crystals~8 min

The glass test — no extra training

Ask a model to describe a crystal it has never seen and it might do okay — crystals repeat neatly. Now ask it to describe glass. No repeating pattern, bonds are bent and twisted, and you need a big box of about 200 atoms just to capture the disorder. If the model only studied small perfect crystals, will it fail?

That is exactly the test the paper sets. The team trained UniTB and UniTB-Flash only on crystals (up to ~20 atoms in the extra data, none amorphous). Then they tested on 18 big supercells, 180 to 216 atoms each: 12 cells of GST — the Ge–Sb–Te alloy used in phase-change memory and photonic switches — covering 7 different compositions along the GeTe to Sb2Te3 line, plus 6 cells of amorphous silicon (four random networks cooled at different rates, a paracrystal, and a diamond crystal as control).

The question is transfer: can the same Hamiltonian that learned ordered neighborhoods predict the electronics when order disappears beyond the nearest neighbors?

From each predicted Hamiltonian the team solved for bands at the center of the Brillouin zone (Γ) and used the Kubo–Greenwood formula — basically velocity × velocity between states — to get optics and transport. At 0.8 eV photon energy (the 1550 nm telecom band) they checked the refractive index n, at 1.5 eV the absorption α that sets how deep light goes, and the DC conductivity σDC that sets the memory's resistance. No retraining, same code for DFT and models.

Both models passed. Averaged over the 12 GST glasses, UniTB matched DFT to 1.77% for n, 1.73% for α and 0.084 decades for conductivity (a factor of 100.084 ≈ 1.2×). UniTB-Flash was 1.98%, 0.79% and 0.062 decades — essentially the same. Even better, across the 7 compositions the ranking was preserved: Spearman correlation ρS = 0.96 for n and conductivity and 1.00 for absorption. Alloy designers care about that trend more than any single number.

They also compared valence-band photoemission spectra: weight each occupied state by its Al Kα cross-section, broaden by 0.45 eV, normalize area to 1. Shape error — half the area between curves — averaged 1.00% for GST and 1.17% for Si with UniTB, and 1.15% / 2.77% with Flash. For the conductivity, the paper notes an extra subtlety: UniTB's velocity uses the derivative of the projected Hamiltonian via the Daleckii–Krein formula, so the ghost-hunting fix from Lesson 6 doesn't break the physics.

Did the raw Hamiltonian also transfer? Yes. On those same 18 cells the mean matrix error was 0.766 meV for UniTB (-12.0% vs its dense baseline) and 1.818 meV for Flash (-10.9% vs its baseline) — the same improvements seen on crystals. Cheaper prior, faster run, but the crystal lessons still held for glass.

008Lesson 8 • Screen like a factory~8 min

How accurate, how fast, and what it found

So did the whole tight-binding-inspired package actually work — accurately, quickly, and on real discovery?

Start with accuracy. UniTB was trained on 29,242 crystals and tested on 2,431 crystals that the Materials Project labels non-magnetic, spanning 68 elements. On every single element in that test, the average matrix error — the average straight difference between predicted and true Hamiltonian entries — was below 0.9 meV. Averaged over all those crystals it was 0.48 meV. That's less than half a thousandth of an electron-volt.

Matrix error sounds abstract, so the paper also checks what you actually use: bands. The band error fw10 is the mean absolute error of the energy levels within ±10 eV of the valence top, equally averaged over bands and k-points. UniTB gets 49.05 meV on average and 21.3 meV at the median. In plain words: most crystals are much better than the average, and a few hard ones pull the mean up.

Small matrix error can still make a fake band, which is why UniTB also uses its spectral projection. Without it, a tiny 1 meV error can be amplified when the overlap matrix S is almost singular. With projection, the numbers above are already the clean, ghost-free bands.

Now speed. Both models must build the prior, the overlap, and the network correction for every new crystal. For a 3,456-atom PbTe supercell on one H200 GPU, UniTB takes 5.11 seconds total and UniTB-Flash takes 1.65 seconds. The difference is almost all the prior: 4.08 s for the grid-based H(0) that fills the cell with a potential on a grid, versus 0.66 s for the grid-free HGF that sums only neighbors inside a cutoff using tabulated two-center integrals. The neural network itself takes about 0.92 s in both. So the cheaper prior costs some accuracy — especially hopping blocks where the grid-free exchange-correlation is approximated — but buys about 3× speed.

A tiny kernel library called SO2CUDA makes that network part faster everywhere. It fuses rotations into and out of each bond frame with the expert matrix products, and it works for any model using SO(2) convolutions. Across 26 tested shapes it was 1.29 to 10.7 times faster than the baselines, and a training step of the onsite network went from 1.64 s to 0.79 s.

That speed-accuracy trade-off suggests a factory line. In a screen of 16,195 public crystal structures for five industrial jobs — interconnect metals ranked by ρ0λ, photonics by refractive index n, photovoltaics by SLME efficiency, high-mobility channels by conduction mass mc, and thermoelectrics by power factor PF/τ — UniTB-Flash ranked every structure with a valid value, UniTB re-checked only the top 200 in each pool, and DFT checked the final top 20.

The result: the fast ranker's ability to keep the true best candidates (recall of the DFT top 20 inside your top K, averaged over K=5 to 60, perfect score 0.893) differed from the accurate model by at most 0.051 — the paper finds no significant difference in any domain. But when you look at the numbers you report, UniTB is 42 to 59% more accurate. So rank fast, refine accurately.

For n-type thermoelectrics that refined list held up under DFT: 19 of 20 beat the median of classic reference materials, and 6 beat the strongest reference Mg2Si itself — LiTe3 at 4.3 times and HfTe5 at 2.66 times its PF/τ. Two of those winners, Ta4SiTe4 and doped Nb4SiTe4, already have large measured power factors in whiskers.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

DFT (density functional theory)

The standard but expensive way to calculate how electrons behave in a material by slowly making the electron density consistent with itself.

Hamiltonian (H)

A big table of numbers that describes electron energies and how they hop between atoms. Its eigenvalues are the bands you measure.

overlap matrix (S)

A table that says how much atomic orbitals overlap. Bands come from solving H c = ε S c, not just H c = ε c.

onsite vs hopping block

The diagonal blocks H_ii (atom with itself) and off-diagonal blocks H_ij (atom i to atom j). Onsite feels the whole neighborhood; hopping mostly cares about the two atoms and distance.

E(3) equivariant

Equivariance means if you rotate the crystal, the predicted Hamiltonian rotates in the matching way. The model is built to guarantee this.

prior Hamiltonian

A cheap starting guess for H made from superposed neutral atoms. UniTB uses H^(0) (needs a grid). UniTB-Flash uses H_GF (grid-free tables).

residual learning

Instead of learning H directly, learn the small correction Δ = H - prior and add it back: Ĥ = P + Δ_θ + Δ^pot. Paper: 5.692 meV → 0.813 meV by doing this.

PDQ-MoE (mixture of experts)

A trick where each bond is sent to 2 of 4 specialist sub-networks (experts). The choice is made from the bond type and the prior's rotation invariants d(P_ij).

charge equilibration (QEq)

Letting electrons shift between atoms and finding the set of atomic charges q_i that minimizes energy with total charge zero. UniTB learns this and adds v_i S_ij shifts.

spurious / ghost state

A fake band in the predicted spectrum caused by a bad eigenvalue when S is ill-conditioned. Even <1 meV matrix error can create one.

spectral projection

Cutting the predicted residual's generalized eigenvalues to [-b,b] with b=10 eV, so every projected band stays within b of the prior's band. Removes strict ghosts in 200/200 test structures.

O(N) vs O(N log N)

How cost grows with atoms N. Grid prior is O(N log N) and grows with cell volume; grid-free prior, overlap and spin blocks are O(N) by summing only neighbors within a cutoff.
