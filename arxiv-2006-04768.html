001The bottleneck~6 min

When Every Word Listens to Every Word

Imagine a group chat where every person has to re-read every other person's message before replying. With 5 people, that's 25 reads. With 50 people, it's 2,500. With 4,000 people, it's 16 million. That's a Transformer doing self-attention.

In a Transformer, every token — think word or word-piece — gets to "attend" to every other token to decide what matters. For a sentence like "the cat sat," the word "sat" looks at "cat" and "the" to understand who sat. The model stores all those look-ups in a table called the context mapping matrix $P$.

$P$ has one row per token and one column per token. If your sequence length is $n$, $P$ is $n \times n$. For $n=512$ — a typical paragraph — that's $512 \times 512 = 262{,}144$ numbers. For $n=4{,}096$ — a short document — that's about 16.7 million numbers. Just one layer, one head. A real model has 12 or 24 layers and 12 heads each, so this multiplies fast.

To build $P$, the model multiplies an $n \times d$ matrix $QW^Q$ by a $d \times n$ matrix $(KW^K)^T$ and then takes a softmax. The $d$ is small (like 64), but the $n \times n$ result is what costs so much. In big-O talk, that cost is $O(n^2)$ — double the length, quadruple the time and memory.

Why should we care about big-O? It describes how cost grows with $n$:

  • $O(n)$ means linear: 2× longer input → about 2× cost. Manageable.
  • $O(n^2)$ means quadratic: 2× longer → 4× cost. Painful fast.

The paper's Table 1 puts this in perspective. A recurrent model is $O(n)$ but has to go step-by-step $O(n)$ sequential steps, so it's slow in a different way. The original Transformer is $O(n^2)$ but can do it in $O(1)$ parallel steps. Sparse Transformer trims it to $O(n\sqrt{n})$. Reformer hashes it to $O(n \log n)$ with $O(\log n)$ steps. The paper's goal — Linformer — is $O(n)$ with $O(1)$ steps: the best of both.

That bottleneck is why training and using large Transformers is expensive for long text, and why this paper asks: do we really need the full $n \times n$ table?

Try it below: drag $n$ from 64 to 2,048 and watch the cell count explode. Toggle between Transformer $O(n^2)$ and Linformer $O(n \cdot k)$ (pick a small $k$ like 128). Watch what happens to the grid and the memory bar — and notice how doubling $n$ quadruples cells for Transformer but only doubles them for Linformer.

002How attention works~8 min

What Self-Attention Actually Computes

Imagine you are at a noisy lunch table with 500 friends. For every word you say, you have to decide who to listen to next. The Transformer does exactly that: for every token (think: a word or piece of a word), it listens to every other token in the sentence, weighs who matters most, and mixes their meanings together.

That weighing is called self-attention. The paper writes it for one "head" — one independent listener — like this:

$$\text{head}_i = \underbrace{\text{softmax}\left[\frac{QW_i^Q (KW_i^K)^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right]}_{P} \; V W_i^V$$

Here is what each letter means, in plain words. $Q$, $K$, $V$ are tables with $n$ rows (one per token) and $d_m$ columns (numbers that describe the token). $W_i^Q$, $W_i^K$, $W_i^V$ are learned glasses that project those tables into a smaller $d$-dimensional space — so $QW_i^Q$ is just "queries after putting glasses on", $KW_i^K$ is "keys after glasses", $VW_i^V$ is "values after glasses".

$A = QW_i^Q (KW_i^K)^T / \sqrt{d}$ is a table of dot-products — how well each query matches each key. If two arrows point the same way, their dot-product is large; if they are perpendicular, it is near zero. Dividing by $\sqrt{d}$ just keeps the numbers from exploding.

Then $P = \text{softmax}(A)$ turns each row of $A$ into weights that sum to 1. More precisely the paper writes $P = \exp(A)\,D_A^{-1}$ where $D_A$ is the diagonal normalizer for each row — that is the same as softmax, it just makes each entry $\exp(A_{ij})$ divided by the sum of its row. Finally we multiply $P \cdot VW_i^V$: each new token representation is a weighted average of all values, with the weights coming from $P$.

Key idea: one row of $P$ answers "for this word, who should I listen to?" The whole $n\times n$ matrix $P$ is the listening plan for the entire sentence.

With many heads, the model just runs several independent listeners and sticks their results together:

$$\text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V)=\text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)W^O$$

where $W^O$ blends the heads back together.

This is powerful but expensive. $P$ itself has $n \times n$ entries — for $n=512$ that is 262,144 numbers per head, per layer. That is why the next lesson will ask: does $P$ really need all that detail?

003The discovery~8 min

The Hidden Pattern: Attention Is Almost Simple

Transformer attention is huge, but maybe it is also wasteful. The paper asks a simple question: how much of that giant n × n context table P actually matters? P is the table where row i says how much word i listens to every other word. For n = 512, that is 512 × 512 = 262,144 numbers per head.

To find out, the researchers opened up a trained RoBERTa model — the 12-layer base version and the 24-layer large version — and fed it 10,000 sentences from two very different datasets, Wiki103 and IMDB. For each attention head they broke P apart with a tool called SVD.

Think of SVD like finding the ingredients of a smoothie. Any table P can be written as a mix of layered patterns, each with a strength score called a singular value, written σ. Big σ = important pattern. Small σ = tiny tweak you barely taste.

They sorted those σ from biggest to smallest and plotted the running total. The math is just:

$$\text{share at }k = \frac{σ_1+σ_2+\dots+σ_k}{σ_1+σ_2+\dots+σ_{512}}$$

That is Figure 1 on the left. If the curve jumps up fast and then goes flat, it means a few big patterns hold almost everything. And that is exactly what they saw — on every layer, every head, and both datasets. The curve is long-tailed: the top 100 to 150 values out of 512 already hold more than 90% of the total. The rest are crumbs.

Figure 1 on the right makes this even clearer. For each layer and head they checked: after the 128th biggest pattern, how much have we already captured? Brighter means more captured, so more compressible. Higher layers (toward the top of the network) are brighter than lower ones. In other words, the deeper the network goes, the simpler its attention gets — it needs even fewer patterns.

The paper's precise claim — Theorem 1, simplified: For any sequence of length n, there is a table ~P with rank about log n that does almost the same job as P on any value vector w: ||~P w − P w|| < ε ||P w|| with high probability. Rank here just means "how many patterns we kept." The point: rank does not need to be anywhere near n; a slowly growing log n is enough.

This is the hidden pattern the whole Linformer idea rests on: attention looks big, but it acts small. If 128 directions are enough when n = 512, we should not need to build all 512 × 512 numbers every time.

Try it yourself below. You get a small 16 × 16 pretend attention map with the same long-tail shape. Slide k to keep only the top k patterns. Watch how the rebuilt map snaps into place, the error drops, and the cumulative curve matches the paper's. Then switch the tail from "Long-tail" to "Flatter" to see why the paper checked many layers — some need more k, some need less.

004From SVD to random maps~7 min

Why Low-Rank Means Compressible

Lesson 3 showed that the attention matrix $P$ — the $n \times n$ table that says how much each word looks at each other word — is secretly simple. For $n=512$, the first ~100 biggest ingredients already hold most of it. That kind of matrix is called low-rank: you don't need 512 directions to describe it, just a few.

If $P$ is low-rank, we should be able to compress it. There is a famous best way to do that.

The perfect compressor (but slow)

Take any matrix apart with Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). You get importance scores $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge ...$ and directions $u_i$, $v_i$. The Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem says the best rank-$k$ copy is simply to keep the top $k$:

$$ P_{\text{low}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^T $$

Each term $\sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ is an $n \times n$ layer, but you only store $2k$ skinny vectors, so the cost drops from $O(n^2)$ to $O(nk)$. If $k \ll n$, that's a big win. The paper writes this as equation (6).

The catch: to build $P_{\text{low}}$ you first have to do an SVD on $P$ — and $P$ is still $n \times n$. Doing an SVD every layer, every head, every batch wipes out the savings. The paper needs a cheaper trick.

Key idea: $P$ is low-rank, so a good small $k$ exists. SVD finds the perfect $k$ directions, but we don't need perfect. A random squish can be almost as good and is basically free.

The cheap trick: a random squish

This is where a classic math result called the Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) Lemma comes in. In plain words:

Take a random matrix $R$ of size $k \times n$ where each entry is drawn from a Normal bell curve with spread $1/k$ (written $N(0,1/k)$). If you multiply any vector $x$ by $R$, its length is almost preserved:

$$ \|R x\| \approx \|x\|, \quad \text{and} \quad x R^T R y^T \approx x y^T $$

That is, dot-products — the heart of attention — survive the squish, as long as $k$ is big enough. How big? Roughly

$$ k = \frac{5 \log(n)}{\epsilon^2 - \epsilon^3} $$

for an error you choose, $\epsilon$. Notice $\log(n)$: for $n=512$, $\log(n) \approx 6$, so $k$ is tiny. The smaller $\epsilon$ you want, the larger $k$ you need, but it still grows slowly.

The paper uses this to build a low-rank stand-in without ever doing an SVD (Theorem 1 and Appendix A):

$$ \tilde{P} = \exp(A) D_A^{-1} R^T R $$

Here $A = QW^Q(KW^K)^T/\sqrt{d}$ and $D_A$ just makes rows sum to 1 for softmax (equation 4). So $\tilde{P}$ is the same as $P$ except with $R^T R$ inserted. Because $R$ is $k \times n$, the rank of $\tilde{P}$ can be at most $k$.

Then a union bound — a fancy way of saying “if each of the $n$ rows is good with high chance, all of them together are still good with high chance” — gives:

$$ \Pr\big(\|\tilde{P}w^T - P w^T\| < \epsilon \|P w^T\|\big) > 1 - o(1) $$

In words: pick any column $w$ of $V$, the squished version $\tilde{P}w^T$ is within $\epsilon$ of the real $Pw^T$, almost certainly.

Go deeper: why random works here. Attention scores are $\exp(QK^T)$. For any fixed $w$, $Pw^T$ is a weighted average. JL says a random projection keeps the length of every $u$ (each row of $P$) when dotted with $w$. Apply JL to each of the $n$ rows separately (error $2e^{-(\epsilon^2-\epsilon^3)k/4}$ per row), then union-bound over $n$ rows: total failure < $2n e^{-(\epsilon^2-\epsilon^3)k/4}$. Set $k=5\log(n)/(\epsilon^2-\epsilon^3)$ and that failure goes to ~0. That's Appendix A in a nutshell.

So we get compression without SVD: keep the math the same, just sandwich a short, random $R^T R$ in the middle. Next we will see how Linformer turns this random idea into learned projection matrices $E$ and $F$ that squeeze keys and values before you even compute attention.

Try it below: pick two random vectors in 200 dimensions. A random $R$ squishes them to just $k$ dimensions. Hit "Re-roll" many times — watch the histogram of the length ratio. At $k=10$ the average is still ~1 but the spread is huge; drag $k$ up and see how quickly it tightens. That's the JL magic the proof relies on.

005The Linformer~8 min

The Linformer Trick: Squeeze Before You Compare

Standard attention builds a huge table: every token asks “how much should I listen to every other token?” For a sentence with $n$ words, that table $P$ has $n \times n$ entries. When $n$ doubles, the work quadruples. That is the $O(n^2)$ bottleneck.

Linformer’s idea is disarmingly simple: squeeze the things you compare before you compare them. Don’t keep all $n$ keys and all $n$ values. Compress each list from length $n$ down to a short length $k$, where $k$ is much smaller than $n$ — for example $k=128$ while $n=512$ or $4096$.

The squeeze formula

A normal attention head is:

$$\text{head}_i = \underbrace{\text{softmax}\left[\frac{QW_i^Q (KW_i^K)^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right]}_{P:\, n \times n} \cdot VW_i^V$$

$QW_i^Q$ are the queries ($n \times d$), $KW_i^K$ the keys ($n \times d$), $VW_i^V$ the values ($n \times d$). The softmax turns dot-products into weights that sum to 1, giving the $n \times n$ context map $P$.

Linformer inserts two learned projection matrices $E_i$ and $F_i$, each $k \times n$ (the paper also writes them as $n \times k$ before transposing — same idea). They act like a squeeze:

$$\overline{\text{head}}_i = \underbrace{\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QW_i^Q (E_i K W_i^K)^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right)}_{\bar{P}:\, n \times k} \cdot \underbrace{F_i V W_i^V}_{k \times d}$$

In words: first $E_i$ squeezes the $n$ keys to $k$ compressed keys ($E_i K W_i^K$ is $k \times d$). Separately, $F_i$ squeezes the $n$ values to $k$ compressed values. Then you do attention between $n$ queries and only $k$ keys, so $\bar{P}$ is $n \times k$, not $n \times n$. No $n \times n$ table ever exists.

📐

Count the work

Standard: $n \times n$ entries → $O(n^2)$. Linformer: $n \times k$ entries → $O(n \cdot k)$. The $k \times d$ projection itself costs $O(n \cdot k)$ too — still linear in $n$.

📌

Fix k, get O(n)

If $k$ is fixed — say $128$ — then $O(n \cdot k)$ is just $O(n)$. Double $n$, you double work, not quadruple it. That is linear complexity.

Why can k stay fixed even for very long text? Theorem 2 proves there exist $E_i, F_i$ with $k = \min\{\Theta(9d\log d /\epsilon^2),\, 5\Theta(\log n /\epsilon^2)\}$ that approximate the original attention within error $\epsilon$. Because keys $QW^Q(KW^K)^T$ have rank at most $d$, the first term has no $n$ in it. In simplified form: $k$ can be chosen like a constant depending on hidden size $d$, not on sequence length $n$. So you don’t need bigger $k$ for longer sentences.

The paper’s Figure 2 shows the payoff: with total tokens held fixed, Transformer inference time climbs steeply as $n$ grows, while Linformer’s stays almost flat. The architecture diagram on that figure is exactly the squeeze above — project $K$ and $V$, then attend.

Try it below: drag $n$ from 128 to 2048 and $k$ from 16 to 512. Watch the $n \times n$ grid shrink to $n \times k$, and see the operation count drop from $n^2$ to $n \cdot k$. Notice what happens when $k \ll n$ versus when $k$ is close to $n$ — and what happens to cost when you keep $k=128$ fixed and just grow $n$.

006Even cheaper~6 min

Sharing and Stretching: Even Cheaper

We have the squeeze trick: shrink the keys and values from length $n$ to length $k$ before doing attention, turning an $n \times n$ job into an $n \times k$ job. If $k$ is fixed — say 128 or 256 — the cost is $O(n \cdot k)$, which is just $O(n)$, linear in sequence length.

But there is a catch. Where do the squeeze matrices $E$ and $F$ come from? In the simplest Linformer, every head in every layer learns its own $E_i$ and $F_i$, each of size $n \times k$. For a common setup — 12 layers, 12 heads — that is $12 \times 12 \times 2 = 288$ separate matrices. Each one is $n$ times $k$ numbers. That is a lot of extra weight to store.

The paper asks: do we really need that many?

👥

Headwise sharing

One $E$ and one $F$ per layer, shared across all 12 heads. That drops 288 matrices to $12 \times 2 = 24$.

🔑

Key-Value sharing

Also force $E_i = F_i = E$ inside each layer — the same squeeze for keys and values. Now just $12$ matrices, one per layer.

🌍

Layerwise sharing

One single $E$ for every layer, every head, keys and values. Just $1$ matrix for the whole model.

Surprisingly, even the most extreme sharing barely hurts. In pretraining, the validation perplexity — a score where lower means the model predicts masked words better — for all three sharing types stays almost on top of the no-sharing curve. The single-matrix layerwise model is essentially just as good. That means you can add Linformer with almost zero extra parameters.

Go deeper: non-uniform $k$ and other squeezes
Figure 1 on the right showed higher layers are more low-rank than lower layers — their spectrum is more skewed. So not every layer needs the same $k$. You can give top layers a smaller $k$ and save even more.
And $E$ and $F$ do not have to be learned linear maps. You can also squeeze with mean or max pooling, or a 1D convolution whose kernel and stride are $n/k$ — essentially averaging every $n/k$ positions into one.

Try it below: pick a sharing level and drag each layer's $k$ to see how the total count of matrices and parameters collapses — and why keeping $k$ big at the bottom and small at the top matches what the spectrum told us.

007Pretraining test~7 min

Does Squeezing Hurt Learning?

We squeezed attention from n × n down to n × k. Does the model get dumber? Linformer tested this the hard way: pretrain from scratch and see.

They used the standard recipe from BERT and RoBERTa: BookCorpus plus English Wikipedia, about 3,300 million words, with a masked-language-modeling task. That means hide 15% of words and make the model guess them. Training ran for 250,000 updates on 64 Tesla V100 GPUs. Lower score is better — the score is called perplexity, which is roughly "how confused is the model?"

How much k do you actually need?

In Figure 3a the sequence length is n = 512. The gray Transformer curve and the blue Linformer curves show validation perplexity as training goes on. With k = 128 — only one quarter of 512 — Linformer is already almost on top of the Transformer. Make k bigger, and the curves get even closer.

In Figure 3b the task is harder: n = 1,024. Now k = 128 has a small gap, but k = 256 again catches up. That's still only one quarter of the length.

Figure 3c checks sharing. Remember the extra matrices E and F that do the squeezing? You can share one E, F per layer across heads, share them between keys and values, even share a single E for the whole 12-layer model. That last option adds just one matrix total. All three sharing curves land almost on top of the no-sharing curve. So the cheapest option works.

The big surprise is in Figure 3d. Fix k = 256 and stretch n from 512 to 1,024 to 2,048 to 4,096. After 250k updates, final perplexity is about the same for all four. If performance depended on the ratio n/k, longer n would need bigger k. It doesn't. It depends on k alone. That matches Theorem 2: k can be a constant like Θ(d log d / ε²) that doesn't grow with n.

In plain words: you need enough kept directions — about 128 to 256 in these experiments — but you don't need to keep more just because the text gets longer. The squeeze keeps what matters.

Try it below: pick a k and an n and watch the perplexity curves. Find the smallest k that closes the gap. Then crank n to 4,096 with that same k — does the gap come back? That's Figure 3d in your hands.

008Results that matter~7 min

Fast, Light, and Just as Smart

So we squeezed attention. Did we break anything? That is what this last lesson checks — and the answer is why Linformer matters.

The paper first pretrains Linformer just like the usual Transformer: same data (BookCorpus + English Wikipedia, 3.3 billion words), same masked-language-model job, 250,000 updates on 64 V100 GPUs. Then it fine-tunes on real tasks and measures accuracy, speed, and memory.

Accuracy first. Here are the downstream results the paper reports for the standard setting of n = 512 tokens:

  • RoBERTa-base (normal Transformer): 92.25 average across four tasks — SST-2 93.1, IMDB 94.1, QNLI 90.9, QQP 90.9.
  • Linformer k=256 with layerwise sharing — that is one tiny projection used everywhere: 92.30 average — SST-2 93.1, IMDB 94.1, QNLI 91.2, QQP 90.8. Slightly better.
  • Even k=128 is close: 91.75 average. And with longer pretraining n=1024, k=256, layerwise sharing still gets 92.18.

For comparison, BERT-base gets 91.90 and a distilled 6-layer BERT gets 90.45 on the same setup. In other words, the squeeze does not hurt learning — and it does not need a bigger k when n gets longer. Fix k=256 and grow n from 512 to 4096, and pretraining perplexity stays about the same (Figure 3d). Performance depends on k, not on the ratio n/k.

Key idea: Linformer is proven O(n) — work grows like n × k with k fixed — not n × n. Theory (Theorem 2) and training both say k can be a constant like 128 or 256.

Now speed and memory — where linear really pays off. Measured on a 16GB V100, with layerwise sharing:

  • n=512, k=128: 1.5× faster, 1.7× larger batch fits in memory.
  • n=4096: 3.4× faster, 14× memory saving.
  • n=8192: 5.5× / 28×.
  • n=16384: 8.6× / 56×.
  • n=65536: 20× faster and about 60× memory saving.

The longer the text, the bigger the win. A standard Transformer hits a wall — doubling length quadruples work. Linformer just doubles work when you double length.

That unlocks things that were impractical before: reading a whole book chapter, a long document, or a high-resolution image as one long sequence, on device or with much less energy. No distillation, no special hardware — just noticing attention was secretly simple.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Sequence length (n)

Length of input sequence, e.g. number of words. Linformer cost grows with n.

Embedding

List of numbers representing a word. Transformers use matrices Q, K, V made of these.

Self-attention

The core Transformer operation where each token creates a new representation by weighted averaging all other tokens.

Context mapping matrix (P)

n×n table P in equation (2) where row i holds how much token i attends to every token j. Softmax makes rows sum to 1.

Softmax

Math function that turns numbers into positive weights that sum to 1. exp(A)/sum(exp(A)).

Complexity O(n), O(n²)

Computers' way to say how time or memory grows with n. O(n²) means 2x length → 4x cost. O(n) means 2x length → 2x cost.

Rank / Low-rank

Rank = number of independent directions needed to describe a matrix. Low-rank = few directions are enough; matrix is compressible.

SVD (Singular Value Decomposition)

Method to break any matrix into singular values (importance scores) and vectors. Keeping only the biggest few gives best low-rank approximation.

Singular values / Spectrum

Importance scores from SVD, sorted biggest to smallest. Long-tail means first few hold most information.

Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection

Random matrix R with entries from Normal(0,1/k) that squashes n dimensions to k while keeping distances. Key to Linformer's proof.

Projection matrices E, F

Linformer's n×k matrices E_i, F_i that squash key (K) and value (V) sequences from length n to k << n.

Projected dimension (k)

Projected length k, e.g. 128 or 256. Linformer cost is O(n·k) ≈ O(n) when k is fixed and much smaller than n.

Perplexity / GLUE tasks

Pretraining score: lower means model predicts masked words better. Finetuning tests same model on downstream tasks like SST-2, IMDB, QNLI, QQP.
