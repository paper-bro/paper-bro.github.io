001Lesson 1 — The problem~8 min

A flashlight the size of a virus

Normal light is too clumsy to see tiny things. In the mid-infrared, one wave of light is about 10,000 nanometers wide. A layer that is 20 nanometers thick — about the size of a virus — is invisible to it. It just washes over it.

Scattering-type scanning near-field microscopy, or s-SNOM, cheats the limit with a needle. It is a sharp tip from an atomic force microscope, about 30 nanometers round at the very end, held just a few nanometers above the sample. When light hits the tip, the tip squeezes the light into a tiny hot spot right under its apex, like a flashlight shrunk to the size of a virus. That hot spot feels the sample only within about 30 nanometers around the tip — far below the normal light limit.

This matters because most real samples are sandwiches. Phone chips, semiconductors, chemical coatings, and stacks of atom-thin materials like graphene are all layers on top of layers. Even a messy surface looks like a neat flat sandwich in the tiny patch the tip can see. The light the tip scatters back carries clues about each layer: what it is made of (described by a number called permittivity, ε), how thick it is, and how the layers talk to each other through trapped light waves called polaritons.

🔬

Rigorous but slow

Full computer simulations (FEM, FDTD, BEM) chop space into tiny pieces and solve the electric field exactly. Accurate, but very heavy — hours for one spectrum — and they still have to guess the tip shape.

⚡

Fast but cheating

Simple formulas like the point-dipole model or the finite-dipole model (FDM) replace the tip by a few fake dots of charge and one fitted number g. Fast, but they smear out how a layered sample responds to different rippliness of the field.

The paper starts from this gap. Earlier multilayer versions of the fast models were only tested on weak, gentle molecular wiggles. When the authors of those models tried a tip sitting resonantly on a thin film, they already saw the biggest errors. For strong resonators that can trap light well — like SiO2, Al2O3 and SiC — the errors get much worse, and the fitted g stops working when you change film thickness or look at a different harmonic.

The goal of this paper is to keep both advantages at once: the speed of a formula and the precision of a supercomputer simulation. In their earlier work the authors solved the tip exactly as a stretched sphere — a prolate spheroid — for a simple bulk sample. Now they extend that exact solution to any flat stack of layers. That is the model this course builds, step by step.

The trick is not a better tip shape, but a better way to make the sample answer back — using a handful of mirror charges hidden underground.

002Lesson 2 — Locking onto the wiggle~9 min

Why the tip wiggles — and why we listen to overtones

Hold your finger near a candle and you feel heat only when you get very close. The s-SNOM tip works the same way with light: the light it scatters barely changes when the tip is 100 nanometers up, but jumps sharply in the last 10 to 20 nanometers.

To catch just that jump, the instrument wiggles the tip up and down like a tiny pogo stick. The paper writes the height as

$$H(t)=H_0 + A\,(1-\cos \Omega t)$$

$H_0$ is the closest gap — about 1 to 5 nanometers, never known perfectly because forces, roughness and a thin contamination layer shift it. $A$ is the tap size, typically 40 to 75 nanometers. $\Omega$ is how fast it taps, a few hundred thousand times a second. So $H$ swings from $H_0$ at the bottom to $H_0+2A$ at the top, sinusoidally.

At any fixed height the scattered light is

$$E_{\text{sca}}(H) \propto (1+\varsigma\, r_p)^2\, \alpha_z(H)\, E_{\text{inc},z}$$

That's Eq.1. $E_{\text{inc},z}$ is the incoming vertical field. $r_p$ is the ordinary Fresnel reflection for p-polarized light — the far-field bounce off the sample. $\varsigma$ just counts that bounce; the paper uses $\varsigma=1$. The interesting part is $\alpha_z(H)$, the vertical polarizability of the tip: how much charge the tip can separate at height $H$. It contains all the near-field interaction. Everything else is just illumination.

Now watch what happens as $H$ wiggles. $E_{\text{sca}}(H(t))$ is no longer a sine wave — the spike near contact makes it pointy. A lock-in amplifier listens to that wiggly time trace and picks out its overtones, like hearing the higher notes of a plucked string:

$$S_n \propto \Omega \int_0^{2\pi/\Omega} E_{\text{sca}}(H(t))\, e^{-i n\Omega t}\, dt \propto (1+\varsigma\, r_p)^2\, \alpha_{n,z}$$

That's Eq.2. $S_n$ is the signal at $n$ times the tap frequency. $\alpha_{n,z}$ is the $n$th Fourier component of $\alpha_z(H(t))$. Labs usually quote $n=2,3,4$. $n=1$ still carries a big far-field background that hardly depends on $H$. $n=2$ and higher come almost only from the strongly nonlinear part right at the apex — that's the background suppression.

One problem remains: the laser flickers and the alignment drifts. So experiments never report raw $S_n$. They divide by a known reference measured right afterwards, often silicon or gold:

$$\sigma_n = \frac{S_{\text{s},n}}{S_{\text{r},n}} = \frac{(1+\varsigma\, r_{p,\text{s}})^2\, \alpha_{\text{s},n,z}}{(1+\varsigma\, r_{p,\text{r}})^2\, \alpha_{\text{r},n,z}}$$

That's Eq.3. The s stands for sample, r for reference. In the whole paper every spectrum you will see is this ratio $\sigma_n$, with its size $s_n=|\sigma_n|$ and its phase $\varphi_n=\arg(\sigma_n)$. It's what lets us compare theory and experiment without guessing the laser power.

Key idea: a smooth up-and-down motion creates a spiky light signal. The higher overtones $n=2,3,4$ keep only the spike — the true near-field.

003Lesson 3 — The tip as a shiny football~10 min

A rugby ball for a tip: the exact spheroid model

A real s-SNOM tip is a bumpy chunk of metal on a cantilever. The paper doesn't model every bump — it replaces the tip with something much cleaner: a stretched sphere, a prolate spheroid, like a rugby ball standing on its point just above the sample.

Why a rugby ball? It has exactly the two things that matter. Its overall length sets how much metal can polarize, and its tiny tip curvature sets how tightly charge piles up at the bottom. Length is L = 2a, where a is the long half-axis. The fat half-axis is b. The sharpness you actually see in a microscope photo is the apex radius:

$$R = \frac{b^{2}}{a}$$

Turn it around: if you know R and L, you know the whole shape: a = L/2 and b = \sqrt{R\,a}. Example from the paper: R=30 nm and L=600 nm → a=300 nm, b≈95 nm. A longer ball with the same tip radius must get a little fatter too, but its point stays just as sharp.

The light itself gets simple here. Mid-infrared light has a wavelength around 10,000 nm. The tip radius (30 nm) and its wiggle (40–75 nm) are hundreds of times smaller. The paper therefore uses the quasi-electrostatic approximation: ignore waves that travel, treat the incident light as a steady vertical field E₀ and solve plain old static electricity. It also keeps only the vertical polarization — the long needle barely feels a sideways field.

Static electricity in rugby-ball coordinates is neat. Define ξ = \cosh μ and η = \cos ν (think latitude and longitude stretched around the ball) and c = \sqrt{a^{2}-b^{2}}, the distance from center to each focus. The surface is ξ = ξ₀ = a/c. Solutions are built from Legendre functions Pₙ and Qₙ — standard wavy patterns on a spheroid, like harmonics on a string, labeled n = 0,1,2,…

Charge on the surface depends only on η (height along the ball) and can be written as a sum over those patterns:

$$\rho(\eta)=\frac{\sum_{n}(2n+1)\upsilon_{n}P_{n}(\eta)}{2\pi c\sqrt{(\xi_{0}^{2}-1)(\xi_{0}^{2}-\eta^{2})}}$$

The paper splits this into ρ₀ — what a lone ball would have in E₀ (known in closed form) — plus ρᵢ — the extra charge pulled in by the sample underneath. Enforcing that the electric potential is continuous and that the jump in electric displacement matches the surface charge at ξ₀ gives a never-ending set of linear equations for the unknown coefficients ϑₙ = υₙ/E₀:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathcal{M}_{mn}\,\vartheta_{n} = \mathcal{C}_{m}$$

with

$$\mathcal{M}_{mn}=\delta_{mn}-\frac{\beta(\varepsilon-1)(2n+1)J_{nm}}{2P_{m}(\xi_{0})\left[\varepsilon\frac{Q_{m}(\xi_{0})}{P_{m}(\xi_{0})}-\frac{\xi_{0}Q_{m}(\xi_{0})-Q_{m-1}(\xi_{0})}{\xi_{0}P_{m}(\xi_{0})-P_{m-1}(\xi_{0})}\right]}$$
$$\mathcal{C}_{m}=-\frac{\beta c(\varepsilon-1)^{2}J_{1m}}{4P_{m}(\xi_{0})\left[\varepsilon Q_{1}(\xi_{0})-\xi_{0}Q_{0}(\xi_{0})+\frac{\xi_{0}^{2}}{\xi_{0}^{2}-1}\right]\left[\varepsilon\frac{Q_{m}(\xi_{0})}{P_{m}(\xi_{0})}-\frac{\xi_{0}Q_{m}(\xi_{0})-Q_{m-1}(\xi_{0})}{\xi_{0}P_{m}(\xi_{0})-P_{m-1}(\xi_{0})}\right]}$$

Don't memorize them. Notice the structure: everything about the ball's material ε and shape Pₙ,Qₙ,ξ₀ sits in the denominators. All influence of the sample sits in one place: β J_{nm}.

β = (ε_{s}-1)/(ε_{s}+1) for a bulk sample is just a number set by the sample's permittivity. J_{nm} is pure geometry:

$$J_{nm}=\int_{-1}^{1} P_{n}(\eta)\,P_{m}(\eta_{q})Q_{m}(\xi_{q})\,d\eta$$

where (ξ_{q},η_{q}) are the coordinates of the sample's mirror charge seen from the ball. J_{nm} is calculated once for a given height H and reused for every color of light.

Finally the field the outside world sees is the dipole moment per field, the vertical polarizability:

$$\alpha_{z}(H)=2ac\,\vartheta_{1}(H)+\alpha_{z0}$$

Only the n=1 coefficient ϑ₁ matters — the dipole part. α_{z0} is the ball alone; it disappears after we wiggle and keep only harmonics n=2,3,4.

Key insight: shape and sample separate. Shape (a,b,c,ξ₀,Pₙ,Qₙ) lives everywhere, sample (β) enters only as βJ_{nm}. Next lesson swaps that single β for a layered sample's k-dependent echo.

In practice you truncate the infinite system at order N (about 20–60 is enough) and increase N until α_z stops changing. The paper goes much further — deriving the image series for layers — but for the tip itself this is it.

004Lesson 4 — How the sample answers back~10 min

The sample's echo: one bounce vs many

Imagine holding a single charged speck a few nanometers above a table. How does the table talk back? In electrostatics the whole answer can be written as an echo.

For a point charge $q$ at height $z_0$ above a flat sample, the potential above the surface is always:

$$\varphi(\mathbf{r},z)= q\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0|^2+(z-z_0)^2}} + q\,\iint \frac{d^2\mathbf{k}_\parallel}{2\pi k_\parallel}\, r_e(\mathbf{k}_\parallel)\, e^{i\mathbf{k}_\parallel\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)-k_\parallel(z+z_0)}$$

The first term is just your speck. The second term is the sample's echo. It is a sum over ripples with in-plane wavevector $\mathbf{k}_\parallel$ — think "how wavy the field is across the surface." Big $k_\parallel$ = tight, short ripples. Each ripple bounces with its own reflection strength $r_e(\mathbf{k}_\parallel)$, the electrostatic reflection coefficient.

For a bulk, uniform slab with permittivity $\varepsilon$ (how easily it polarizes), $r_e$ does not depend on waviness at all:

$$r_e(\mathbf{k}_\parallel) \equiv -\beta_0 = \frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}$$

$\beta_0 = (\varepsilon-1)/(\varepsilon+1)$ is just a number. The $k_\parallel$ integral can then be done on paper, and the whole echo becomes exactly one image charge $q_i = -\beta_0 q$ sitting the same distance under the surface at $-z_0$. One bounce, done. For a uniaxial crystal with its axis straight up, replace $\varepsilon$ by $\sqrt{\varepsilon_\perp \varepsilon_\parallel}$ — same idea.

A thin layer of thickness $d$ and permittivity $\varepsilon_l$ on a substrate $\varepsilon_s$ is different. Now the wave feels two interfaces — the top and the buried one — and has to travel down and up through $d$. The result depends on $k_\parallel d$:

$$r_e(k_\parallel)= -\frac{\beta_1+\beta_2 e^{-2k_\parallel d}}{1+\beta_1\beta_2 e^{-2k_\parallel d}}$$

with $\beta_1=(\varepsilon_l-1)/(\varepsilon_l+1)$ for air–layer and $\beta_2=(\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_l)/(\varepsilon_s+\varepsilon_l)$ for layer–substrate. The factor $e^{-2k_\parallel d}$ is the round-trip loss for a wavy field crossing the film. Tight ripples (large $k_\parallel$) barely reach the bottom; gentle ripples do.

If $|\beta_1\beta_2|<1$ you can expand that fraction as a geometric series $1 - x + x^2 \dots$ with $x=\beta_1\beta_2 e^{-2k_\parallel d}$. Each power becomes one more image at depth $z_0+2nd$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$ — an infinite line of underground mirrors. When $\varepsilon_l$ goes negative — as it does inside the Reststrahlen band of phonons in SiC or SiO2 — $|\beta_1\beta_2|$ can exceed 1 and that neat series breaks. Exactly the strongly resonant case the old models were warned about.

That failure is not a math curiosity. A negative-$\varepsilon_l$ with low loss lets the film trap light as a surface wave. $r_e(k_\parallel)$ then spikes sharply at that wave's $k_\parallel$, and a single image can never make a spike.

Predict first: for $\varepsilon_l=-5$, $\varepsilon_s=12$, will $|\beta_1\beta_2|$ be above 1?

005Lesson 5 — Trick 1: fake charges underground~11 min

Underground copies: the generalized image method

Last lesson the sample answered a point charge with an echo. For a solid block, that echo is simple: one underground mirror copy. For a thin film — a layer of thickness $d$ on a different substrate — the echo depends on how wiggly the field is. Physicists label wiggliness by $k$, the in-plane wavevector. A flat field has small $k$, a ripply field has large $k$.

That’s the headache. The layer’s electrostatic reflection coefficient is

$$ r_e(k) = -\frac{\beta_1 + \beta_2 e^{-2kd}}{1+\beta_1\beta_2 e^{-2kd}} $$

with $\beta_1=(\varepsilon_l-1)/(\varepsilon_l+1)$ for the top surface and $\beta_2=(\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_l)/(\varepsilon_s+\varepsilon_l)$ for the buried interface, $\varepsilon_l$ the layer permittivity and $\varepsilon_s$ the substrate one. The factor $e^{-2kd}$ says: to feel the buried interface, the field must cross the layer (down $d$ and back up $d$). When $\varepsilon_l$ turns negative — inside a phonon Reststrahlen band like SiC — $|\beta_1\beta_2|$ can exceed 1 and the usual infinite series of images blows up.

💡 The GICM trick: hide the $k$-dependence inside one number $x=e^{-2kd}$. As $k$ goes from 0 to ∞, $x$ slides from 1 to 0 — a closed interval $[0,1]$. For a real lossy material, $r_e(x)$ is smooth and finite there. A classic math theorem (Weierstrass) says any such smooth curve can be made as close as you want by a polynomial in $x$.

So the paper writes, in a simplified version,

$$ r_e(k) \approx -\beta_1 - \sum_{n=1}^{K} C_n e^{-2nkd} $$

Pick the numbers $C_n$ by a quick least-squares fit. Now each term $e^{-2nkd}$ is exactly the $k$-signature of an image charge. Fourier-transforming gives a very physical picture (Fig. 1c):

$$ \varphi \approx \frac{q}{\sqrt{r^2+(z-z_0)^2}} - \frac{\beta_1 q}{\sqrt{r^2+(z+z_0)^2}} - \sum_{n=1}^{K} \frac{C_n q}{\sqrt{r^2+(z+z_0+2nd)^2}} $$

One copy $q_0=-\beta_1 q$ at mirror depth $z_0$, plus $K$ deeper copies $q_n=-C_n q$ spaced every $2d$, like reflections inside the slab. More generally you can use a different spacing $d_\text{eff}$: $r_e\approx-\beta_1-\sum C_n e^{-2nk d_\text{eff}}$. The paper finds $d_\text{eff}\le d$ converges fastest; for a stack of many layers, choose the spacing equal to the thinnest layer. Too large $d_\text{eff}$ makes the images too far apart to catch a sharp resonance.

Plugging it into the tip model

Recall the spheroid tip from Lesson 3: all sample influence entered only through $\beta J_{nm}$, a geometric coupling integral $J_{nm}$ multiplied by that one $\beta$. GICM changes that one spot:

$$ \beta J_{nm} \;\to\; \beta_1 J_{nm} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} C_k J_{nm}^{k} $$

$J_{nm}^{k}$ is the same integral, just computed for the $k$-th underground spheroid. Nothing else in the linear system $\sum M_{mn}\vartheta_n=C_m$ changes. That’s why the method stays analytic and fast.

In practice $K=5$ already matches heavy finite-element (FEM) simulations for a 20-nm hypothetical film (Lesson 7), $K=10$ works for most real films like SiO₂, and only very thin low-loss SiC near its phonon needs $K=20$ — still seconds on a laptop instead of hours.

Use the lab below to feel convergence: start with $K=0$ (one mirror) and watch the sharp polariton bump refuse to appear, then add copies. Slide $d_\text{eff}$ to 2.5× and see the same $K=15$ suddenly fail — the copies are too spread out.

006Lesson 6 — Trick 2: sum of waves~10 min

Building from ripples: the Fourier picture

Lesson 5 replaced a messy reflection with a handful of underground mirror charges. This lesson shows the other road to the same answer: don't fit images at all — keep the reflection as a sum of ripples.

Recall the sample's answer to a point charge. For every waviness $k_{\parallel}$ — think of it as how tightly the field ripples sideways — the surface has a reflection number $r_e(k_{\parallel})$. For a plain bulk sample that number is the same for all ripples: $r_e = -\beta_0$. For a thin layer it wiggles with $k_{\parallel}$ because a ripple has to travel down to the buried interface and back, picking up $e^{-2k_{\parallel}d}$:

$$r_e(k_{\parallel}) = -\frac{\beta_1+\beta_2 e^{-2k_{\parallel}d}}{1+\beta_1\beta_2 e^{-2k_{\parallel}d}}$$

That formula was the problem: when the layer's permittivity $\varepsilon_l$ turns negative in a phonon band, $r_e$ develops a sharp peak at the polariton wavevector and the image idea from last lesson needs many terms.

The Fourier Integral Method, or FIM, skips the fitting step. It takes the reflected part of the potential — the integral over all $k_{\parallel}$ — and plugs it straight into the boundary condition on the rugby-ball tip. The rest of the math stays exactly the same infinite linear system $\sum M_{mn}\vartheta_n = C_m$ from Lesson 3. Only the coupling term changes. Where the bulk model had $\beta J_{nm}$, the layered model now has

$$\mathcal{J}_{nm}= -\pi P_n(\xi_0)\int_0^{\infty}\frac{r_e(k_{\parallel})}{k_{\parallel}} I_{n+1/2}(k_{\parallel}c)\, I_{m+1/2}(k_{\parallel}c)\, e^{-2k_{\parallel}(a+H)}\, dk_{\parallel}$$

$P_n$ is the Legendre shape, $\xi_0=a/c$ describes how stretched the tip is, $I$ is a modified Bessel function that knows the tip's size $c$, and $e^{-2k_{\parallel}(a+H)}$ is a filter: only ripples whose wavelength is comparable to the tip-sample gap $H$ matter. Big $k_{\parallel}$ — very fine ripples — are exponentially ignored.

In words: FIM says "add up the response to every ripple, weighted by how much the tip can feel that ripple." GICM says "fake the same wiggly $r_e$ with a polynomial and turn each power into an underground charge." They are mathematically the same.

Why two methods then? Numerically they are mirror images. If you insert the GICM polynomial $r_e \approx -\beta_1 -\sum C_n e^{-2nk_{\parallel}d_{\text{eff}}}$ into the FIM integral, each term integrates analytically to exactly the $J_{nm}^k$ image term from last lesson. Doing the integral gives a closed form with a hypergeometric function $_4F_3$ (Eq. S77 in the paper) — so GICM needs no numerical $k$-integral at all.

Typically GICM needs only 5 to 20 images, while FIM needs 20 to 40 $k$-points, so both are fast. FIM becomes mandatory for hyperbolic materials like hexagonal boron nitride (hBN). There $\varepsilon$ is negative in one direction and positive in the other, in two bands: type-I 760–825 cm$^{-1}$ and type-II 1366–1610 cm$^{-1}$. One hBN flake already supports many guided modes, so $r_e(k_{\parallel})$ wiggles rapidly many times. A polynomial would need huge $K$ and becomes unstable. FIM avoids this by integrating along a gently deformed path in the complex $k$-plane that steps away from the sharp poles, and by compressing the geometry part onto a few "skeleton" momenta.

The lab lets you see this equivalence break down on purpose. Toggle between an ordinary film with one resonance and a hyperbolic stack with five, and watch the wave integral and the image sum track — or fail to track — each other.

Try: start with the ordinary film, $K=1$, $H=3$ nm. Increase $K$ and watch the red fit snap onto the black $r_e(k)$ and the mismatch in $J$ drop below 5%. Then switch to hyperbolic hBN: even $K=15$ leaves wiggles. Nudge $d_{\text{eff}}/d$ to 2.5 and see why the paper insists on $d_{\text{eff}} \le d$.

007Lesson 7 — Test against a supercomputer~10 min

20 nanometers vs the supercomputer

Take the exact same needle — same length, same sharpness, same wiggle — and slide a 20-nanometer film under it. The light the needle scatters changes shape completely. That is what the authors tested first, before touching any real material.

They invented a fake material on the computer. Its ability to polarize, written $\varepsilon$, was built from three textbook pieces at once (Fig.1d): a broad Drude piece ($\omega_p=500$, $\gamma=150$ cm$^{-1}$) for free electrons like in a metal, a strong Lorentz vibration ($A=1200$ at $1000$ cm$^{-1}$) for a strong phonon, and a weak Lorentz vibration ($A=500$ at $1800$ cm$^{-1}$) for a faint molecular bond. One film, three personalities.

They put that 20-nm film on silicon ($\varepsilon_{Si}=12$) and asked two calculators for the tip signal, both with the identical geometry $R=30$ nm, $L=600$ nm, tapping $A=50$ nm, gap $H_0=3$ nm: a heavy finite-element simulation (FEM) that meshes space into tiny cubes and solves everything numerically, and their new analytical MESM with only $K=5$ underground image charges.

The answer: the two curves lie on top of each other for every color and for overtones $n=2,3,4$ when normalized to bulk silicon. Fast math with 5 fake charges = supercomputer precision.

But the spectrum itself is new physics. In a thick bulk crystal each resonance gives one peak. In a thin film you have two surfaces — top and bottom — each can host its own surface wave. The two waves talk through the film and hybridize into two joint modes, often called even and odd. In the experiment that looks like one Lorentz peak splitting into two.

Not all peaks split equally. The faint molecular peak at 1800 cm$^{-1}$ barely notices the bottom interface and stays single. The strong phonon at 1000 cm$^{-1}$ and the Drude background split clearly — because their electric fields are strong enough to reach across the 20 nm and couple.

📏

Thickness matters

5-nm SiC on Si needs $K=20$ to converge; 20-nm hypothetical film needs $K=5$; most everyday cases like SiO2 900–1200 cm$^{-1}$ need $K=10$ (Fig.S3).

⚡

Loss saves you

If Re($\varepsilon$)<0 and Im($\varepsilon$)<0.1, the polariton is ultra-sharp in wavevector $k$. The polynomial in $x=e^{-2kd}$ needs many terms to catch that spike.

🧱

Stack still works

A 10-nm SiO2 on 20-nm SiC on Si is not just a sum of two spectra — modes shift and distort. MESM with the thinnest-layer spacing still matches FEM (Fig.S5).

008Lesson 8 — Real films: one setting fits all~10 min

Three thicknesses, one truth: PMMA

PMMA is a good honesty test. It's the clear plastic in plexiglass: in the mid-infrared it has only one gentle wiggle near 1730 cm−1 — a weak molecular vibration. That sounds boring, which is the point. If a model is just fudging numbers, it can still fake a single boring peak. Catching it takes more than one peak.

The experiment in the paper does exactly that. Using data from Mester et al., the authors look at PMMA layers of 16 nm, 40 nm and 100 nm, all sitting on silicon (Si, εSi=11.7), measured with the same tip. The tip in the model is a rugby-ball spheroid with R=30 nm, tapping amplitude A=40 nm and length L=400 nm — any L from 300 to 500 nm works almost the same for this weak resonance. The only numbers they allow to adjust are the closest approaches: H0,PMMA=3 nm above PMMA and H0,Si=4.2 nm above the Si reference. Both are in the physically reasonable 1–5 nm window where real tips actually hover.

From those, the MESM calculates the normalized signal σn = Ss,n/Sr,n that experiments quote. Its size is sn=|σn| and its shift is φn=arg(σn) — amplitude and phase at harmonic n. The key result: one single geometry fits everything at once. With no re-tuning, the MESM reproduces sn and φn for n=3 and n=4, and for all three thicknesses. As PMMA gets thicker, the peak grows and changes shape because thicker film samples different “wavinesses” k of the near field. Changing harmonic from 3 to 4 probes a different nonlinear slice of how αz(H) varies as the tip taps. Passing both tests together means the physics is right, not just the fit.

A stricter test beats a pretty fit. Fitting one spectrum only proves you have enough knobs. Fitting three thicknesses × two harmonics with one knob setting proves the knob means something real — here, the actual tip shape and gap.

Now give the older fast model the same chance. The finite-dipole model (FDM) keeps the same R, L, A, H0 but adds one complex fudge factor g. Tuned to g = 0.76·exp(0.07i) — about magnitude 0.76 and angle 4 degrees — it can match one PMMA curve reasonably well. But keep that g and switch thickness or harmonic, and it fails. The paper quantifies this as |Δs4| = |s4calc − s4exp| and |Δφ4|: the FDM errors are up to 10 times larger than the MESM errors across the three thicknesses. In short: MESM is transferable, FDM is not.

That transferability is what makes quantitative tomography possible — turning light into thickness and ε without cheating per image. 009Lesson 9 — Strong resonance and the payoff~11 min

From one film to photoswitchable polaritons — and what's next

Think of a thin drum skin stretched over a different material. Tap it, and the skin and the floor underneath vibrate together. Make the skin a 50-nanometre sheet of silicon carbide — SiC — and make the floor a piece of InAs that you can flip from glass-like to metal-like with a flash of light. That is the paper's toughest test.

Why harder than the PMMA films from last lesson? SiC is a strong resonator. Inside its Reststrahlen band — roughly $797$ to $973\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ — its real permittivity becomes negative. In plain words: $\varepsilon$ tells how much a material polarizes; negative $\varepsilon$ means it pushes back and reflects almost everything, trapping light as surface phonon polaritons. A 50-nm film then does that drum trick twice: the wave on the top surface hybridizes with the wave on the bottom surface, giving two distinct peaks inside that band instead of one.

The InAs substrate adds a photo-switch. Without the pump pulse InAs is a normal dielectric with $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{InAs}}\approx 11.8$ (positive, transparent). With a near-infrared pump it fills with free electrons and is described by a Drude metal model: $$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{InAs}}(\omega)=\varepsilon_{\infty}\left[1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2+i\gamma_p\omega}\right]$$ with $\omega_p=845\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $\gamma_p=125\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Here $\omega_p$ is the plasma frequency — how fast the electron sea sloshes. Suddenly the floor is metallic. The SiC phonon and the InAs plasmon now couple ultrastrongly, and the measured near-field spectrum $s_n=|\sigma_n|$, $\varphi_n=\arg\sigma_n$ for harmonics $n=2,3$ changes dramatically — same physical stack, totally different light fingerprint.

The paper asks: can one model follow that switch without cheating? The MESM answers yes, with one unchanged tip: apex radius $R=75\,\mathrm{nm}$, length $L=2.0\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, tapping $A=75\,\mathrm{nm}$. That $L$ is longer than for PMMA because strong resonators are sensitive to it; make it too long and you break the quasi-static approximation ($R,A\ll\lambda$, here $\lambda\sim10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ so electrostatic treatment is okay, but not if $L$ approaches $\lambda$). And you must not fix $H_0$. For a strongly resonant film the tip and sample form ultra-narrow gap modes — razor-sharp spikes whose position jumps with a nanometre of $H_0$ (Fig.S4c). You never see them in real data because roughness and wiggle average them out. The paper mimics this by averaging spectra over $H_{0,\mathrm{SiC}}=1$–$5\,\mathrm{nm}$ and smoothing with a $12.5\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ Gaussian, normalized to bulk non-excited InAs at $H_{0}=0.1\,\mathrm{nm}$. With just that, K around 10–20 image charges, the MESM reproduces both $n=2$ and $n=3$ amplitude and phase — both InAs states, same numbers, in seconds.

Transferability is the point. In quantitative spectroscopy you want changes in the spectrum to mean changes in the sample ($\varepsilon$, thickness $d$), not changes in your fudge factor. MESM's geometry transfers across thicknesses, harmonics, and now across a light-switched substrate.

The older finite-dipole model (FDM) cannot. Using the same $R$ and $A$ but its best fitted $L=400\,\mathrm{nm}$ and complex parameter $g=1.6e^{0.27i}$ for non-excited InAs, it misses the lower SiC peak and shows spurious fast wiggles — a sign it does not converge. Keep that $g$ and flip InAs to metallic: it stays almost flat where the big experimental peak should be (Fig.S7a). Refit to $g=1.65e^{0.007i}$ and it can mimic the photoexcited case, but now it blows up the non-excited spectrum (Fig.S7b). No single $g$ works for both states of the same sample — exactly the failure you saw for PMMA, amplified.

Paper does not claim magic. $L$ has no direct ruler on the real tip, $H_0$ is uncertain to nanometres (STM-controlled tips may help), gap-mode averaging is phenomenological, and the whole theory is quasi-static — toward visible wavelengths you need retardation. The MESM still needs the same careful convergence checks you practiced with $K$ and $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$.

The payoff: a fast, systematically convergent forward model plus an open tool at snomcalculator.streamlit.app that runs the same GICM/FIM engine in your browser. That lets you invert spectra — extract local $\varepsilon(\omega)$ and $d$ — for tomography of van-der-Waals stacks, doped semiconductors, or photoswitchable polaritons. Try it below: flip the InAs switch yourself and see which model stays honest without retuning.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

s-SNOM (scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy)

A microscope that uses a sharp wiggling tip to concentrate light and feel the optical response very close to a sample surface, far below the normal light limit.

near-field

Light interaction that happens within nanometers of the tip; it decays very fast with distance and carries nanoscale information.

dielectric permittivity ε

Material property that tells how strongly it polarizes in an electric field. Written ε, often complex: real part stores energy, imaginary part absorbs it.

electrostatic reflection coefficient r_e(k)

Response of a surface in electrostatics to each spatial wave of the field. For bulk it's a single number β=(ε-1)/(ε+1). For a thin layer it depends on wavevector k_parallel.

quasi-electrostatic approximation

When tip-sample distance is small compared to light wavelength, light can be treated as static electric field. Valid here because R ~30 nm and A ~40-75 nm << mid-IR wavelength ~10 µm.

prolate spheroid

Stretched sphere shaped like a rugby ball / American football. Used to mimic the s-SNOM tip: length L=2a, waist radius b, apex curvature R=b²/a.

polarizability α_z(H)

How easily the tip's charge separates in an external field. Depends on tip shape and on distance H to sample. Demodulated versions α_{n,z} determine measured signal.

demodulation harmonic n

Tip height oscillates as H(t)=H₀+A(1-cos Ωt). Detected light is filtered at multiples n of tapping frequency Ω; n=2,3,4 are used to remove far-field background.

normalized near-field signal σ_n

Normalized signal σ_n = S_{s,n}/S_{r,n} = [(1+ς r_{p,s})² α_{s,n}]/[(1+ς r_{p,r})² α_{r,n}]; sample signal divided by a known reference (e.g. Si).

image charge method

Trick for bulk sample: response to a charge q at height z₀ is same as adding one fake charge q_i=-β q at depth -z₀ underneath the surface.

GICM (generalized image charge method)

Paper's trick 1: approximate layered r_e(k) by polynomial in x=e^{-2kd} : r_e≈-β₁-∑ C_n e^{-2nkd}. Becomes series of K image charges at depths z₀+2nd with strengths -C_n q. Needs K=5 simple, 10 typical, 20 for low-loss SiC.

FIM (Fourier integral method)

Paper's trick 2: keep the k-integral and directly replace βJ_nm → J_nm = -πP_n(ξ₀)∫ r_e(k)/k I_{n+½}(kc)I_{m+½}(kc)e^{-2k(a+H)}dk. Needs ~20-40 k-points. Equivalent to GICM.

coupling integral J_nm

J_nm = ∫ P_n(η)P_m(η_q)Q_m(ξ_q)dη describes electrostatic coupling between charge pattern n on real tip and pattern m of its image. Depends only on geometry.

Reststrahlen band

Spectral region where a polar material's real permittivity is negative and reflects strongly. For SiC around 797-973 cm⁻¹.

thin-film polariton / surface phonon polariton

Mixed light-and-vibration wave trapped in a thin layer. A 20-nm film splits the resonance into two branches (even/odd) from coupling of top and bottom surfaces.

FDM (finite dipole model)

Older tip model using a few discrete effective charges and a fitted complex number g. Works for single bulk spectrum but fails to transfer across thicknesses/harmonics or strongly resonant layers.

FEM (finite-element method)

Heavy computer simulation that divides space into tiny elements and solves Laplace's equation exactly. Accurate but very slow.

gap modes

Narrow gap modes between tip apex and sample that create sharp spikes sensitive to H₀ ~1-5 nm. Averaged over in paper to match experiment.
