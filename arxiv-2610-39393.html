001The puzzle~7 min

A 30-year chance is not a place

A long-term earthquake forecast sounds useful: "6% chance in the next 30 years." But where will it start? Imagine police saying "6% chance of a break-in on your street this year" — that tells you nothing about which house.

That was the problem in central Kyushu, Japan. Two long cracks run through the land: the Futagawa fault zone trending ENE-WSW and the Hinagu fault zone running about 80 km from northeast to southwest. Japan's Earthquake Research Committee (ERC) had studied trenches and geology and said: in the next 30 years, about up to 6% chance the central part of Hinagu will rupture, and up to 16% for the southern part. High enough to worry about, too vague to act on. A percent is not a place, and it says nothing about whether stress is building right now.

Then in April 2016 the ground proved it. On 14 April an M6.5 quake hit the northern Hinagu, on 15 April an M6.4, and on 16 April the M7.3 that ruptured the Futagawa fault and broke the surface with mostly right-lateral slip. The 99% idea will return later — but the important map fact is simple: the northern part had broken. The central and southern Hinagu had not.

The ERC told us how often to expect a rupture in a segment. It did not tell us where inside that segment the next rupture would nucleate — its exact starting point at depth.

Scientists had seen this gap before. At Parkfield, California, they had made a map of a different signal — the b-value — seven years before the 2004 M6.0 earthquake. That map correctly highlighted where the fault was most stressed, and later 99% of the slip in 2004 happened inside that low-b patch. The pattern had even stayed in place for 35 years. Yet the map still could not say where the break would begin. In 2004 the rupture started at the southern end of the old 1966 rupture and ran north — the authors called it a surprise.

This course is about a team that tried to close that gap. After the 2016 Kumamoto sequence they circled a small red rectangle on the central Hinagu — 130.6 to 130.8°E and 32.5 to 32.6°N — and in print in 2019 said: if another large quake comes, this is where it is most likely to start.

In the lab below, try the difference yourself. Run 100 "alternate Earths" with the ERC's 6% and 16% chances. Watch the dots land everywhere along the fault — that's a cloud, not a test. Then switch to the "map pin" view where a place is named in advance. Now a single future hypocentre can prove you right or wrong. Move the sliders and watch what changes — and what stays unknowable.

002The tool~9 min

Counting quakes to measure stress

Imagine counting rocks after a landslide. If you find 1,000 pebbles for every boulder, the slope was breaking in tiny bits. If you find 10 pebbles for every boulder, bigger chunks were breaking. Earthquakes work a lot like that.

Seismologists count them with one simple rule found in 1944 by Gutenberg and Richter:

$$\log_{10} N = a - bM$$

Here M is magnitude — a number for how big a quake is. N is how many quakes in your region and time window have magnitude M or larger. log10 just means "power of 10": log10 100 = 2 because 102 = 100. So the rule says: if you plot log10 N against M, you get a straight line.

Two letters control that line:

  • a — productivity. How high the line sits. More active place? Higher a, more quakes of every size. Paper's course region had more than 3×105 quakes with M≥0 since 2000 — very active.
  • b — slope. How fast big quakes become rare. Steep line (big b, about 1.0 to 1.2) = lots of small quakes, few large. Flat line (small b, about 0.6 to 0.7) = relatively more large quakes.
The paper's stress meter: lab experiments and the Earth's crust both show b goes down when differential stress goes up. Low b ≈ 0.6–0.7 = highly stressed. High b ≈ 1.0–1.2 = relaxed.

That is not just a theory. After the 2016 Kumamoto sequence in Japan, b rose along the parts of the Futagawa and northern Hinagu faults that had ruptured — stress had been released — but it fell in only one unruptured place: the central part of the Hinagu fault zone. And between the M6.5 on 14 April and the M7.3 on 16 April 2016, b rose and the decay of quakes steepened within days as the fault adjusted — b can change on a timescale of days.

Where the paper goes further: the full method also finds the completeness magnitude Mc together with b using the EMR method, corrects for 0.1-magnitude bins, and checks uncertainty with 1,000 bootstrap resamples. For this lesson you only need the line and what its slope means.

003How to map it~10 min

From dots on a map to a volume at depth

Japan's earthquake catalog is a cloud of dots: each tiny quake has a longitude, latitude, depth and magnitude. For Kumamoto the paper used the JMA catalog — magnitudes M ≥ 0, depths shallower than 25 km, inside 130.0–131.3°E and 32.0–33.2°N. That's more than 300,000 dots from 2000 to 6 Sep 2026. How do you turn dots into a stress map?

You don't measure stress directly. You sample it. At each point on a fine map grid — cells 0.02°×0.02°, about 2×2 km — you draw a cylinder of radius r = 5 km around that point and collect every quake inside it. If there are at least 50 quakes above the completeness magnitude Mc (the smallest magnitude the network reliably sees), you estimate a b-value for that spot and color the cell: blue for low b ~0.6–0.7 (high stress), red for high b ~1.0–1.2 (relaxed).

Finding Mc and b together is the trick. The EMR method fits two parts at once: above Mc the dots follow the Gutenberg-Richter law, below Mc they fall off because the network misses them. b is then the maximum-likelihood slope with a small correction for 0.1-magnitude bins, and its wobble is checked by bootstrapping — resampling the same 50+ quakes 1,000 times with replacement.

A map view like that squashes all depths together. But a rupture doesn't start everywhere at once — the 2026 M7.1 started at a single point 16 km deep. To see depth you need a slice.

The authors cut a slice along line A–B following the Hinagu fault zone: from 130.268°E, 32.159°N to 130.97°E, 32.943°N. They took every quake within 5 km on either side of that line — a 10-km-wide swath — and projected it onto the vertical plane through the line. Then they placed nodes every 1 km along the fault and in depth, and did the same 5-km-radius sampling in the plane of the section.

That's why the map and the depth section tell different stories. On the map the red rectangle that was circled in 2019 looks like a patch; in the section it is the top view of a 3D volume that stretches downward. A point can look “outside” the rectangle on the map and still be inside the same low-b volume at depth.

Try it below: change the sampling radius and watch the trade-off. A tiny circle is sharp but leaves white holes where you don't have 50 quakes. A big circle fills the map but blurs the stressed zone away. The paper found r=5 km to be the sweet spot — and it explains why being 2.9 km outside the rectangle still counts.

004The clue after 2016~10 min

The red rectangle that broke the pattern

After the 2016 Kumamoto earthquakes broke the northern end of the Hinagu fault and its neighbor the Futagawa fault, you might expect the whole area to relax. And almost everywhere it did. Scientists measured the b-value — the slope in log10 N = a − bM that tells you how many small quakes you get for every large one — and saw it rise. Low b around 0.6 to 0.7 means highly stressed (relatively more big quakes); high b around 1.0 to 1.2 means relaxed. After 2016, b rose along the ruptured faults, as if stress had been let out.

But one small patch did the opposite. On a map of b-values made from earthquakes between 1 Jan 2017 and 11 Mar 2019, the blue low-b color got deeper in exactly one place while it faded to yellow and red elsewhere. The authors drew a red rectangle around it: longitude 130.6 to 130.8°E, latitude 32.5 to 32.6°N, in the central part of the Hinagu fault zone that had not ruptured in 2016. Of nine rectangular test regions laid along the two fault zones, this was the only one where b fell — by 0.11 — and the test said that fall was highly significant.

Why not just look and say "it looks bluer"? Because noisy catalogs can wiggle. The paper used a formal comparison called the Utsu test.

Here is what the test does, in plain words. You have two samples: N1 quakes before 2016 with slope b1, and N2 quakes after with slope b2. Could both samples come from one shared slope, or do you really need two different slopes? The Akaike Information Criterion (ΔAIC) measures which story fits better. The paper's formula is:

$$\Delta AIC = -2(N_1+N_2)\ln(N_1+N_2) + 2N_1\ln(N_1+N_2\frac{b_1}{b_2}) + 2N_2\ln(N_2+N_1\frac{b_2}{b_1}) - 2$$

From that, the chance that one shared b could explain both samples is roughly $P_b \approx \exp(-\Delta AIC/2 - 2)$. Scientists then look at $\log_{10} P_b$. If $\log_{10} P_b \le -1.3$ (that is $P_b \le 0.05$, a 5% chance), the difference is called significant. If $\log_{10} P_b \le -2.6$, it is highly significant. The paper also demanded a second check: the absolute change $|b_2-b_1|$ must be bigger than the sum of the two bootstrap uncertainties — the wiggle you get by resampling 1,000 times. In the red rectangle, $\log_{10} P_b = -2.75$, so it passed both rules. In every other region, b rose significantly or did not change.

The same square lit up in two completely different ways. The p-value that describes how fast aftershocks fade — rate $\lambda(t)=k(c+t)^{-p}$ — was close to 1 there, which in the Dieterich model means stress is staying constant or building. On the ruptured faults p was greater than 1 (stress falling fast), and south of the square p was less than 1 (falling slowly). And a physics model of post-earthquake slow slip by Pollitz and colleagues placed afterslip on the Futagawa and northern Hinagu faults and ended it right at the southern edge of the 2016 small-quake cloud — leaving the central patch with no release, so stress stayed high. The authors announced this spot at a press conference at the Kumamoto Prefectural Government on 5 Sep 2019. A later study in 2024 with a different b-method still found stress high there.

That is what made the red rectangle special: not that it was the most active place, but that it was the only place that got more stressed while everything around it relaxed.

A note on what the paper goes further than: the full Methods describe the whole-magnitude-range (EMR) way of finding b and Mc together and the Pollitz viscoelastic model. For this lesson, think of those as the careful accounting that makes the simple bluer-means-more-stressed reading trustworthy.

005Holding steady~8 min

Seven years of staying stressed

In 2016 the Hinagu fault and its neighbour the Futagawa fault broke in three big steps — an M6.5, an M6.4 and an M7.3. Before that break happened, a cross-section through the fault — a slice showing distance along the fault left-to-right and depth top-to-bottom — showed a blue blob at depth. Blue here means low $b$-value, about $0.6\text{–}0.7$.

Remember from the last lesson: low $b$ means more big quakes relative to small ones, which in labs and in the Earth means higher stress. That blue blob sat right around where the junction between the Hinagu fault, which trends northeast–southwest, and the Futagawa fault, which trends east-northeast–west-southwest, bends. The three 2016 hypocentres all fell inside that low-$b$ volume. Fault bends are natural stress traps: the straighter parts can slip and relax, but the kink stays loaded.

After the 2016 sequence that junction blob changed colour. On a new cross-section the same area turned yellow to red, $b$ around $0.9\text{–}1.2$ — stress released. But one place did not change. In the central part of the Hinagu zone, around distance $0$ km on the section and at about $16$ km depth, a blue blob with $b$ about $0.6\text{–}0.8$ stayed. That was the red-rectangle area from the map, $130.6\text{–}130.8^\circ\text{E}$, $32.5\text{–}32.6^\circ\text{N}$, seen now in side view.

The paper asks: did that blob flicker away if you look closer in time? They split the seven years from $12$ Mar $2019$ to just before the $2026$ M7.1 into two halves with roughly equal numbers of quakes — $12$ Mar $2019$ to $31$ Dec $2021$ and $1$ Jan $2022$ to just before the quake. Both halves show the same blue spot in the same place. And going further back, that central spot has been blue since $2000$, before and after both the $2016$ and $2026$ sequences, while the straight segments around it turned red after each sequence.

A stress meter that stays blue through seven years and two big sequences is not noise. It points to a bend that never got to slip. Try it below: step through the four time windows and watch the old 2016 blue spot turn red while the central blue spot that predicted the $2026$ start holds steady. Watch the $b$-value at the future M7.1 hypocentre stay low while the 2016 spot rises.

006The test~9 min

The day the prediction was judged

On 28 July 2026 at 16:27 a magnitude 7.1 earthquake started on the central Hinagu fault. A rapid report that night gave a rough place: 130.7°E, 32.6°N, very shallow. A later, more careful location put the true start — the hypocentre, the point at depth where the break began — at 130.680°E, 32.626°N and 16 km down. The same day a M6.1 followed at 17:08 and a M5.8 the next evening, with surface rupture and mostly right-lateral slip (one side sliding past the other).

Seven years earlier, scientists had drawn a red rectangle on a map: 130.6–130.8°E and 32.5–32.6°N, and said this small part of the central Hinagu was the most likely place for the next rupture to begin. At first glance the new epicentre — the map dot of the start — looks like a near miss. It sits just north of that rectangle, 2.9 km outside its edge.

Here is why the paper still calls it a hit. A b-value on the map is not just what happened exactly at that dot. Remember, log10 N = a − bM tells how many larger versus smaller quakes occur, and low b about 0.6–0.7 means highly stressed. To make each dot on the map, the team collected every quake with magnitude above completeness within a cylinder of radius 5 km around that node, and only plotted a value if there were at least 50 quakes. That 5-km radius is their stated rule.

A point 2.9 km from the edge is therefore still inside the sampling volume that gave the rectangle its low b. And a map alone mixes together all depths shallower than 25 km. The break actually began at 16 km. In a cross-section along the fault — distance along the fault versus depth — the blue low-b body (0.6–0.7) extends downward. The 16-km hypocentre lies inside it, exactly where both preliminary slip models (GSI with two planes F1 and F2, and NIED with one plane, both around Mw 6.8 and with large slip greater than 1.2 m crossing the rectangle) later placed their main slip.

Paper detail: before the quake the low-b volume was 0.6–0.7 (blue). After the quake, from 4 Aug to 6 Sep 2026 with the first busy week excluded, b around the hypocentre rose to 0.9–1.2 (yellow to red) — exactly what you expect when stress is released. The low value before and the rise after are both part of the test.

After the quake the meter moved as predicted for stress release. Excluding the very busy first week, seismicity from 4 Aug to 6 Sep 2026 shows b around the hypocentre rose to about 0.9–1.2, from blue to yellow and red. To the authors' knowledge this makes it the first time the starting point of a large earthquake was named in print beforehand.

007Why bends matter~8 min

The bend that kept its low b

Not all parts of a fault are equal. Some sections are straight and smooth. Others have a kink — the direction of the crack turns a little. That kink is called a bend, and the paper shows it is the key to the Kumamoto forecast.

Think of trying to tear paper that has a slight fold in it. The straight parts tear easily. The fold bunches up and resists. On a fault, the straight segments can slip in an earthquake and release their stress. The bend does not slip as much, so stress stays trapped there. Small quakes keep happening around it, and because stress is still high, there are relatively more larger small quakes — so the b-value stays low.

That is exactly what the cross-sections show. At about −20 km along the section through the Hinagu fault zone, the fault bends. In the GSI slip model the strike — the compass direction of the fault — goes from 210° on the northern plane F1 to 225° on the southern plane F2, and the dip — how steeply it tilts down — goes from 61° to 44°. A survey done after the 2016 sequence had found almost the same thing: 212° dipping 60° northeast of that point and 226° dipping 50° southwest, matching within 2° in strike and 6° in dip. The large slip of the 2026 M7.1 — more than 1.2 m — ends right there. The surface ruptures mapped in 2026 run about 37 km southwest from the northern end of the Hinagu zone to about this bend.

Before 2016, the low-b spot (0.6–0.7, blue) sat at the junction where the Hinagu fault (NE–SW) meets the Futagawa fault (ENE–WSW). The M6.5, M6.4 and M7.3 quakes all started there. In 2026 the rupture stopped at the next bend. Bends can both start and stop ruptures — as King & Nábělek showed in 1985 — and along these fault zones they are where b stays low.

Here is why the paper pays so much attention to this one spot: its b-value has been low since 2000, before and after both the 2016 and 2026 sequences. Straight sections turned from blue (low b, stressed) to yellow and red (high b, 0.9–1.2, relaxed) after they slipped, but the bend at −20 km never did. It sits at the southwest end of where 2026 slipped the most. In 2019 the same logic — a persistent low-b zone at the end of a slipped area — correctly pointed to where the 2026 M7.1 would begin. Now the same pattern points south again.

008What next~10 min

The next place to watch is not a date

In the last lesson the 2026 rupture stopped at a kink in the fault around 20 km along the cross-section. The low b-values there never went away. They were there since 2000, before and after both the 2016 and 2026 sequences. That kink is now the end of the big slip, and the b-value there is still low, about 0.6 to 0.8, blue to cyan. The paper says that is where the next rupture is most likely to begin, just as a similar bend was where the 2016 sequence began.

But a place to watch is not just the kink itself. The faults continue southwest into the Yatsushiro Sea. There the quakes are too few to make a b-value map at all, so a different tool is needed. The authors calculated how much the 2026 slip pushed or pulled on those sea faults.

$$ \Delta\text{CFS} = \Delta\tau + \mu' \,\Delta\sigma_n $$

This is the change in Coulomb failure stress on a fault that might slip next, called a receiver fault. $\Delta\tau$ is how much shear stress changed in the direction that receiver would slip. If it is positive, it is pushing that fault toward slipping. $\Delta\sigma_n$ is how much normal stress changed, pulling the two sides apart. Pulling apart is positive for unclamping because an unclamped fault is easier to slip. $\mu'$ is the effective coefficient of friction, how much the unclamping matters. The paper used $\mu' = 0.4$, a typical value.

If $\Delta\text{CFS}$ is positive, that receiver is closer to failure. If negative, it is farther. The change was computed in an elastic half-space, a simple flexible Earth, with Poisson's ratio 0.25 and Young's modulus $8\times10^{10}$ Pa, which gives a shear modulus $3.2\times10^{10}$ Pa, at a typical depth of 10 km for the seismicity along the Hinagu fault zone, using the program Coulomb 3.4.

Two source models were used for the 2026 quake. The GSI model has two planes F1 and F2 with a maximum slip about 2.0 m. The NIED model has one plane, strike 211°, dip 76°, rake −170°, maximum slip 5.2 m, moment magnitude $M_w$ 6.8. The receivers were given the same orientations: GSI-F2 type 225/44/−156 and NIED type 211/76/−170, plus their conjugates 117/74/−49 and 119/80/−14. That makes four cases to check.

At the low-b bend itself, the numbers are not trusted. With the GSI source it is within ±0.5 bar, with the NIED source below −5 bar, because that plane ends only about 3 km from the bend with more than 1.2 m of slip at its edge. Changes calculated on and very near the faults that slipped are not reliable, so the paper does not interpret them.

On the sea faults the story is clear for all four cases. The change is positive everywhere. With the GSI source on GSI-F2 receivers it is 0.6 to 0.9 bar in the northeastern 10 km nearest the 2026 tip and falls to 0.1 to 0.3 bar 30 km to the southwest. With the NIED source on NIED receivers the maximum is 1.0 bar in the northeast and the same 0.1 to 0.3 bar in the southwest. On the conjugate planes the maxima are 0.9 bar for the GSI-F2 type and 1.7 bar for the NIED type, falling to 0.2 to 0.6 bar in the southwest. In plain words, the 2026 rupture brought the northeastern part of the Yatsushiro Sea faults, the part closest to the tip, measurably closer to failure.

Those sea faults are the part of the Hinagu fault zone to which the Earthquake Research Committee had already given the highest 30-year probability, up to 16%, on geological evidence alone. A future rupture starting at the −20 km low-b bend could go southwest into the sea rather than northeast where the large 2026 slip already happened. The paper does not predict when. The b-value tells you place, not time. The 2019 zone waited seven years for its quake, and nothing in b says when the next one will come, or whether the zone might persist without one. What the paper calls for is monitoring: keep following the b-value at the bend and seismicity along the sea faults and communicate it before, not after.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Gutenberg-Richter law

The straight-line rule log10 N = a - bM. N is the number of quakes of magnitude M or larger. a measures how active a region is, b is the slope.

b-value

The slope b in the Gutenberg-Richter law. Low b (about 0.6-0.7) means relatively more large quakes; high b (about 1.0-1.2) means mostly small quakes. In lab and Earth, b falls as differential stress rises, so it works as a stress meter.

Completeness magnitude Mc

The smallest magnitude that a network reliably records. Above Mc the catalog follows the Gutenberg-Richter law; below it earthquakes are missed. The paper estimates Mc together with b using the EMR method.

Epicentre vs hypocentre

Map place where the surface location (epicentre) differs from the true start point underground. Hypocentre is the 3D point at depth (2026 M7.1 at 16 km); epicentre is its projection on the map.

Fault bend

A kink where a fault's strike and dip change. In the GSI model F1 strikes 210° dips 61°, F2 strikes 225° dips 44°. Bends are known to start and stop ruptures and keep low b-values because slip on straight parts leaves stress unreleased at the bend.

Omori-Utsu p-value

How fast aftershocks decay: rate λ(t)=k(c+t)^-p, t is time after the mainshock. p≈1 means stress is constant/increasing, p>1 rapidly falling, p<1 slowly falling. Central Hinagu had p≈1 while ruptured areas had p>1.

Coulomb stress change ΔCFS

Change in Coulomb failure stress on a receiver fault: ΔCFS = Δτ + μ'Δσn. Δτ is shear stress change in slip direction, Δσn is normal stress change (positive for unclamping), μ' is effective friction (0.4 in paper). Positive ΔCFS brings a fault closer to failure.

Afterslip

Slow slip after an earthquake without shaking, plus viscoelastic relaxation of lower crust and mantle. After 2016 Pollitz model shows afterslip on Futagawa and northern Hinagu but stopped at central Hinagu, leaving its stress constant/increasing.

EMR method

The whole-magnitude-range method that fits the observed magnitude distribution with a Gutenberg-Richter law above Mc and a detection curve below it to find Mc and b together, with 0.1-magnitude bin correction and bootstrap uncertainties (1,000 resamples).
