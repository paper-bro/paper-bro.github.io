001The Zoo Problem~8 min

Why a Dozen Different Neural Nets Are Really One Idea

Your phone uses a different kind of neural network for almost everything. One spots faces in photos. Another predicts how a molecule will behave. Another picks the fastest driving route. On the surface they look like completely different animals — researchers even call it the “zoo” of architectures.

The paper behind this course says the zoo is an illusion. All those networks are doing the same job: learning a signal that lives on a shape, while respecting what that shape allows.

🖼️

Grid

Photos live on a flat grid of pixels. Slide the picture left or right — it’s still the same cat. Translation shouldn’t change the answer.

🕸️

Graph

Social networks or molecules live on graphs: dots (people, atoms) connected by links. Renumber the dots — the friendship or chemistry stays the same.

🌍

Curved surface

Weather or 3D scans live on a sphere or bumpy surface. Rotate the sphere — the pattern is the same, just turned.

This insight is old. In 1872 Felix Klein proposed his Erlangen Program: a geometry is defined by what stays the same when you transform it. Geometric Deep Learning borrows that idea: pick the domain and its symmetry group, and you have picked the geometry — and the right neural network.

Why symmetries tame learning: Without any rules, the set of possible models — the “hypothesis class” — is astronomically huge. Imagine every way you could color 4 spots with 2 colors: $2^4 = 16$ rules. Demand “translation doesn’t matter” and all 4 spots must share a color: only $2^1 = 2$ rules remain. That shrinking is called inductive bias. It’s a good bias: you throw away the nonsense answers before training even starts, so you need far less data to find a good one.

The paper argues this is how we beat the curse of dimensionality. High-dimensional data looks hopeless — a tiny $100 \times 100$ color image already lives in $\mathbb{R}^{30{,}000}$. But real data isn’t scattered everywhere in that huge space. It hugs a much smaller, highly symmetric world. A CNN assumes “shifts don’t matter.” A Graph Neural Network assumes “renaming nodes doesn’t matter.” A spherical network assumes “rotations don’t matter.” Different symmetry, same trick.

In the lab below, try that trick yourself. Choose a domain, turn on its natural symmetry, and watch how a giant pile of possible models collapses down to a small, structured pile — the exact pile each “different” architecture is searching in. Notice which symmetry actually fits which domain.

002Collections & Chains~10 min

From Dots on Paper to Deep Chains That Learn

Think of a set as a bag of dots. No repeats inside — {1,2,3} is the same as {3,2,1} — and order doesn't matter. That's it. This boring-sounding idea is where math actually starts, before numbers get fancy rules.

Some famous bags: the empty bag {} written ∅ with size (cardinality) |∅|=0; ℕ = {1,2,3,…} the naturals; ℤ = {…, -2,-1,0,1,2,…} the integers; ℚ the fractions like p/q; ℝ all real numbers including π and √2; and ℂ the complex numbers a+bi. |A| just counts how many distinct dots are inside — for example |{1,2,3}|=3.

A map is a rule that takes every dot from one bag to a dot in another bag. We write F: A → B. A is the domain (where you start), B is the codomain (where you can land). For each input a in A, F(a) is defined and lives in B.

A function is just a special map whose output is a number. Maps can be picky in three useful ways: injective means different inputs never land on the same output (no collisions); surjective means every possible output gets hit at least once (no gaps); bijective means both — a perfect pairing, so you can undo it with an inverse F⁻¹.

The big move in deep learning is composition — chaining maps. If f: X → Y and g: Y → Z, their composition is

$$ (g \circ f)(x) = g(f(x)) $$

in plain words: do f first, then do g to the result. LeNet-5, the early network for reading handwritten digits, is a chain like this: image → edges → parts → digit. Each box transforms feature maps and hands them to the next box. Deep just means a long chain.

Why does this matter? It defines what a network could learn. For any input space 𝒳 and output space 𝒴, the hypothesis class is the set of functions the architecture could possibly become:

$$ \mathcal{F} \subset \{ f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y} \} $$

The simplest class is linear (really affine) regression:

$$ \mathcal{F}_{\text{lin}} = \{ f_{w,b}(x)= w^{\top}x + b \;|\; w \in \mathbb{R}^{d},\; b \in \mathbb{R} \} $$

that's just tilted flat sheets — not very flexible.

A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) grows the class by composing. In a simplified version, with L layers:

$$ f_{\theta}(x) = \sigma_{L}\big(W^{(L)}(\cdots \sigma_{2}(W^{(2)}(\sigma_{1}(W^{(1)}x+b^{(1)}))+b^{(2)})\cdots)+b^{(L)}\big) $$

Each W^{(l)}x+b^{(l)} is a stretch-and-shift (affine), and each σ is a non-linear bend like ReLU (cut off negatives) or tanh (smooth S-curve). Without σ, even a 10-layer chain is still just a flat line. With σ, the chain can kink and wiggle into rich shapes.

The paper's point: choosing an architecture is choosing a hypothesis class — deciding what bag of functions to search in. Geometric tricks later will shrink that bag intelligently.

003Symmetry~12 min

How to Turn a Rotation Into a Rule for AI

Imagine you have to teach a computer to recognize a square. Turn the square 90°, flip it — it’s still a square. A good rule for AI should know that. The paper’s big idea is to turn that intuition into math you can program: describe the allowed moves as a group, then demand your neural network respects it.

A group is just a tidy set of moves with four guarantees. Take the rotations of a square:

$$C_4 = \{0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ\}$$

1) Closure: combine any two moves and you stay in the set — 90° then 180° = 270°, which is still in $C_4$. 2) Associativity: $(a\circ b)\circ c = a\circ(b\circ c)$ — grouping doesn’t matter. 3) Identity: there’s a do-nothing move $e$, here $0^\circ$, with $e\circ a = a$. 4) Inverse: every move can be undone, $a^{-1}$, like $90^\circ$ undone by $270^\circ$. $C_4$’s Cayley graph is just a map of how $R$=rotate and $F$=reflect connect these moves.

Different-looking groups can be the same structure in disguise. The paper points out $C_4$ is essentially the same as integers modulo 4:

$$\mathbb{Z}_4 = \{0,1,2,3\}$$

with addition mod 4. The map $\phi(0^\circ)=0, \phi(90^\circ)=1, \phi(180^\circ)=2, \phi(270^\circ)=3$ preserves combining: $\phi(a\circ b)=\phi(a)\ast \phi(b)$. Any map that does this is a homomorphism (“same shape”); if it’s also bijective (one-to-one and onto), it’s an isomorphism, written $C_4 \cong \mathbb{Z}_4$. We can swap one view for the other without losing anything.

A group by itself does nothing until it acts on data. A (left) group action is a rule

$$\alpha: G \times X \to X,\quad (g,x) \mapsto g\cdot x$$

where $X$ is your data — say, all images. It must obey $e\cdot x = x$ (doing nothing changes nothing) and $(g\circ a)\cdot x = g\cdot(a\cdot x)$ (doing $a$ then $g$ together equals doing them one after another). For $C_4$ on images, $90^\circ \cdot x$ is just “the Mona Lisa rotated 90°.”

From there, collect everything you can reach from one example:

$$\text{Orb}(x) = \{ g\cdot x \mid g\in G \}$$

This is the orbit — the four rotated Mona Lisas for $C_4$. Now we can write the two AI rules. A function $f:X\to Y$ is $G$-invariant if it collapses the orbit:

$$f(g\cdot x)=f(x)\quad \forall g\in G, x\in X$$

— a face-recognizer that returns the same name for all four rotations. It is $G$-equivariant if it moves with the action:

$$f(g\cdot_X x)=g\cdot_Y f(x)$$

— a box-detector whose output square also rotates 90° when the input does. The symbols $\cdot_X$ and $\cdot_Y$ just remind us the same group may act differently on inputs and outputs.

Key trick: instead of hoping the network learns this, we build it in. A simple $G$-average over the orbit makes any function invariant; a group convolution builds equivariance directly:
$$(f\star \psi)(x)=\sum_{g\in G} f(g\cdot x)\,\psi(g^{-1})$$
Here $\psi(g)$ is a learnable filter weight for each group element $g$, and $g^{-1}$ is the undo-move. Summing over all $g$ guarantees the result shifts predictably with $g$ — the paper proves $(f\star\psi)$ is $G$-equivariant.

Stack several equivariant layers to capture patterns, then add one final invariant pooling — that’s how Geometric Deep Learning gets both expressive features and robust answers without needing extra data.

In the lab below, try it yourself: pick a square or triangle, apply rotations and flips, and toggle between invariant and equivariant. Watch the orbit gallery of $4$ (or $3$) images — does your function give one value for the whole orbit, or does its output turn with the input?

004Measuring~10 min

How AI Judges Near and Far

Your phone decides two photos are "close" and files them together. An AI decides two molecules are "similar" and predicts they behave alike. But what does close even mean to a computer? It needs a ruler — and it can choose many different rulers.

This lesson is about three rulers that build on each other: size of one thing, distance between two things, and angle between directions.

How big is one vector?

A norm, written $\|u\|$, is any size-measuring function that behaves like physical length. It must satisfy three rules for any vectors $u,v$ and number $\alpha$:

$$ |\alpha|\cdot\|u\| = \|\alpha u\| \quad\text{(stretching scales size)}, $$ $$ \|u+v\| \le \|u\|+\|v\| \quad\text{(triangle inequality: detour is never shorter)}, $$ $$ \|u\|=0 \;\Rightarrow\; u=0 \quad\text{(only the zero vector has zero size).} $$

The paper focuses on the $L_p$ family. For a vector $u=(u_1,\dots,u_n)$:

$$ \|u\|_p = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} |u_i|^p\right)^{1/p} $$

That tiny $p$ changes everything. $p=1$ adds absolute values: $\|u\|_1 = \sum |u_i|$ (Manhattan distance). $p=2$ is the familiar Euclidean length $\|u\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum u_i^2}$. $p=\infty$ just takes the biggest entry $\|u\|_\infty = \max |u_i|$. The same idea works for functions, with the sum becoming an integral: $\|f\|_p = (\int |f(x)|^p dx)^{1/p}$.

How far apart are two vectors?

Once you can measure size, distance is easy: distance is the size of the difference.

$$ d(u,v) = \|u-v\| $$

Every norm gives a metric (distance function) that is non-negative, symmetric $d(u,v)=d(v,u)$, zero only when $u=v$, and obeys the triangle inequality $d(u,w)\le d(u,v)+d(v,w)$. In Geometric Deep Learning this is how we decide if two embeddings, atoms, or nodes are neighbors — for example, connecting atoms whose Euclidean distance $d_2$ is below a threshold to build a geometric graph.

The paper also mentions a distance between whole sets: the Hausdorff distance. Imagine two cloud shapes $A$ and $B$. For each point in $A$ find its closest point in $B$, take the worst one; do the same $B$ to $A$, and take the larger. If that number is small, the shapes almost coincide.

Which way are they pointing?

Norms tell size and distance, but not direction. For that we need an inner product, written $\langle u,v\rangle$. For real vectors it's simply

$$ \langle u,v\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i = u_i v_i \;\text{(sum over }i\text{)} $$

It induces a norm by $\|u\| = \sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}$, and comes with two famous consequences:

$$ |\langle u,v\rangle| \le \|u\|\cdot\|v\| \quad\text{(Cauchy-Schwarz: the dot can't beat the product of lengths)} $$ $$ \cos\angle(u,v) = \frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\cdot\|v\|}, \qquad \langle u,v\rangle=0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; u\perp v \;\text{(right angle)} $$

That cosine is why AI can compare meaning, not just size: two word or image embeddings can be long or short but still point the same way. When vectors are orthogonal, the paper's Pythagorean theorem says $\|\sum v_i\|^2 = \sum \|v_i\|^2$ — exactly like right triangles.

Not every size comes from an angle. A norm comes from an inner product only if it passes the parallelogram law: $2\|u\|^2+2\|v\|^2 = \|u+v\|^2+\|u-v\|^2$. You can test this — $L_2$ passes, $L_1$ fails. So with $L_1$ you lose notions like orthogonality and angle, which is why smooth optimization and attention prefer inner products.

In short: norm = how long, metric = how far, inner product = which direction. The lab lets you feel the difference — morph the unit ball from a diamond ($L_1$) to a circle ($L_2$) to a square ($L_\infty$), and drag two vectors to see distances and cosine change live. Watch for when cosine hits zero.

005Calculus~12 min

The Tiny Slopes Where Learning Happens

Imagine a hilly landscape made of foggy bumps. Height is error — how wrong your model is. Valleys are good models, peaks are terrible ones. Learning means walking downhill in the fog, and you can only feel the slope right under your feet.

The paper makes this picture precise. A scalar field like loss is a rule $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ that gives one number for each point in space. A vector field like a velocity map gives a little arrow at each point, $F: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. In deep learning a signal is the same idea on any domain $\Omega$, written $F:\Omega \to \mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}^m$, where $m$ is just the number of channels.

To walk, you need local slope. The partial derivative $\partial f / \partial x_i$ asks: if I nudge only $x_i$ a tiny $\epsilon$, how fast does $f$ change? That limit is

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{f(x_1,\dots,x_i+\epsilon,\dots,x_n)-f(x)}{\epsilon} $$

A directional derivative does the same in any direction $d$. Pack all partials together and you get the gradient

$$ \nabla f(x) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right) $$

which gives a first-order prediction: a tiny step $dx$ changes $f$ by $\langle \nabla f(x), dx\rangle$, so $f(x+dx)\approx f(x)+\langle \nabla f(x),dx\rangle$. For a vector field, the same table of partials is the Jacobian $(J_F)_{ij}= \partial F_i/\partial x_j$ — it tells how space is stretched locally.

The paper adds two global tools. Divergence $\mathrm{div}\,F = \sum_i \partial F_i/\partial x_i$ measures outward flow from a tiny volume. The divergence theorem $\int_{\Omega} \mathrm{div}\,F\,dV = \int_{\partial \Omega}\langle F,\hat n\rangle\,dS$ says total outflow inside equals flow through the boundary — conservation in math form. The Laplacian $\Delta f = \mathrm{div}\,\nabla f$ measures how $f$ bulges at a point. The paper proves $\Delta$ is rotation-invariant because it is the trace of the Hessian, $\Delta f = \mathrm{tr}(\nabla^2 f)$, and orthogonal changes leave the trace unchanged.

Learning uses exactly this. Your loss function $\mathcal{L}(w)$ is a scalar field over weights $w$. Gradient descent steps opposite the uphill direction:

$$ w_{t+1}=w_t - \eta \,\nabla \mathcal{L}(w_t) $$

Here $\eta$ is the learning rate, a single number for step size. Small $\eta$ = careful, stable. Large $\eta$ = fast but you can overshoot and even diverge. The paper emphasizes Lipschitz control, $ |f(x)-f(y)| \le L|x-y| $: slope never exceeds $L$, which keeps steps bounded. Smoothness classes $\mathcal{C}^0, \mathcal{C}^1, \dots, \mathcal{C}^\infty$ just count how many continuous derivatives you have.

Deep nets are chains $x \mapsto f_1(x) \mapsto f_2(f_1(x))\dots$. The chain rule stitches their Jacobians: $d/dx\,(g\circ f)=J_g(f(x))\cdot J_f(x)$. Applied backward through layers, that product is backpropagation — each layer’s $\nabla_{w^{(l)}}\mathcal{L}$ is a product of Jacobians from the top down.

In the lab, click to drop yourself somewhere on a loss terrain. Press Step or Play and watch $w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\nabla L$ in action. Try a tiny $\eta$ versus a huge one from the same start and see why step size trades speed for stability — and how the chain of local slopes guides you to a valley.

006Curved Worlds~12 min

Donuts, Spheres, and the Shape of Data

Up to now our data lived on flat grids. But a photo of a globe, a brain scan, or the set of all valid face-images is curved. To work there we need a way to talk about shape without measuring angles first — that's topology — and then a way to add measuring back on curved surfaces.

Topology starts with open sets. In ordinary space an open ball is all points within radius $r$ of $x$:

$$B(x,r)=\{y \mid d(x,y) < r\}$$

Think of a soap bubble without its skin. A set $U$ is open if every point in $U$ sits inside some tiny ball still entirely in $U$ — no point is on the edge.

Collect all open sets into a family $\mathcal{T}$. The paper calls $(X,\mathcal{T})$ a topological space when it obeys three simple rules: the empty set $\emptyset$ and whole space $X$ are in $\mathcal{T}$; any union of open sets is open (you can glue many bubbles); any finite intersection of open sets is open. That's it — arbitrary unions, finite intersections. This minimal rulebook lets us define closeness without a ruler.

A map $F:X\to Y$ is continuous if the preimage of every open set is open: $F^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$ whenever $U$ is open in $Y$. No tearing allowed — nearby inputs stay nearby.

Two spaces look the same to a topologist if you can stretch one into the other without cutting or gluing. That special map is a homeomorphism: a bijection (one-to-one and onto) that is continuous both ways. A cube surface and a sphere are homeomorphic — inflate the cube's faces and round its edges. Both have Euler characteristic $2$, a number that never changes under homeomorphisms.

Homotopy is looser. You may collapse, squash, or shrink parts. The paper shows a circle shrinking to a point: every circle point goes to the same point. That map is onto but not one-to-one — many to one — so it can never be a homeomorphism. It preserves fewer invariants (circle has Euler $0$, point has $1$).

A manifold is the paper's way to say locally flat. Near any point $p$ on the manifold $\mathcal{M}$ there is a small patch $U$ and a homeomorphism

$$\varphi: U \to \mathbb{R}^n$$

called a chart, like a paper map of a tiny neighborhood of Earth. An atlas is a collection of charts covering all of $\mathcal{M}$ whose overlap transitions $\psi_{\alpha\beta}=\varphi_{\beta}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}$ are smooth. If those transitions are smooth, we have a smooth manifold; add a smoothly varying inner product $g_p$ on each tangent space $T_p\mathcal{M}$ and we get a Riemannian manifold where we can finally measure lengths and angles on the curved surface.

🧭

Tangent bundle

$T\mathcal{M}=\bigsqcup_{p\in\mathcal{M}} T_p\mathcal{M}$ is all tangent planes bundled together. A vector field $V(p)\in T_p\mathcal{M}$ picks one wind-arrow at every point.

📏

Geodesic & exp

The geodesic is the shortest path staying on the surface. $\exp_p(v)$ walks from $p$ along that geodesic in direction $v$ for distance $\|v\|$. $\log_p$ walks back.

The paper also flags gauges: a choice of coordinates $\omega_p:\mathbb{R}^n\to T_p\mathcal{M}$ in each tangent plane. Different gauges are related by $\omega'_p = \omega_p\circ\tau$. A good convolution must be gauge-equivariant, $\psi(\tau(v))=\tau(\psi(v))$, so the answer doesn't depend on which north you picked.

Finally, the manifold hypothesis: all your $256^{H\times W}$ possible images form a huge cube, but real faces lie near a much lower-dimensional curved sheet inside it. Walk along the sheet and faces morph sensibly; cut straight through the ambient cube and you get ghostly noise. That's why learning can work in seemingly impossible dimensions.

007Vibrations~12 min

Finding the Natural Frequencies of Anything

Any shape has favorite ways to vibrate — like a guitar string that loves to wiggle in one big arch, then two smaller arches, then three. Those favorite patterns are the shape's natural frequencies. If you can find them, you can describe almost any pattern on that shape as a simple recipe: "a bit of tone 1 plus a bit of tone 2…"

This is the heart of spectral theory. It works for a string, a sphere, a city map, or a social network — because all of them can be studied with the same math.

From wobbly sequences to solid spaces

Imagine writing down a list of numbers that gets closer and closer together, like 0.3, 0.33, 0.333, … We call that a Cauchy sequence when for any tiny tolerance ε, all late entries stay within ε of each other: ||v_m - v_n|| < ε. Intuitively, the sequence is trying to converge.

But does the limit actually exist inside our world? The rational numbers ℚ fail: a Cauchy sequence of fractions can try to converge to √2, which is not a fraction. A space where every Cauchy sequence does have its limit inside is called complete.

A Banach space is a complete normed space. A Hilbert space is a complete space with an inner product ⟨u,v⟩ — so you can also measure angles. The space L² of square-integrable functions (∫|f|² < ∞) is the Hilbert space we use for signals.

Here's the useful picture the paper gives: a function in L² is like an infinite-dimensional vector. Pick an orthonormal basis of building-block functions φ₁, φ₂, …. Then any function f is just its list of coordinates:

$$ f \equiv [f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots], \quad f_k = \langle f, \phi_k \rangle $$

Each f_k is an inner product — a shadow projection of f onto that basis direction — exactly like finding x and y coordinates of an arrow.

Natural frequencies are eigenvectors

An operator A is just a rule that transforms one vector (or function) into another. A special vector that only gets stretched, not rotated, is an eigenfunction:

$$ A v = \lambda v $$

v is the pattern, λ (lambda) is the stretch factor — its eigenvalue. For matrices you know these as eigenvectors; for functions we say eigenfunctions, same idea.

When A is self-adjoint — meaning ⟨Au,v⟩ = ⟨u,Av⟩ for all u,v, the infinite-dimensional version of a symmetric matrix — two nice things happen:

  • all λ are real numbers, and
  • eigenfunctions for different λ are orthogonal (⟨v,w⟩=0), so they point at right angles.
Spectral Theorem (simplified): a compact self-adjoint operator has countably many real eigenvalues λ₁, λ₂, … and its orthonormal eigenfunctions form a basis. Any vector can be built from them.

That is why natural frequencies matter: they give you the perfect coordinate system for that operator. The classic example in the paper is the Laplacian Δ = d²/dx² on a circle [-π,π]. Its eigenfunctions are e^{inx} with eigenvalues -n² — pure tones. For a general operator that is not self-adjoint we use singular values instead:

$$ A w = \sum_n \sigma_n \, \langle v_n, w \rangle \, u_n $$

with left and right directions u_n, v_n and stretch amounts σ_n ≥ 0. For matrices this is the SVD you may have heard of: A = U Σ V*.

Fourier is just a change of basis

Once you have an orthonormal basis {v_α}, any u can be written as

$$ u = \sum_{\alpha} \langle u, v_{\alpha} \rangle \, v_{\alpha} \quad\text{and}\quad \hat{u}_{\alpha}=\langle u, v_{\alpha}\rangle \text{ are Fourier coefficients.} $$

For periodic functions that is the familiar Fourier series: f(x)= Σ ˆf_n e^{inx}. The paper stresses this is nothing mysterious — it is the same as writing u = V(V†u) for vectors.

And it preserves geometry. Parseval's identity says:

$$ \langle u,w\rangle = \sum_{\alpha} \hat{u}_{\alpha}\,\overline{\hat{w}_{\alpha}},\quad \|u\|^2 = \sum_{\alpha} |\hat{u}_{\alpha}|^2 $$

In words: distance and energy measured in the original world exactly equals distance and energy summed over frequencies. The Fourier map is an isometry — no information lost.

Heat shows why low frequencies survive

The heat equation Δf = f_t asks: if temperature f(x,t) starts as g(x) on a circle, how does it even out? Expanding g in the Laplacian's eigenfunctions e^{inx} gives a beautifully simple answer:

$$ f(x,t)=\sum_n a_n \,e^{inx}\,e^{-n^2 t} = g \star h_t $$

Each frequency n just shrinks by e^{-n²t}. High n (fast buzz) dies exponentially faster than low n (slow wobble). The result is a convolution with the heat kernel h_t — a low-pass filter that blurs away detail but keeps the overall energy accounted for by Parseval.

This is exactly how Geometric Deep Learning thinks about graphs and manifolds later: the eigenvectors of their Laplacian are their "natural frequencies," Fourier is projection onto them, and smoothing is damping high frequencies.

Try it below: build any wiggly signal from 5 pure tones, then turn up heat time t and watch the spectrum collapse — high bars vanish first, while total energy matches whether you add it in space or in frequency.

008Networks~14 min

The Math That Lets AI Talk on Graphs

Your Instagram friends, the atoms in a painkiller, and the roads in your city look nothing alike — but a single math trick lets the same kind of AI learn on all of them. That trick is to think of everything as a graph.

A graph is just an ordered pair $G=(V,E)$. $V$ is the set of nodes — people, atoms, intersections — and $E$ is the set of edges — friendships, bonds, streets. We write $(v_i,v_j)\in E$ when there is an edge from node $v_i$ to $v_j$. If the edge works both ways we call the graph undirected; if it has a direction, it is a digraph. The neighbourhood $\mathcal{N}(v_i)=\{v_j\mid (v_i,v_j)\in E\}$ is everyone directly linked to $v_i$.

Computers store this wiring in matrices. The adjacency matrix $A$ has entry

$$A_{ij}=\begin{cases}1 & (v_i,v_j)\in E\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

and the degree matrix $D$ is diagonal with $D_{ii}=\sum_j A_{ij}$, the number of edges at $v_i$. That number is the degree centrality $\text{deg}(v_i)$. From here we get distances: the shortest path $d_G(v_i,v_j)=\min_{P}\sum_{e_k\in P} w(e_k)$ is the cheapest route adding edge weights $w(e_k)$, and the diameter $\text{diam}(G)=\max_{i,j} d_G(v_i,v_j)$ is the longest shortest route.

⚪

Point cloud $N_N$

No edges at all: $E=\emptyset$. Like isolated LiDAR points before you connect them.

🕸

Complete $K_N$

Every pair connected. One step from anyone to anyone: $d_G=1$.

🧢

Geometric graph

Nodes have positions in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$. Connect if $d(v_i,v_j)\le \epsilon$ (unit-disk) or link each node to its $k$ nearest neighbours (k-NN, $k$-regular).

Other useful shapes: bipartite graphs split $V=V_1\cup V_2$ with edges only across the split (users ↔ products), regular graphs where every node has degree $k$, and trees with no cycles. Real graphs often show homophily — friends tend to share the label — measured by $h=\frac{1}{|E|}\sum_{(v_i,v_j)\in E} \mathbf{1}(y_i=y_j)$, the fraction of edges inside the same class.

Numbering nodes $0,1,2...$ is arbitrary. A valid graph model must not care. That is why we need functions that are permutation-invariant: $\bigoplus(x_1,\dots,x_N)=\bigoplus(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(N)})$ for any reordering $\sigma$ in the symmetric group $S_N$. Sum, mean and max are examples. In matrix language a reordering is a permutation matrix $P$ with one $1$ per row and column, and the rewired graph is $A' = P A P^{\top}$. Its eigenvalues, degrees and connectivity stay the same — only labels move.

So far a graph is just wiring. Learning needs numbers on the nodes. We put a feature field $F:V\to\mathbb{R}^D$ with $F(v_i)=x_i$, stacked into a matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ whose $i$-th row is $x_i^{\top}$. Think colours, atom types, or user embeddings.

How bumpy is that field? The paper builds the graph Laplacian $L = D-A$ and its normalized cousin $L_{\text{norm}} = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}$. Its quadratic form is the Dirichlet energy

$$x^{\top}Lx = \tfrac12\sum_{(v_i,v_j)\in E} w_{ij}\,(x_i-x_j)^2$$

When neighbours disagree a lot, energy is high; when the signal is smooth, it is low — exactly like $\langle \nabla f,\nabla f\rangle$ on a curved surface. Because $L$ is symmetric, it has an eigen-decomposition $L = U\Lambda U^{\top}$ with orthonormal $U=[u_1,\dots,u_N]$ and eigenvalues $\Lambda=\text{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N)$. That gives a graph Fourier transform $\hat f = U^{\top}f$ and back $f = U\hat f$: smooth basis vectors have small $\lambda$, wiggly ones have large $\lambda$.

Finally, how does information move? A Graph Neural Network layer does three things locally:

$$x_i^{(l+1)} = \phi\Bigl(x_i^{(l)},\, \bigoplus_{j\in\mathcal{N}(v_i)} \psi(x_i^{(l)},x_j^{(l)})\Bigr)$$

$\psi$ builds a message $m_{ij}$ from $i$ and $j$, $\bigoplus$ aggregates all messages in the neighbourhood with a permutation-invariant sum/mean/max, and $\phi$ updates the centre node. Isomorphism needs a bijection preserving edges both ways; the Weisfeiler–Lehman test repeatedly recolours nodes by neighbourhood to check it. Stack a few such layers and you have a GNN that works on molecules, social nets, and meshes with the same code.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Set

A collection of distinct objects. Order doesn't matter and repeats aren't allowed. Example: {1,2,3}.

Map / Function

A rule that assigns to each element of a set A an element of a set B, written F: A → B. A function is a map whose output is a number.

Composition

Chaining two maps: (g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)). Deep networks are long chains of simple maps.

Hypothesis class

The set of all functions an architecture could possibly learn, F ⊂ {f: X → Y}. Choosing an architecture means choosing this set.

Group

A set G with a way to combine elements (a ∘ b) that satisfies associativity, has an identity e, and every element has an inverse. Used to model symmetries like rotations.

Group action

How a group moves points of a set: α: G × X → X, (g,x) ↦ g·x, with e·x=x and (g∘a)·x = g·(a·x).

Orbit

All points you can reach from x using the group: Orb(x) = { g·x | g in G }. Example: the 4 rotated copies of an image.

Invariant / Equivariant

f is invariant if f(g·x)=f(x) — output doesn't change. Equivariant if f(g·x)=g·f(x) — output transforms predictably.

Vector space

A vector space needs only two abilities: you can add vectors and stretch them by numbers (scalars), with sensible rules.

Tensor

A multi-dimensional array. Scalar=rank 0, vector=rank 1, matrix=rank 2. In vision we use [B,C,H,W] tensors.

Norm

A size measure ||u|| on vectors with: ||αu||=|α|·||u||, ||u+v||≤||u||+||v||, and ||u||=0 only for u=0.

Metric

Distance between points. From a norm: d(u,v)=||u−v||. A metric satisfies non-negativity, symmetry, and triangle inequality.

Inner product

⟨u,v⟩ — a product returning a number that yields length ||u||=√⟨u,u⟩, angle cos∠=⟨u,v⟩/(||u||·||v||), and notion of orthogonal (⟨u,v⟩=0).

Gradient

Derivative in all directions. ∇f=(∂f/∂x₁,…,∂f/∂xₙ). Gives linear approximation f(x+dx)≈f(x)+⟨∇f,dx⟩.

Jacobian

Matrix of all first partial derivatives of a vector field F: (J_F)ᵢⱼ=∂Fᵢ/∂xⱼ. Tells how space is stretched locally.

Laplacian

For scalar field f: Δf = div∇f. Measures how much f bulges at a point. Rotation-invariant.

Gradient descent

w_{t+1}=w_t − η∇L(w_t). Follow the steepest downhill direction to lower loss L. η is learning rate.

Backpropagation

Applying chain rule backwards through layers to compute ∇L efficiently. Underlies training.

Open set

A set U where every point has a small ball B(x,r)={y|d(x,y)<r} still inside U. No boundary points included.

Topological space

A set X with a collection T of open sets containing ∅ and X, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections.

Homeomorphism

Spaces that can be continuously deformed into each other with a continuous inverse. Cube and sphere are homeomorphic.

Homotopy equivalence

Weaker equivalence: one space can be continuously shrunk/collapsed onto the other. Circle can collapse to a point (not injective). Preserves fewer invariants.

Manifold

Space that locally looks like Rⁿ. Described by charts φ:U→Rⁿ and an atlas where overlaps have smooth transition maps.

Tangent space

At point p, the flat vector space of all directions you can move: T_pM. The bundle TM is all tangent spaces together.

Riemannian manifold

Smooth manifold with smoothly varying inner product g_p on each T_pM. Lets you measure lengths and angles on the curved space.

Geodesic

Locally shortest path staying on the surface. Not a straight line in ambient space.

Exponential map

exp_p(v) — follow the geodesic from p in direction v for distance ||v|| to land on the manifold. log_p is its local inverse.

Manifold hypothesis

Idea that high-dimensional data (e.g., all 256^{H·W} images) actually cluster near a much lower-dimensional curved manifold of valid examples.

Banach / Hilbert space

Normed vector space where every Cauchy sequence (points getting arbitrarily close) has a limit inside the space. Hilbert space = complete inner-product space.

Eigenfunction / Eigenvalue

Non-zero vector with Av=λv. λ is eigenvalue — stretch factor that keeps direction (up to sign). For self-adjoint operators λ is real and different eigenfunctions are orthogonal.

Singular value

For general operator: Aw=Σ σ_n ⟨v_n,w⟩ u_n. σ_n≥0 are singular values. For matrices: A=UΣV*. Coincide with |λ| when self-adjoint.

Fourier coefficient

Coefficients in orthonormal basis expansion: u=Σ ⟨u,v_α⟩ v_α. ⟨u,v_α⟩ is Fourier transform. Works for vectors and functions.

Parseval's identity

Geometry preserved: ⟨u,w⟩=Σ Û_α overline(ŵ_α) and ||u||²=Σ|Û_α|². Lets us work with coefficients instead of original objects.

Graph

G=(V,E): nodes V plus edges E⊂V×V. Encoded by adjacency A (A_ij=1 if edge) and degree D_ii=Σ_j A_ij.

Graph Laplacian

L=D−A, normalized L_norm=I−D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}. Quadratic form xᵀLx=½Σ w_ij(x_i−x_j)² = Dirichlet energy = smoothness on graph.

Permutation-invariant

Aggregator ⊕ where ⊕(x₁,…,x_N)=⊕(x_{σ(1)},…,x_{σ(N)}) for any permutation σ. Examples: sum, mean, max. Keeps graph results independent of node numbering.

Message passing

Message passing layer: x_i^{l+1}=φ(x_i^l, ⊕_{j∈N(i)} ψ(x_i^l,x_j^l)). Learnable messages ψ, invariant aggregation ⊕, update φ.
