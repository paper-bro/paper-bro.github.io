001Lesson 1~6 min

Millions of Auctions You Never See

Every time you open a webpage, an auction you never see has just finished. In about 100 milliseconds, hundreds of advertisers decide what that single ad slot is worth to them, submit a bid, and the highest bid wins. This system, called real-time bidding or RTB, has run this way since around 2009.

Here's the catch. This is a second-price auction. The winner does not pay her own bid. She pays the market price — written as $z$ — which is the highest bid among all the competitors. Think of it like an eBay snipe: if you bid 80 cents and the next best bidder bid 67 cents, you win but you only pay 67 cents.

For one ad chance we know three things. $\bm{x}$ is the feature of the request: who the user is, what site and slot it is, what ad is being considered. $b$ is the price you offer. $z$ is that hidden highest competitor price. You must pick $b$ using only $\bm{x}$, without knowing $z$. $z$ is wildly jumpy from one impression to the next because you are guessing against hundreds or thousands of other bidders who do not talk to each other.

Key idea: you cannot usefully try to model every other bidder one by one. Instead, treat $z$ as a random variable for each request $\bm{x}$, and learn its whole distribution — written $p(z|\bm{x})$ in the paper — so you can answer "if I bid $b$, what is my chance to win?"

That chance is why the paper matters. Papers like Ren et al. 2018 and Lin et al. 2016 show that profit-maximizing bidding needs the winning probability for any bid you might try, not just a single price guess. If your win chance rises a lot from 30 to 40 cents but your cost does not, you might want to bid higher. If raising from 80 to 100 barely helps, you should save your budget. Without $p(z|\bm{x})$, you are bidding blind.

The next lessons will build that distribution step by step, show why most of the data hides $z$ from you, and how a deep model can learn from both wins and losses without assuming a neat formula.

Try it now: use the auction game below. Move your bid $b$ and run 500 hidden auctions. Watch the green wins versus red losses form around your black bid line, see what you actually pay (always $z$, never $b$), and notice the trade-off between bidding too low and burning budget by bidding too high.

From a Price Distribution to a Winning Chance

Imagine you could rewind the same ad auction 1,000 times and record what the highest competitor bid each time. You would get a pile of numbers — 12, 45, 47, 120... That pile has a shape. Some prices show up a lot, some rarely. That shape is the market-price distribution, written p(z|x).

Here z is the market price (the price you must beat to win), x is what you know about this request — user, website, ad slot — and p(z|x) is the chance the market price equals each possible value. The paper builds a function T that learns to predict that whole shape for every single request:

$$p(z|\mathbf{x}) = T(\mathbf{x})$$

But advertisers don't just want a histogram. They want one number: if I bid b, will I win? That's the winning function:

$$W(b|\mathbf{x}) = \text{Pr}(z < b | \mathbf{x}) = \int_{0}^{b} p(z|\mathbf{x}) \, dz$$

In plain words: add up all the chances for prices below your bid. If 70% of past market prices were below 50, then W(50)=0.70. The opposite is the losing function S(b)=1-W(b)=∫b∞ p(z)dz, the chance you still lose.

A peak in p(z) = a jump in W(b). Where many auctions cluster at the same price, your winning chance shoots up as soon as you pass that price. Flat parts of p(z) make W(b) rise slowly.

003Lesson 3~7 min

The Missing Price Problem

Imagine you bid $60 on a sneaker ad. If the hidden market price that day is $41, you win — and the auction tells you "you paid $41." You get a perfect record: (x, b=60, z=41). If the market price is $95, you lose — and the auction tells you nothing, just "you lost." Your record is (x, b=60, z=null). All you know is the true price was at least $60.

This is the difference between a winning log and a losing log. In the paper's shorthand, each auction gets a label $w$:

$$w = 1 \text{ if } b > z \text{ (win, }z\text{ known)} \quad\text{and}\quad w = 0 \text{ if } b \le z \text{ (lose, only know } z \ge b\text{)}$$

The wins are called uncensored — the price is fully seen. The losses are right-censored — the true value is hidden to the right of your bid. The paper borrows the language of medicine: think of the market price $z$ as how long a patient lives, your bid $b$ as how long you watch, winning as the event happening while you watched, and losing as the patient still alive when you stopped looking.

Why wins alone lie: Expensive auctions are harder to win, so your winning pile is mostly cheap auctions. If you average only wins, you will think the market is cheaper than it really is.

The numbers make this concrete. On iPinYou — 19.5 million impressions across 9 campaigns — the overall winning rate was only 0.2384 and the true average market price was 82.07. But split the logs: in $D_{win}$ the average seen price was just 25.05, while in $D_{lose}$ the hidden price averaged 99.92. YOYI is the same story on 402 million impressions: wins average 24.45, losses hide 87.48, winning rate 0.5035. Train on wins only and you learn from the cheap 25-dollar world and ignore the 100-dollar world.

Early survival-analysis fixes tried to count. The Kaplan-Meier estimator just counts wins and losses in segments, like "all sports-site auctions share one distribution." Cox models assume the chance of winning has a fixed mathematical shape multiplied by a tweak for each request. Both help with censorship, but they force coarse groups or rigid curves — exactly the limits Lesson 4 will test.

004Lesson 4~7 min

The Problem With One-Size-Fits-All Curves

Auction prices don't look tidy. One request might peak at 30, another at 150, and a third has two peaks at once. The paper shows real histograms that are spiky and multi-peaked — very different from a neat bell curve.

Older methods tried two shortcuts.

Shortcut 1: count by group. Take all auctions that look similar — same campaign or same ad-slot size — lump them together, and count. This is the Kaplan-Meier (KM) idea from medicine and its fancier cousin STM, a tree that clusters samples and then counts inside each leaf. It is simple and needs no shape guess, but everyone in the same cluster gets the exact same prediction. It is coarse-grained, not personalized to your single request.

Shortcut 2: assume a neat shape. Pick a famous formula — log-normal by Cui et al. 2011, Gaussian by Wu et al. 2015, Gamma by Zhu et al. 2017, or Weibull / log-logistic inside Cox models — and learn just its few knobs, like mean and variance. The paper notes these assumptions were rejected by statistical tests in Yuan et al. 2014: real data refuse to fit one bell. You get a smooth, one-hump curve that can never grow a second peak where the real data has one.

There is a third shortcut too: just predict one number, the expected market price. That sounds useful, but it gives no spread. You cannot answer "what if I bid 50? what if I bid 80?" because you need the full chance-to-win curve $W(b)$ for any $b$, not just an average.

Key insight: coarse counting is too blocky, fixed formulas are too rigid, and point estimates cannot give $W(b)$. The paper wants all three at once: fine-grained per-request, no shape assumed, and a full distribution.

DLF's promise is to drop the shape guessing entirely. Instead of writing down a formula for $p(z)$, it will build $p(z)$ step by step across prices directly from data — flexible enough for any wiggles, personalized for each $\bm{x}$, and able to give $W(b)$ for any bid $b$.

In the lab, try it yourself: pick a true wiggly market and try to match it with a Gaussian bell, a Gamma hump, or a coarse segment average. Watch the leftover green gap — that is the error you cannot fix without a flexible model. Then switch to "flexible" to see what dropping the assumption buys you.

005Lesson 5~9 min

How DLF Thinks in Steps, Not One Guess

Guessing one market price for an ad is like guessing one number for storminess — it rarely fits. The paper's trick is to stop guessing once. Instead, walk up the price ladder dollar by dollar and ask a tiny question at each step.

First, cut prices into little buckets. Because real bids are integers, the authors use intervals Vl = (bl, bl+1] with size 1. In the real data that means up to L = 300 steps, from $1 to $300. For each bucket we can talk about three things: the chance the true market price lands exactly in that bucket pl, the chance you are still losing if you bid there S(bl), and the chance you would already have won W(bl).

Here's the tiny question the model learns, called the conditional hazard:

$$h_{l}=\text{Pr}(z \in V_{l} \;|\; z \ge b_{l-1}) = \frac{p_{l}}{S(b_{l-1})}$$

In plain words: if the market price wasn't cheaper than your previous bid, what's the chance it is right here in this next dollar? Think of checking locked boxes in order. You only open box 6 if boxes 1-5 were empty. h6 is your chance to find the prize in box 6 given the first five were empty.

The conditional makes all the math click. If you know every hl, you can rebuild everything else with just multiplication — the probability chain rule.

Why multiplication? To still be losing after bidding b, you must have missed at every cheaper step. So you multiply the chance of missing each one:

$$S(b|\bm{x}) = \prod_{l \le l_{b}} (1-h_{l}) \qquad W(b|\bm{x}) = 1 - S(b|\bm{x})$$

And the chance the true price is exactly in bucket l is the chance you missed all earlier buckets, then hit this one:

$$p_{l}= h_{l}\,\prod_{k < l} (1-h_{k})$$

No Gaussian bell curve or Gamma shape is forced in. Any bumpy, multi-peaked distribution — like the real ones in Figure 2 — can be built by choosing the right sequence of hls. A tall h creates a peak in p and a steep jump in W; a tiny h makes things flat.

So who decides the hls? Not a fixed formula. For each ad request \bm{x}^{i} (user, site, ad info turned into a 32-number embedding plus the price b_{l}), a recurrent neural network — specifically an LSTM — predicts:

$$h_{l}^{i}= f_{\theta}(\bm{x}^{i}, b_{l} \mid \bm{r}_{l-1})$$

\bm{r}_{l-1} is the hidden memory passed from the previous price step. Like a person who remembers "prices around $50 have been crowded lately," the network lets what it learned at $1, $2, … inform its guess at $6. That shared memory is what captures sequential patterns over the price space. Each LSTM cell does O(C) work, so for L steps the total is O(C L), easily run in parallel on a GPU — the paper reports 22 milliseconds per ad, fast enough for the 100 ms real-time limit.

Next, try the ladder yourself. Nudge each h_{l} slider and see how \prod(1-h_{l}) builds a win curve W(b) and a price density p_{l} right before your eyes. Flip the memory switch to feel what the RNN's remembering does versus independent guesses.

Two Teachers: Getting the Price Right and the Win Right

So far the model learns a chain of tiny chances — $h_l$ at each price step. But how does it know if that chain is any good? The paper gives it two teachers with two different report cards.

Teacher 1 cares about the exact price. It only gets to look at winning auctions, where the true market price $z^i$ is known and lies in some interval $V_{l^i}$.

In words: $p^i_{l^i}=h^i_{l^i}\prod_{l

Teacher 2 doesn't know exact prices at all. It only knows did you win or lose at the bid $b^i$ you actually submitted — and it can look at every auction, wins and losses. Recall $W(b|x)=1-\prod_{l\le l_b}(1-h_l)$ is your win chance and $S(b|x)=1-W(b|x)$ is your lose chance.

⬆️

For wins: $L_{win}$ pulls UP

$$L_{win}=-\sum_{\mathbb{D}_{win}}\log W(b^i|x^i) = -\sum_{\mathbb{D}_{win}}\log\Big[1-\prod_{l\le l^i}(1-h_l)\Big]$$ If you bid $b^1>z^1$ and won, $W(b^1)$ should be near 1. If the model says $W(b^1)$ is small, $L_{win}$ punishes it — it wants to pull up the winning curve after the true price.

⬇️

For losses: $L_{lose}$ pushes DOWN

$$L_{lose}=-\sum_{\mathbb{D}_{lose}}\log S(b^i|x^i) = -\sum_{\mathbb{D}_{lose}}\sum_{l\le l^i}\log(1-h_l)$$ If you bid $b^2$ and lost, you only know $z\ge b^2$. The model should keep $W(b^2)$ low (so $S(b^2)$ high). If it predicts you'd win, $L_{lose}$ pushes $W$ down.

The key trick: together they are just ordinary win/lose classification. If $w^i=1$ when $b^i>z^i$ and $w^i=0$ otherwise, then $$L_2=L_{win}+L_{lose}=-\sum_{\mathbb{D}}\big[w^i\log W(b^i)+(1-w^i)\log(1-W(b^i))\big]$$ — the cross-entropy loss. No price label needed, only win or lose.

Figure 4 shows the intuition: for a win, push $W$ down on $[0,z]$ and pull it up on $[z,\infty)$, especially between $z$ and your bid $b$; for a loss, just push $W$ down everywhere below $b$ because the truth is hidden.

The final objective balances both teachers:

$$\min_{\theta}\; \alpha L_1 + (1-\alpha)L_2,\quad \alpha=0.25\text{ in the paper}$$

$\alpha$ was tuned so the two gradients are the same size and training is stable. Batches are fed alternately under each loss. Earlier work used only $L_1+L_{lose}$; the paper's RNN baseline uses only $L_1+L_{win}$ to prove that $L_{lose}$ really matters — without it the model over-estimates winning.

When DLF Nails the Hidden Price

This is the paper's "show me" moment. The authors pick one real auction from the test set where the hidden competitor price turned out to be z = 67. For that same ad request they ask every model the same two questions.

First: where do you think the market price is? That is the bottom curve, the price chances p(z | x). A high peak means "I bet the price will be here." Second: if I bid b, how likely am I to win? That is the top curve, the winning chance W(b|x) = Pr(z < b | x). You know from Lesson 2 that one builds the other: W(b) is just the running total of p(z) up to b. So a sharp peak in p makes W jump steeply right after that price. The authors draw a black vertical line at the true 67 so you can check who was right.

What Figure 6 shows: only DLF's p(z) spikes sharply at 67 and its W(b) jumps steeply there. The classic parametric models — Lasso-Cox, DeepSurv and DWPP — all look alike: one smooth, broad bell centered too high, so W rises slowly and never jumps. Gamma is skewed the wrong way. KM and STM are flat staircases, the same for everyone in a segment. RNN has almost the same wiggly shape as DLF but its peak is off-center and its W sits too high.

That picture is the paper's three claims made visible.

1. Accurate forecasting. A good landscape forecast puts high chance exactly where the price really lands. ANLP, the paper's main score, is just -log p(z*|x) — smaller when you peaked at truth. DLF nails it, so its ANLP is lowest. The others spread their bet and pay a big penalty, which is why Table 2 ranks DLF first on every campaign.

2. Flexible, no fixed shape. DLF does not say "all prices are Gaussian" or "all are Gamma." It builds any shape from a chain of tiny conditional bets h_l = Pr(z in V_l | z >= b_{l-1}) at each of the L=300 price steps, chained with S(b)= ∏ (1-h_l). That lets it draw narrow peaks, second bumps, or flat tails. The parametric models cannot — they are stuck with one smooth hill, which the authors note was already rejected by statistical tests on real data.

3. Effective censorship handling. RNN in the figure is DLF's twin: same LSTM, same L1 and L_win, but without the losing-log teacher L_lose = -Σ log S(b). Without those "you would have lost if you bid this low" examples, it over-estimates W(b) and nudges its p to the right. DLF adds that push-down from losses to the pull-up from wins, so the same flexible shape lands in the right place. Seeing RNN and DLF side-by-side isolates exactly what the censorship loss fixes.

In the lab below you can replay Figure 6 yourself. Pick a model, move the true hidden price, and watch the bottom p(z) and top W(b) react. Check the ANLP proxy and the winning bias at b = z*+10 — the two numbers the paper is optimizing for — and see why a flexible chain beats a fixed formula.

Proving It Matters for Real Auctions

So far we built a flexible chain that learns from both wins and losses. But does that flexibility actually help when real money is on the line? The paper tests it on two huge real-world markets — not a toy simulation.

Dataset 1 is iPinYou: 64.7M bids, 19.5M ad impressions across 9 advertiser campaigns in 2013. The last 3 days are held out as the test set. Dataset 2 is YOYI: 402M impressions over 8 days in January 2016. Prices are integers, bucketed into intervals of size 1, with L = 300 possible prices.

Two scores matter. Both use only the true market price, which we do know in the test logs:

  • ANLP — Average Negative Log Probability. For each test request you look up the probability your model gave to the true price zi:
    $$\bar{P} = -\frac{1}{|D_{test}|} \sum_{(x^i,z^i)} \log p(z^i|x^i)$$
    Lower is better. In plain words: did you put a high peak where the truth actually was? This directly tests the PDF p(z|x).
  • C-index — Concordance index. Pick any winning pair and losing pair at a bid b. Does the model rank the winner's chance higher? It is the same as AUC for “would I win if I bid b?” Higher is better, 0.5 is random, 1.0 is perfect. This tests the CDF W(b|x).
The headline result — exact numbers from the paper: iPinYou overall ANLP 4.774 for DLF vs 5.148 for STM (the previous best survival-tree), 5.544 for DeepHit, 15.366 for counting-only Kaplan-Meier. YOYI ANLP 4.453 vs 4.503 for STM. For ranking, iPinYou C-index 0.911 vs 0.807 for STM (+12.9%), 0.858 for DeepHit; YOYI 0.924 vs 0.886. Every star * means p < 10-6 — t-test for ANLP, Mann-Whitney U for C-index. The RNN with the same chain but no losing-loss L_{lose} only reaches 0.823, proving censorship handling adds the boost.

This is not just one lucky campaign. Table 2 and 3 show DLF is best on every iPinYou campaign (1458, 2259, 2261, 2821, 2997, 3358, 3386, 3427, 3476) and on YOYI, under both metrics.

Why the paper says it is practical: no assumed Gaussian or Gamma shape, fine-grained per-request predictions instead of “everyone in this cluster gets the same curve”, trained on both winning and losing logs, and 22 ms average inference — well inside the ~100 ms budget to decide a bid. Complexity is O(C·L) for L price steps, parallel on GPU, so it can run beside a click-prediction model. The paper calls it the first end-to-end deep, assumption-free landscape forecaster.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

market price (z)

The highest bid from all your competitors in one ad auction. In a second-price auction the winner pays this price, not their own bid.

bid landscape forecasting

Predicting the full probability distribution of the market price for each ad request, written p(z|x) where x is info about the user, publisher and ad.

winning function W(b)

Winning probability given a bid b: W(b)=Pr(z<b)=∫₀ᵇ p(z)dz. In the paper's discrete version W(bₗ)=Σ_{j<l} Pr(z in Vⱼ).

losing function S(b)

Losing probability S(b)=Pr(z≥b)=1-W(b)=∫_b^∞ p(z)dz. Called survival function in medical analogy.

right-censored data

Data where you don't see the true value. In RTB you only know the true market price z if you win (z<b). If you lose you only know z ≥ b.

D_win and D_lose

Winning logs D_win where z is known, and losing logs D_lose where z is hidden. Table 1: iPinYou overall winning rate 0.2384, average market price 25.05 in wins vs 99.92 in losses.

conditional hazard hₗ

Conditional winning chance at price step l: hₗ=Pr(z in Vₗ | z ≥ b_{l-1})=pₗ/S(b_{l-1}). The paper predicts hₗ for each of L≤300 price intervals of size 1.

RNN / LSTM

A neural network that passes a hidden memory vector r_{l-1} from one price step to the next. The paper uses LSTM: hₗ = f_θ(x,bₗ | r_{l-1}).

ANLP

Average Negative Log Probability: -1/|D_test| Σ log p(zⁱ|xⁱ). Lower is better. Measures how well you predict the true market price distribution.

C-index

Concordance index. Fraction of win/lose pairs ordered correctly when you rank by predicted W(b). Same as AUC for one event. Higher is better (0.5=random, 1=perfect).
