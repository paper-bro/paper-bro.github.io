How People Win Arguments Without Cooperating

Most language theories assume people talk to help each other—but in a courtroom, a debate, or a tough negotiation, speakers have opposing goals. This course unpacks Strategic Dialogue Assessment (SDA), a framework that scores how every reply builds or damages a speaker's case, and reveals why even advanced reasoning AIs get fooled by clever wordplay.

001The big problem~6 min

Why polite rules fail when speakers want opposite things.

Imagine asking a friend, “Did you eat the last slice of cake?” and hearing, “Well, Sam was in the kitchen earlier.” In everyday chat, you assume your friend is trying to be helpful. You rely on philosopher Paul Grice’s unspoken guidelines for conversation—the Gricean maxims of Quality (be truthful), Quantity (give enough detail), Relevance (stay on topic), and Manner (be clear). Even when your friend bends the topic rule, you work out the implicature—the hidden meaning beyond their literal words—and understand they are denying eating the cake.

Now drop that exact same reply into a courtroom cross-examination. Here, the conversation is non-cooperative: the speakers want opposite things. A lawyer tries to corner a witness into a damaging admission, while the witness tries to protect their side and look trustworthy. When goals clash, polite rules stop being a shared pact and turn into strategic tools.

🎭

Appearing cooperative

Speakers in an adversarial debate often mimic polite, clear answers to project credibility. Answering “Yes” or “No” to “Are you taking any medication?” both appear cooperative, yet one may help the witness while the other hurts their case.

♟️

Being strategic

Being strategic means choosing words that effectively advance your own goal. Sometimes that means looking helpful; other times it means dodging a trap without telling an outright lie.

Figure 1 shows how easily a cooperative reading and a strategic reading pull apart on a real courtroom exchange. A flight attendant is cross-examined about whether she falsified an incident report. Instead of answering yes or no, she says her colleague once “corrected” an incident report, adding “to my knowledge.” Under a cooperative lens (blue box), a reader treats her odd detour as a creative hint that still follows the truth—a helpful denial that always scores a win.

A strategic reader (yellow box) spots three shields at once: deflection (shifting attention to a colleague), hedging (“to my knowledge”), and euphemism (softening “falsified” to “corrected”). The implied denial is not fully trusted, yet it still earns a real, temporary gain because she avoids admitting guilt right now—though she could be trapped later if evidence contradicts her.

Why GPT-4o got fooled: When the paper’s authors tested GPT-4o on this exact exchange, the AI noticed her deflection, labeled the reply non-cooperative simply for breaking Gricean rules, and concluded her answer increased her risk of losing. It mistook breaking a polite rule for losing the argument, missing the short-term safety her dodge achieved.

002Language as a game~7 min

Modeling an argument as a game with a scoreboard.

Imagine choosing your words the way a chess player picks a move. In game-theoretic pragmatics, every reply has a score—called utility—that measures how well it helps you reach your goal.

Early game models of language were built for teammates. In Signaling Games (Lewis, 1969; Franke, 2009) and Rational Speech Act (RSA) theory (Frank and Goodman, 2012), a speaker and a listener share the same payoff: both win when the listener guesses the true situation, such as telling apart some from all. That works when speakers cooperate over a tiny list of possibilities. In a courtroom, though, opponents want opposite outcomes, real-world situations are too messy to list, and a witness is not just talking to the lawyer—they are performing for the jury.

To handle rivals, Message Exchange (ME) Games (Asher et al., 2017) add a third-party judge—called the jury—who acts as a scoreboard for each turn $k$ spoken by player $i$:

$$\parallel\tau_k\parallel_i = (\text{COH}_i(k) + \text{RES}_i(k)) \times \text{CONS}_i(k) \times \text{P}_k(\text{Good}_i) \times \text{win}_i(k)$$

Think of $\parallel\tau_k\parallel_i$ as a multiplication scoreboard made of five checks:

  • Coherence, $\text{COH}_i(k)$ ($-1$ or $+1$): does the reply fit sensibly with the earlier conversation?
  • Responsiveness, $\text{RES}_i(k)$ ($-1$ or $+1$): does it directly connect to the question just asked? Added together, coherence and responsiveness give $-2$, $0$, or $+2$.
  • Consistency, $\text{CONS}_i(k)$ ($0$ or $1$): does the speaker avoid contradicting their own earlier claims? A contradiction gives $0$.
  • Credibility, $\text{P}_k(\text{Good}_i)$ (from $0$ to $1$): how trustworthy the speaker seems at turn $k$.
  • Winning potential, $\text{win}_i(k)$ ($0$ or $1$): whether a realistic path to winning still remains after this turn.

Because all the factors multiply together, failing even one zero-check wipes out the whole turn. Yet two big roadblocks stop this raw formula from scoring real courtroom transcripts:

🔍

1. Hidden numbers

Nobody can read a juror’s mind to measure true credibility $\text{P}_k(\text{Good}_i)$ directly, and checking $\text{win}_i(k)$ would require knowing every possible future path of the entire trial.

⚖️

2. Too coarse for real tactics

In the West Memphis Three trial, expert witness Richard Ofshe gave two coherent, consistent replies: admitting he heard no yelling on police tapes, versus insisting police asked questions “very directly specifying what the answers should be.” Both pass every ME check and earn the same score—even though one hurts his case and the other helps it.

003Commitments & QUDs~6 min

How every answer locks you into a commitment.

In the last lesson, the Message Exchange (ME) Game jury got stuck because it gave two very different courtroom answers the exact same score. To fix that, Strategic Dialogue Assessment (SDA) treats conversation not just as swapping facts, but as managing commitments. Whenever you answer a question—whether you state a fact directly or imply it between the lines—you publicly lock yourself into a claim that the jury and opposing lawyer can hold you to.

Not every claim moves a trial forward, though. To tell whether a commitment actually counts, SDA checks it against the Question Under Discussion (QUD)—the active question on the table right now. Only replies that address the current QUD count as meaningful contributions toward a speaker's goal. If a prosecutor asks, "What coercive tactics did the police use?" and the witness replies, "First, I need to break the interrogation down into parts," that answer gives information, yet it dodges the active QUD and makes no relevant commitment.

Once a reply is checked against the QUD, SDA classifies the speaker's commitment at turn $i$ (written $C_i$) into one of four categories and gives it a base score $f_c(C_i)$:

$$f_c(C_i) = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } C_i = \text{beneficial} \\ +0.5 & \text{if } C_i = \text{neutral} \\ -0.5 & \text{if } C_i = \text{none} \\ -1 & \text{if } C_i = \text{detrimental} \end{cases}$$
✅

Beneficial (+1)

Addresses the QUD and helps the speaker's side—like defense expert Richard Ofshe testifying that police questions "were very directly specifying what the answers should be."

⚖️

Neutral (+0.5)

Addresses the QUD cleanly without helping or hurting either side's main argument—like answering "Mary" to confirm your name, or clarifying how long an interview lasted.

💨

None (−0.5)

Dodges, postpones, or goes off-topic, failing to make any commitment that answers the active QUD.

⚠️

Detrimental (−1)

Addresses the QUD but concedes something harmful to the speaker's goals, such as admitting there may be no record of police misconduct.

Notice why neutral ($+0.5$) and none ($-0.5$) do not both score $0$. Listeners expect a real answer to the question asked. Giving a straightforward neutral answer cooperates with the QUD and keeps your footing, earning a small $+0.5$ credit. Dodging with none breaks that expectation: even if you avoid admitting something damaging, refusing to engage costs you $-0.5$.

The question flips the score: The exact same words can switch categories when the QUD changes. Asked "Are you taking any medication?" to test mental stability, "Yes, sir" is detrimental (−1) and "No, sir" is beneficial (+1). Asked "What is your name?", both answers ignore the QUD and drop to none (−0.5).

004Credibility & Maxims~7 min

Measuring credibility through clarity, relevance, and honesty.

Imagine a witness on the stand who gives an answer that helps her side—say, pointing out that police pressured a suspect—but mumbles, wanders off-topic, and sounds evasive. Should that shaky reply count just as much as a crisp, confident answer? Intuitively, no: how trustworthy you sound shapes how strongly your words land.

In the ME Game scoreboard from Lesson 2, two hidden terms handled this: speaker credibility ($\textnormal{P}_k(\textnormal{Good}_i)$) and winning potential ($\textnormal{win}_i(k)$). Neither can be read directly off a transcript—we cannot peek inside a jury's head, and estimating winning potential would require knowing every possible future turn in the trial. So Strategic Dialogue Assessment (SDA) leaves winning potential aside and approximates credibility using observable clues in how the speaker talks.

🎯

Relevance (0.4)

Did the speaker stay on topic and address the question, or veer into unrelated details?

💬

Manner (0.4)

Was the reply clear and direct, rather than vague, hedged, or convoluted?

🔍

Quality (0.2)

Did the speaker appear sincere and truthful, rather than making an obviously false claim?

Notice that breaking Quality has a smaller weight ($0.2$) than breaking Relevance or Manner ($0.4$ each). In a trial, what is true is often disputed, so SDA uses a lighter weight to avoid over-penalizing perceived rather than proven falsehoods.

SDA also tracks Consistency ($\textnormal{Const}_i$, weight $0.2$): does this turn contradict an earlier commitment? Unlike ME Games—where one contradiction wipes out all value forever—real speakers can still make valid points after slipping up. So SDA applies a steep fine equal to $20\%$ of all Benefit points earned up to that turn ($\textnormal{Const}_i \times \sum_{j=1}^{i}\textnormal{BaT}_j$). The bigger your lead, the harder a self-contradiction hits.

The strategic twist: Breaking Relevance or Manner flips roles depending on your move. On a beneficial ($+1$) or neutral ($+0.5$) answer, sounding vague or off-topic leaks credibility into your Penalty ($\textnormal{PaT}_i$), shrinking your gain. On a detrimental ($-1$) answer, hedging or indirectness works as damage control: the full $1.0$ base loss still hits Penalty, but your indirectness returns a mitigation credit to Benefit ($\textnormal{BaT}_i$).
Go deeper: How maxim weights enter BaT and PaT

Let $f_c(C_i)$ be the base score ($+1$, $+0.5$, $-0.5$, or $-1$), and let $M_i = \textnormal{Rel}_i + \textnormal{Man}_i + \textnormal{Qual}_i$ be the sum of violated maxim weights (from $0$ to $1.0$). For a beneficial or neutral move, $\textnormal{BaT}_i = f_c(C_i)$ and $\textnormal{PaT}_i = |f_c(C_i)| \times M_i + \textnormal{Const}_i \times \sum_{j=1}^{i}\textnormal{BaT}_j$. For a detrimental move, $\textnormal{BaT}_i = |f_c(C_i)| \times M_i$ and $\textnormal{PaT}_i = |f_c(C_i)| + \textnormal{Const}_i \times \sum_{j=1}^{i}\textnormal{BaT}_j$. For a none dodge ($-0.5$), $\textnormal{BaT}_i = 0$ and $\textnormal{PaT}_i = 0.5 + \textnormal{Const}_i \times \sum_{j=1}^{i}\textnormal{BaT}_j$.

005The SDA scoreboard~7 min

Turning words into BaT, PaT, and NRBaT.

Scoring a single answer is useful, but a courtroom cross-examination is a tug-of-war across many turns. Instead of squashing everything into one opaque number like the original ME Game jury function, SDA splits every turn $i$ into two transparent scores: Benefit at Turn ($\text{BaT}_i$) for gains and Penalty at Turn ($\text{PaT}_i$) for losses. Just as a 3–2 soccer score tells you far more than “+1,” keeping gains and penalties separate shows whether a speaker is winning cleanly or scraping by through damage control.

Here is how SDA turns Lesson 4’s pieces into $\text{BaT}_i$. A beneficial ($+1$) or neutral ($+0.5$) commitment keeps its full base score $f_c(C_i)$. A none move (dodging the active question) gets $0$ benefit. If the witness makes a detrimental admission ($f_c(C_i) = -1$), hedging or answering indirectly—represented by maxim violations $\text{Rel}_i$, $\text{Man}_i$, and $\text{Qual}_i$—returns a partial mitigation credit to $\text{BaT}_i$ (with the vertical bars $|f_c(C_i)|$ dropping the minus sign):

$$\text{BaT}_i = \begin{cases} f_c(C_i) & \text{if } C_i \in \{\text{beneficial},\, \text{neutral}\} \\ |f_c(C_i)| \times (\text{Rel}_i + \text{Man}_i + \text{Qual}_i) & \text{if } C_i = \text{detrimental} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Next comes $\text{PaT}_i$. A detrimental ($1$) or none ($0.5$) move pays its full base penalty $|f_c(C_i)|$, whereas a beneficial or neutral move only pays a penalty proportional to any maxim violations. On top of that, if the witness contradicts an earlier turn ($\text{Const}_i = 0.2$), SDA adds $\text{Const}_i$ times all the benefits accumulated from turn $1$ through turn $i$ ($\sum_{j=1}^{i} \text{BaT}_j$):

$$\text{PaT}_i = \begin{cases} |f_c(C_i)| + \text{Const}_i \times \sum_{j=1}^{i} \text{BaT}_j & \text{if } C_i \in \{\text{detrimental},\, \text{none}\} \\ |f_c(C_i)| \times (\text{Rel}_i + \text{Man}_i + \text{Qual}_i) + \text{Const}_i \times \sum_{j=1}^{i} \text{BaT}_j & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

To judge who is ahead overall, you might try subtracting running penalties from running benefits. The catch is that total gains and total penalties often grow at different paces. So SDA puts both cumulative sums onto a shared ruler using z-score normalization ($Z$): for each list of turn totals, subtract the list’s average ($\mu$) and divide by its typical spread ($\sigma$, the standard deviation). Zero means average, $+1$ means one typical step above average, and $-1$ means one step below. Subtracting the z-scored penalties from the z-scored benefits gives Normalized Relative Benefit at Turn ($\text{NRBaT}_i$):

$$\text{NRBaT}_i = Z\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i} \text{BaT}_j\right) - Z\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i} \text{PaT}_j\right)$$

Why build $\text{NRBaT}$ instead of simply counting who “won” each turn? Earlier game-playing AI research (on poker and auctions) used Normalized Relative Advantage ($\text{NRA}$), which tracks the witness’s wins ($\text{win}_w$) minus the questioner’s wins ($\text{win}_q$), divided by all scoring turns: $\text{NRA}_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^i \text{win}_w^j - \sum_{j=1}^i \text{win}_q^j}{\sum_{j=1}^i \text{win}_w^j + \sum_{j=1}^i \text{win}_q^j}$. In a real courtroom exchange about medication, three human raters using $\text{NRA}$ swung wildly apart after turn 5 because one gave the witness wins while another gave none.

Key insight: Because $\text{NRBaT}$ anchors each turn to explicit commitments and Gricean maxims before normalizing, different raters stay far more consistent than when judging subjective “wins” with $\text{NRA}$.

006Real trial data~7 min

Testing SDA on three famous courtroom trials.

To test whether SDA works on real high-stakes arguments, the researchers built the Crooked Path Dataset (CPD) from courtroom cross-examinations in three famous U.S. trials: the West Memphis Three trial (1994), the O.J. Simpson trial (1995), and the Enron trial (2006). In total, CPD contains 4,452 speaking turns, including 3,325 question-and-answer pairs (the remaining turns are mostly lawyer objections): 1,226 Q/A pairs from West Memphis Three, 2,025 from O.J. Simpson, and 74 from Enron.

Three trained annotators—a journalist and two linguistics students paid 20 dollars an hour—read roughly 800 turns in order, about 200 per side per trial. For each turn, they labeled the commitment type, any Gricean maxim violations, and consistency, plus a gut judgment of who seemed to be winning and why. The surprise was how sharply agreement split. On overall win-or-loss verdicts, annotators barely agreed ($\text{Fleiss' }\kappa = 0.29$, on a scale where $0$ is random chance and $1$ is perfect agreement). Yet on SDA's structured scores, they agreed strongly: $\text{Spearman's }\rho = 0.83$ for NRBaT, $0.66$ for PaT, and $0.65$ for BaT.

The dataset also revealed a sharp asymmetry between cooperative and adversarial talk. In direct examination—when a friendly lawyer questions their own witness—answers cluster around high net benefit with rare maxim violations. In cross-examination, where lawyer and witness want opposite things, turns spread wide across gains and losses as witnesses try to sound helpful while dodging traps. (The authors note this direct-versus-cross comparison comes from a single witness whose full transcripts were available, so it serves as an illustrative baseline rather than a final population count.)

Best of all, turn-level BaT and PaT actually predict human outcome judgments. Using logistic regression—a formula that turns scores into a win probability—each $+1.0$ in BaT multiplies the odds of a favorable outcome by $4.33$ ($\beta = 1.47$), while each $+1.0$ in PaT multiplies the odds by $0.17$ ($\beta = -1.77$), shrinking them fast. Together, BaT and PaT classify outcomes with $74.6\%$ accuracy and an AUC of $0.80$ (where $0.50$ is a coin flip and $1.00$ is perfect sorting), beating the best zero-shot LLM judge at $0.68$.

Logic beats juror bias

Why wasn't SDA's prediction $100\%$? When annotators gave logical reasons for a verdict—like a witness admitting medication use—SDA's predictive power jumped significantly ($p < .0001$). When verdicts rested on personal emotions—like disliking a witness's habits—accuracy dropped ($p < .05$). SDA tracks the strategic force of words, not personal prejudice.

007AI vs. strategy~7 min

Bigger models see strategy better, but reasoning models overthink.

Once SDA could score courtroom transcripts reliably, the authors put modern AI to the test. They evaluated 12 large language models—six pairs comparing standard instruction-tuned models against reasoning-enhanced versions, from 7-billion-parameter open models up to proprietary systems like GPT-4o-mini, o3-mini, and Gemini-2.5-Flash. Every model received the exact same zero-shot instructions as the human annotators, with temperature set to $0.1$ for steady answers.

The results revealed a sharp split between surface manners and real strategy. Averaged across all models (written as $\mu$), AI systems matched or exceeded human agreement on Gricean maxim violations: Quality ($\mu = 0.93$), Relevance ($\mu = 0.80$), and Manner ($\mu = 0.52$), partly because violations are relatively rare in the dataset. Yet on what a reply actually accomplishes in the fight, models struggled badly. Average agreement plunged on commitment type ($\mu = 0.14$), $\text{BaT}$ ($\mu = 0.23$), $\text{PaT}$ ($\mu = 0.13$), and $\text{NRBaT}$ ($\mu = 0.27$)—far behind human annotators ($0.59$ to $0.83$). Adding few-shot examples or extra prompting rules barely moved the needle (all shifts stayed under $0.1$).

📏

Bigger Models See More

Scaling up model size boosted strategic accuracy. Measuring the average shift ($\Delta\mu$) from smaller to larger models, bigger models gained $+0.16^*$ on $\text{BaT}$, $+0.12$ on $\text{PaT}$, and $+0.10^*$ on commitment type (asterisks mark statistically significant gains), plus $+0.12^*$ on Manner. Cumulative $\text{NRBaT}$ was noisier ($+0.08$) because local errors sometimes cancel out over a long transcript.

🌀

Reasoning Models Overthink

Explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning—where an AI writes out step-by-step thoughts before answering—generally hurt. Reasoning variants scored worse on $\text{PaT}$ ($\Delta\mu = -0.10^*$, losing 22 of 30 comparisons), $\text{NRBaT}$ ($-0.09$), and $\text{BaT}$ ($-0.03$), with GPT-o3-mini as a partial exception. Thinking longer slightly helped spot Manner violations ($+0.06$), but dulled the models' sense of strategic losses.

Reading the models' Chain-of-Thought traces explains why. Reasoning models frequently overcomplicate replies and mistake surface damage control—such as hedging, minimizing, or polite qualification—for an actual win. When an expert witness implies a court rejected his testimony in "one case," the model's trace initially spots the harm, then talks itself into labeling the answer beneficial because the witness sounded cautious. In another exchange, a trace invents a nonexistent contrast and praises a damaging guilty-plea admission. Flipping that single commitment label inverts the base score and turns a penalty into a fake gain.

Where SDA stops—and what comes next

SDA scores immediate, turn-by-turn effects, so it does not capture long-horizon traps where a speaker accepts a small hit now to avoid a bigger disaster later. It also scores whole turns rather than mixed sub-turn clauses, groups all harms into four coarse commitment levels, and leaves out unobservable winning potential ($\text{win}_i(k)$) and juror biases. Even so, SDA turns abstract game theory into a working yardstick for testing whether AI understands adversarial speech.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Non-cooperative discourse

A conversation where the speakers have opposing goals, so one person's win is the other's loss, such as a courtroom cross-examination.

Pragmatics

The study of how context, speaker intentions, and unspoken rules shape what words actually mean beyond their dictionary definitions.

Gricean maxims

Four classic conversational guidelines proposed by philosopher Paul Grice: Quality (be truthful), Quantity (give the right amount of info), Relevance (stay on topic), and Manner (be clear).

Implicature

An implied meaning a listener infers from what a speaker says (or avoids saying) in context, rather than what was literally stated.

Message Exchange (ME) Games

A formal game-theoretic model of adversarial conversations where a third-party 'jury' scores each speaking turn based on coherence, consistency, credibility, and winning potential.

Question Under Discussion (QUD)

The active question or topic that the current conversation is trying to resolve, which determines whether a reply actually contributes to the discussion.

Commitment

Publicly taking a stance on a fact or claim (either literally or by implication) that a speaker can later be held accountable for.

SDA

Strategic Dialogue Assessment, the paper's framework that scores conversational moves using commitment types, Gricean maxim violations, and consistency.

BaT

Benefit at Turn: the strategic gain a speaker earns on a single turn from making a helpful commitment or softening a damaging one.

PaT

Penalty at Turn: the strategic loss a speaker suffers on a single turn from a damaging admission, dodging the question, violating conversational rules, or contradicting themselves.

NRBaT

Normalized Relative Benefit at Turn: the cumulative difference between z-scored benefits and z-scored penalties up to a given turn, showing who is ahead overall.

CPD

The Crooked Path Dataset: 4,452 courtroom turns (3,325 Q/A pairs) collected from cross-examinations in the West Memphis Three, O.J. Simpson, and Enron trials.

Chain of Thought (CoT)

Step-by-step intermediate text generated by reasoning-focused AI models before giving a final answer.
