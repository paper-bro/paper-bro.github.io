001Lesson 1 • The problem~6 min

A flood of repeats — how many different did you see?

Imagine you watch every click on a popular video site for one day. The stream of IDs flies past:

$$a^m = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m), \qquad a_i \in [n]$$

That just means: $m$ is how many arrivals you saw in total, each $a_i$ is an ID between $1$ and $n$ (so $n$ is how many different IDs could exist), and $a^m$ is the whole ordered list. The question the paper studies is not $m$, but

$$F_0 = |\{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_m\}|$$

Read $F_0$ as "eff-zero": the size of the set you get after removing duplicates. It's how many different IDs actually showed up.

🔁

Repeats don't count

Stream $(3,1,3,1,5)$ has $m=5$ arrivals, $n$ could be huge, but $F_0=3$ distinct values $\{1,3,5\}$. Adding another $3$ makes $m=6$ and $F_0$ stays $3$.

💾

Storing everything is costly

To know $F_0$ exactly you'd normally remember every distinct ID — that can be millions. The streaming challenge: use only $s$ memory slots where $s$ is tiny compared to $F_0$.

That's the Distinct Elements Problem from Section 1 of the paper. You see $a_1$, then $a_2$, once, in order. You may keep at most $s$ candidates in a pocket list $L$. After the last arrival you must output a guess $\hat{F_0}$ — your estimate of $F_0$.

How good must the guess be? The paper uses the standard goal:

$(\epsilon, \delta)$-approximation. Your estimate $\hat{F_0}$ is good if it lands in the interval $(1\pm\epsilon)F_0$, meaning $$(1-\epsilon)F_0 \le \hat{F_0} \le (1+\epsilon)F_0.$$ We require this to happen with high probability: $$\mathsf{Pr}\big[\hat{F_0} \neq (1\pm\epsilon)F_0\big] \le \delta.$$ In words: $\epsilon$ controls “how close” (e.g., $\epsilon=0.1$ is within 10%), $\delta$ controls “how often you’re allowed to fail” (e.g., $\delta=0.05$ is at most 5% of streams).

The paper does not invent a new problem — it proves why existing sampling-based solutions work. The most famous is the CVM algorithm from Chakraborty et al., which Donald Knuth wrote about and called Algorithm CVM / Algorithm D and D'. All of them keep at most $s$ pairs $(x,r)$ and raise or lower a cutoff to stay within the pocket. This course unifies them and shows they are unbiased and $(\epsilon,\delta)$-accurate for the right $s$.

Try the lab below: build a stream, slide $m$ and the target $F_0$, and watch how repeats inflate $m$ without changing $F_0$. That gap is exactly why we need clever sampling instead of just counting arrivals.

Going deeper: $n$ vs $m$ vs $F_0$. $n$ is the universe size (e.g., all possible user IDs). $m$ is the stream length you actually observe. $F_0$ is what you want to learn, and $0\le F_0\le \min(m,n)$. Storing a bit for each of $n$ or storing all $m$ arrivals both can be far larger than $s$.

002Lesson 2 • The framework~8 min

A tiny pocket and a moving cutoff

Imagine a long queue of candidates. Each person can come back many times, but you have a tiny pocket that holds at most $s$ name-tags — say $s=3$. You need to guess how many different people ever showed up without storing everyone.

The paper unifies several clever samplers into one simple machine. It is called $\text{Cutoff}[D,C]$ and it has just three knobs:

  • $s$ — how big your pocket is.
  • $D$ — a score distribution that gives every appearance a random number $q_t$ in $[0,1]$.
  • $C$ — a rule for when to tighten your standards.

Here is what happens for each arrival $a_t$ at time $t$. The paper numbers these steps 1 to 4:

  1. Sample a score: draw $q_t \sim D$.
  2. Do we include $a_t$? First throw out any old copy of the same person: $\text{Remove}(L_{t-1},a_t)$. Then include the new copy only if $q_t < p_{t-1}$ — its score beats the current cutoff. That gives a temporary list $L'_t$.
  3. Room? If $|L'_t| \le s$ you are done: keep $p_t=p_{t-1}$ and $L_t=L'_t$.
  4. Overflow? If $|L'_t| > s$ you must tighten: $p_t = C(L'_t,p_{t-1})$ and keep only $L_t = \text{Filter}(L'_t,p_t)$, meaning $\{(a,q)\in L'_t : q < p_t\}$.

We start empty: $L_0=\emptyset$ and $p_0=1$ so at first everything passes. The whole history $(L_t,p_t)$ for $t=1\dots m$ is called the transcript, and Definition 1.1 says it is completely determined by the random scores $q^m = (q_1,\dots,q_m)$.

A key twist: only the last appearance matters in the end. The paper calls that last score a credit score. If Alice appears three times, her first two scores are forgotten — only her final $q$ can keep her in the final pocket $L_m$. But those earlier non-credit scores were not useless: they may have caused an overflow at that earlier time and forced $p_t$ to drop for everyone after.

When the stream ends, how do you guess? If $L_m$ is empty you guess $0$. Otherwise:

$$ \hat{F}_0 = \frac{|L_m|}{D([0,p_m))} $$

In words: take how many tags you kept, divide by the chance a random score would be below your final cutoff. For the simplest case where $D$ is uniform on $[0,1]$, that chance is just $p_m$, so $\hat{F}_0 = |L_m|/p_m$. If you kept 2 tags and your final bar was $0.25$, you guess about $8$ distinct people — you kept roughly a quarter of them.

Different choices of $D$ and $C$ give the famous variants: DonD, DonD', CVM1 and CVM2 are all the same machine with different knobs. Try the cutoff machine below. Step through one arrival at a time, watch $q_t$ compared to $p_{t-1}$, and see when the pocket overflows and $p_t$ has to move.

003Lesson 3 • Knuth's D~8 min

Knuth's rule — cut the highest score

Imagine you can keep only s = 3 cards in your pocket while a long line of different IDs walks past — say 100,000 different IDs, but your pocket holds 3. You give each arrival a random score $q_t$ in $[0,1]$ from the uniform distribution $U$ — like a dice roll smoothed out to any decimal between 0 and 1. You keep a card only if its score beats your current cutoff $p_t$.

In Knuth's algorithm D, written in the paper as $\text{DonD} = \text{Cutoff}[U,\max_{\text{scr}}]$, the rule is beautifully simple. $D = U$ means "scores are uniform on $[0,1]$" and $C = \max_{\text{scr}}$ means "on overflow, set the new cutoff to the maximum score you currently hold." Formally $\max_{\text{scr}}(L) = \max\{q : (a,q) \in L\}$.

Step by step for each arrival $a_t$:

  1. Draw $q_t \sim U$.
  2. Make $L'_t$: drop any old copy of $a_t$, then add $(a_t,q_t)$ only if $q_t < p_{t-1}$. Otherwise you add nothing.
  3. If $|L'_t| \le s$, do nothing: $p_t = p_{t-1}$, $L_t = L'_t$.
  4. Else overflow: $p_t = \max_{\text{scr}}(L'_t)$ and $L_t = \text{Filter}(L'_t,p_t) = \{(a,q)\in L'_t : q < p_t\}$ — throw away exactly the card(s) with that biggest score.
Key insight: $p_t$ never goes up. And by induction $|L_t| \le s$ always. In fact the else only fires when $|L'_t| = s+1$ and the arriving $a_t$ was not in $L_{t-1}$ but passed its score check — a new distinct ID just made the pocket overflow by one.

Why does $p_t$ exist as a past score? Because whenever it drops, it is set to $\max(L'_t)$, which is some $q_j$ you saw before. Between overflows it stays flat — a decreasing staircase. You drop exactly the current worst score, so the bucket returns to size $s$.

Compare this to its cousin $\text{CVM2}' = \text{Cutoff}[G_{\infty},C_{\text{CVM2'}}]$ from the paper. There the cutoff rule ignores scores: on overflow it always halves — $p_t = p_{t-1}/2$ and $L_t = \text{Filter}(L'_t,p_t)$. CVM1' is the same but it Aborts if $\max_{\text{scr}}(L'_t) \ne p_{t-1}/2$. So: DonD cuts to the data (the actual maximum), CVM2 halves blindly.

The discrete version the paper actually analyzes is $\text{DonD}_{\text{disc}} = \text{Cutoff}[U_N,\max_{\text{scr}}]$ with $U_N$ uniform on $\{0,1/N,\dots,(N-1)/N\}$ for a large power of two $N$ — a computer-friendly version of the same smooth dice.

In the lab, step through a stream of distinct IDs with your chosen $s$. Slide $s$ down to 2 and watch overflows fire more often and $p_t$ tumble faster. Switch to "halve" mode to see CVM2's fixed $1, 1/2, 1/4,\dots$ staircase versus DonD's irregular drops that always land on a real past score.

004Lesson 4 • Two kinds of randomness~7 min

Smooth dice or halving dice?

Until now every score was like a smooth roll of a dice: any number between 0 and 1 was possible. The paper shows you can also use a "halving dice" that only lands on 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, ... — and nothing else changes.

To compare them, the authors define a linear score distribution. In plain words: the chance that a score falls below $x$ is $x$ itself. Write it as $D([0,x)) = x$. That means a threshold twice as small is hit half as often, no matter how you dice.

Two examples are both linear:
U — the usual uniform on $[0,1]$ (support $Ω=[0,1]$). $\Pr(q < 0.3)=0.3$.
$G_{\infty}$ — the halving dice on $\{2^{-1},2^{-2},\dots\}$ with $\Pr(2^{-k})=2^{-k}$. Even though values are spaced out, $\Pr(q < 2^{-k}) = 2^{-k}$ still holds, so it is linear too.

On a real computer we use discrete versions. Pick $N'$, let $N=2^{N'}$. Then $U_N$ lives on $\{0,1/N,\dots,(N-1)/N\}$ and $G_{N'}$ lives on $\{2^{-N'-1},2^{-N'},\dots,2^{-1}\}$ with $G_{N'}(2^{-k})=2^{-k}$ for $k\le N'$ and $G_{N'}(2^{-N'-1})=2^{-N'}$ for the last bucket. As $N$ grows, $U_N$ looks like $U$.

How are they linked? With a simple map $g$:

$$g_{N'}(x)=2^{-k}\;\text{if }2^{-k}\le x < 2^{-k+1},\qquad g_{N'}(x)=2^{-N'-1}\;\text{if }0\le x\le 2^{-N'-1}$$

Take your smooth uniform draw $x$ and round down to the next power of two. If $x\sim U$ then $g_{\infty}(x)\sim G_{\infty}$, and if $x\sim U_{2^{N'}}$ then $g_{N'}(x)\sim G_{N'}$. Paper Eq.(1) is that this rounding never moves far:

$$x/2 \le g(x) \le x\quad\text{for }x\ne 0$$

So a geometric score is always within a factor of 2 of its uniform parent.

Why care? DonD is defined as $\text{Cutoff}[U,\max_{\text{scr}}]$ and DonD' as $\text{Cutoff}[G_{\infty},\max_{\text{scr}}]$. But DonD' is the same as running DonD on uniform scores and then applying $g$ to every score ($\text{Cutoff}[U,\max_{\text{scr},g}]$ with $\max_{\text{scr},g}(L)=\max g(L)$). For coupled runs with $q_i=g(u_i)$ the cutoffs satisfy $p'_t \le p_t \le 2p'_t$ — the same factor-2 link. The paper will use this later to carry bounds from the halving dice to the smooth dice.

005Lesson 5 • Why it stays tidy~8 min

Fair sampling — the same rule for everyone

Imagine every distinct name in a stream gets one final score — its last roll of the dice. Call it the credit score. All the earlier rolls for that same name? The paper calls them non-credit scores: they don't decide if the name survives, but they do push the cutoff around.

Write \(\mathcal{F}_t\) for the set of times that are the last appearance of their value up to time \(t\), and \(\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_m\) for the end of the whole stream. If the stream is (A,B,A), then \(\mathcal{F}=\{2,3\}\) because B's last time is 2 and A's is 3. For each \(j\in\mathcal{F}\) the score \(q_j\) is a credit score. There are exactly \(F_0\) of them — one per distinct value.

When \(F_0 > s\) the bucket must overflow at some point, so the final cutoff \(p_m\) drops below 1. In the paper's notation let \(c_r = q_{(r)}^{\mathcal{F}}\) be the \(r\)-th smallest credit score and \(T_r\) the time it appears. Then \(p_m\) is always equal to some past score \(q_j\).

The fair rule: a cutoff algorithm is fair (Definition 3.1) if for every time \(t\) and every last-occurrence \(j\in\mathcal{F}_t\) $$ a_j \in L_t \iff q_j < p_t $$ In words: to be in the pocket \(L_t\) you only need your own credit to beat the current cutoff \(p_t\). No history, no extra points.

Why does DonD satisfy this? The cutoff never goes up. More precisely, Lemma 3.2 (Knuth's Lemma M) says any decreasing cutoff algorithm is fair. Check \(\text{DonD}=\text{Cutoff}[U,\max_{\text{scr}}]\): on overflow it sets \(p_t = \max\{q:(a,q)\in L'_t\}\) and keeps only \(q < p_t\), so \(p_t \le p_{t-1}\). Same for \(\text{DonD}'\) and CVM2. So all of them use one bar for everyone.

Where does \(p_m\) come from? (Lemma 3.3 in simplified form) Fix a set \(S\) with \(|S|=x\le s\) and freeze all non-credit scores \(q^{[m]\setminus\mathcal{F}|_S}=\alpha\). There is a single time \(J(\alpha)\) determined only by that frozen \(\alpha\) such that $$ S\subseteq \text{set}(L_m) \iff p_m = q_{J(\alpha)} \text{ and } q_t < q_{J(\alpha)}\ \forall t\in\mathcal{F}|_S $$ So whether the whole group \(S\) survives depends only on whether its own credits all sit below one fixed threshold \(q_{J(\alpha)}\) that the rest of the stream already decided. The proof builds a fake run where S-credits are forced to 0 and the rest to 1 and shows the real run filters down to it.

This separation is the engine for the next lesson: because \(p_m\) is fixed once \(\alpha\) is fixed, the chance a name survives given \(\alpha\) is just \(\Pr[q_i < q_{J(\alpha)}]\), which for a linear distribution equals \(q_{J(\alpha)}\) itself.

006Lesson 6 • Right on average~7 min

Why the guess averages to the truth

Sometimes an estimate is wild on one stream but perfect on average. Paper-bro calls that unbiased: if you ran the same algorithm on many fresh streams with the same true F0, the average guess would land exactly on F0. This lesson proves DonD, DonD' and CVM2 have that property — no systematic under- or over-counting.

Theorem 3.5 in the paper: For DonD, DonD', and CVM2, E[hatF0] = F0. That is 0-biased — right on average.

One ID at a time

Fix one distinct value, say a = "cat". Let i = Last(a,m) be the time of its last appearance — its credit score is q_i. Let I_a be 1 if a survives in the final pocket set(Lm) and 0 otherwise. Then the final guess is a sum over IDs:

$$\hat F_0 = \frac{|L_m|}{p_m} = \sum_{a\in A} \frac{I_a}{p_m}$$

So if we can show each ID contributes 1 on average, we are done. That is, we want E[ I_a / p_m ] = 1 for every a. Add up F0 of them and you get F0.

The trick: freeze everything except one coin

Imagine freezing all random scores except the credit score of our chosen a. Call those frozen scores alpha = q^{[m] \setminus \{i\}}. Lemma 3.3 from last lesson then says the final cutoff p_m is stuck — it must equal q_j for some fixed time j = J(alpha) that depends only on alpha, not on q_i.

Conditioned on alpha, p_m = q_j is just a number. Then survival is simple:

$$I_a = 1 \iff q_i < q_j = p_m$$

By fairness, you are in iff your own last score beats the cutoff. So I_a / p_m is either 0 (if you miss) or 1/q_j (if you make it).

Linear dice make the math cancel

Here the paper uses that the score distribution D is linear: for any threshold x,

$$D([0,x)) = x, \quad\text{ so }\quad \Pr(q_i < x) = x$$

In words, the chance your random score lands below x is exactly x. This holds for uniform on [0,1] and also for the geometric dice G_{\infty} on powers of two — both are linear by design.

Apply it with x = q_j:

$$\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{I_a}{p_m} \Big| \alpha \right] = \frac{1}{q_j}\cdot \Pr(q_i < q_j \mid \alpha) = \frac{1}{q_j}\cdot q_j = 1$$

The 1/q_j penalty when you are kept exactly cancels the q_j chance of being kept. Average 1, no matter what alpha was. Take the average over all alpha and you still get 1.

In a simplified version: E[I_a/p_m | alpha] = (1/q_j)·Pr(q_i < q_j) because conditional on alpha, q_j=J(alpha) is fixed while q_i is still fresh. Linearity gives Pr(q_i < q_j)=D([0,q_j))=q_j. Sum over a by linearity of expectation: E[|Lm|/pm]=|A|=F0. The paper notes this works for any Cutoff[D,max_scr] or CVM2 with linear D.

Try the lab: it runs 1,000 tiny streams with 5 distinct IDs and a pocket of only s=2. Single guesses jump around — 2, 6, 10 — but the running average settles on 5. Switch between uniform and geometric dice and change s to see the unbiased average persist.

007Lesson 7 • How wrong can we be?~9 min

Taming the error — two kinds of bad luck

Even if your sampler is fair on average, you still want it to be close most of the time. The paper splits the failure into two very different kinds of bad luck — and tames each one separately.

First, what does “close” mean? Define an error event for each possible final cutoff p:

$$E^{+}_{p}:\; |L_{m}|/p > F_{0}(1+\epsilon)\;\text{and}\; p_{m}=p$$

That is: we ended with cutoff p and our estimate |Lm|/p overshot by more than ε. Swap > for < and you get E-p for undershooting. Take the union over all p ≥ p0 and you get E≥p0 — “we were wrong while the cutoff stayed reasonably high.”

The second kind of bad luck is simply pm < p0: the cutoff fell too low. So for any threshold p0 you can write in plain words:

Error = (cutoff fell too low) or (cutoff stayed ≥ p0 but the count was still off).
Mathematically, Pr(Error) ≤ Pr(pm < p0) + Pr(E≥p0). Pick a good p0 and bound each piece.

To bound either piece you need a tool for “how far does a random count stray from its average?” That tool is a Chernoff bound. Lemma 4.2 says for any fair, linear sampler, |Lm| when pm=p behaves like a binomial — flip a coin with heads prob p for each of the F0 distinct items:

$$\Pr[L_{m}=B \wedge p_{m}=p] \le p^{|B|}(1-p)^{F_{0}-|B|}$$

The chance of exactly the set B surviving is no more than the coin-flip chance. So the number kept, |Lm|, is Binomial(F0,p) with average F0p.

The Chernoff shortcut (Fact 4.1)
For N coin flips with mean Np:
$$\Pr[\text{count} \le Np(1-\epsilon)] \le e^{-Np\epsilon^{2}/2},\quad \Pr[\text{count} \ge Np(1+\epsilon)] \le e^{-Np\epsilon^{2}/3}$$
Key idea: the bigger Np is, the exponentially smaller the tails. If Np ≥ 3\epsilon^{-2}\log(1/\beta) then both tails ≤ β.

Now choose p0 cleverly. The paper sets k = ⌈log2(2F0/s)⌉ and p0 = 2-k. This guarantees

$$s/2 \ge F_{0}p_{0} \ge s/4$$

in words: the average bucket size at p0 is about s/4 to s/2. So making s large directly makes Np = F0p0 large, which makes Chernoff tails tiny.

Lemma 4.4 uses exactly that: pm < p0 can only happen if just before the drop we already had ≥ s items at cutoff p0. That is the upper tail EP+(F0,p0,1), bounded by e-F0p0/3. If s ≥ 12 log(2m/δ) this is ≤ δ/2m per time step, so union over m steps gives ≤ δ/2. Lemma 4.5 does the same for DonD' and CVM1' (with s ≥ 12 log(4m/δ)), and Lemma 4.6 lifts it to the uniform DonD using the coupling p't ≤ pt ≤ 2p't, at the cost of a factor 2: s ≥ 24 log(4m/δ).

That handles the “cutoff too low” half. The remaining half E≥p0 — being wrong even though the cutoff stayed high — is also a Chernoff tail problem, tackled in the next lesson with two different tricks for geometric versus uniform scores.

008Lesson 8 • The final guarantees~9 min

The payoff — provably accurate with a tiny pocket

We have seen the cutoff sampler keeps at most s scored items, that it is fair, and that on average it is exactly right: $\mathbf{E}[\hat F_0]=F_0$. But "right on average" is not enough. You want a promise like "within 10 percent, 95 percent of the time." That is an $(\epsilon,\delta)$-approximation: $\Pr[\hat F_0 \neq (1\pm\epsilon)F_0]\le \delta$.

The paper proves that promise for all four variants — DonD, DonD', CVM1 and CVM2 — with an explicit recipe for how big $s$ must be. Two different recipes, because uniform dice and halving dice behave differently.

The two $s$ formulas the paper proves:

Pick $k=\lceil \log_2(2F_0/s)\rceil$ and $p_0=2^{-k}$, so $s/2 \ge F_0 p_0 \ge s/4$. Then:

For G-scores (DonD', CVM1', CVM2'): $s = \max\{24\log\frac{4m}{\delta},\; \frac{6}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{8}{\delta}\}$ is enough.

For uniform scores (DonD): $s = \max\{24\log\frac{4m}{\delta},\; \frac{24}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{96}{\epsilon^2\delta}\}$ is enough.

The first term $24\log(4m/\delta)$ makes the chance the cutoff gets too tiny — $p_m < p_0$ — at most $\delta/2$. The second term makes the chance you are wrong even when $p_m\ge p_0$ also at most $\delta/2$. Together $\delta$.

Why does DonD need a larger second term — $24$ instead of $6$, and $\log(96/\epsilon^2\delta)$ instead of $\log(8/\delta)$? With G-scores the cutoff can only be $1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8,\dots$ So the error event $E_{\ge p_0}=\bigcup_{p\ge p_0}E_p$ is a union over doubling powers. By Lemma 4.2 the tail for each $p$ is $\le (\delta/8)^{2^i}$, so the sum is a super-fast geometric series: $(\delta/8)^1+(\delta/8)^2+(\delta/8)^4+\cdots \le \delta/2$. Uniform scores can be any number in $[0,1]$, so that tidy series disappears.

C1 vs C2 — where did $p_m$ actually come from?

To fix that, the paper invents a trick. Fix a set $S$ of size $x\le s$ you hope ends up in the list. Replace its last scores by $0$ and everyone else's last scores by $1$, keep all other scores the same, and rerun the algorithm to get a ghost cutoff $p'_m$. That ghost depends only on non-credit scores.

Now order the true credits $c_1 < c_2 < \cdots < c_{F_0}$ and let $c_{x+1}$ be the first credit outside $S$. Theorem 5.2 says exactly one of two things happens:

  • C1: $c_{x+1} \ge p'_m$. Then the real cutoff equals the ghost: $p_m=p'_m \le c_{x+1}$. Your set $S$ was decided without looking at $c_{x+1}$.
  • C2: $c_{x+1} < p'_m$. Then the real cutoff is that next credit: $p_m=c_{x+1} < p'_m$. The outsider set the threshold.

In both cases the paper can bound $\Pr[\text{set}(L_m)=S \wedge E^+_{\ge p_0}]$ by $p^x(1-p)^{N-x}$-type binomial tails, maximized at $p=x/N'$ with $N'=N(1+\epsilon)$ for the high-error side and $N(1-\epsilon)$ for the low-error side. Chernoff (Fact 4.1) then gives $\le \exp(-\epsilon^2 s/24)$. You have to sum over about $s$ possible values of $x$, which is why you need $\exp(-\epsilon^2 s/24)\le \delta/4s$ — that extra $s$ inside the log is where the $24$ and the $\epsilon^2$ in the denominator of DonD come from.

The payoff: with those $s$, $\Pr(E^+_{\ge p_0})\le \delta/4+\delta/4$ and $\Pr(E^-_{\ge p_0})\le \delta/4+\delta/4$ — so total error $\le \delta$. The paper thus unifies CVM and Knuth's D/D' as cutoff algorithms, proves all are unbiased, and gives the first tight $(\epsilon,\delta)$ pocket sizes. DonD' and friends get away with a $×4$ smaller constant because powers-of-two collapse the sum; DonD pays more but works with smooth uniform dice.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Distinct elements / F0

The number of distinct values in a stream a1,...,am. Written F0 = |{a1,...,am}|. Example: stream (cat, dog, cat) has F0 = 2.

Streaming algorithm

An algorithm that sees a1, then a2, etc., once, and can keep only a small memory (here at most s items), not the whole stream.

Bucket limit s

Bucket limit s. The maximum number of (element, score) pairs the algorithm is allowed to store at any time.

Score qt and score distribution D

For each appearance at time t, draw qt in [0,1] from a score distribution D. This random number decides if we keep the element.

Cutoff pt

Number pt in [0,1]. We keep only pairs with score < pt. The algorithm lowers pt when the bucket overflows. Filter(L,p) = {(a,q) in L : q < p}.

Transcript

The list of pairs L_t and cutoff pt after each step t=1..m. Written A(q^m) = (L_t, pt). Completely determined by the scores q^m.

Last occurrence / credit score

The last appearance time of each distinct value, and its score at that time. Only this last credit score matters for the final list.

Fair algorithm

A cutoff algorithm is fair if for any time t and any j that is a last occurrence up to t: aj is in Lt exactly when qj < pt.

Estimate hatF0

Final guess. If Lm is empty guess 0, otherwise hatF0 = |Lm| / D([0,pm)). For uniform D this is |Lm| / pm. For D on powers of two it is |Lm| / pm similarly with D's probabilities.

Unbiased / delta-biased

Unbiased means on average correct: E[hatF0] = F0. Biased by at most delta means |E[hatF0] - F0| <= delta.

(epsilon, delta)-approximation

Guarantee: Pr[ hatF0 is NOT between (1-epsilon)F0 and (1+epsilon)F0 ] <= delta. Small epsilon = accurate, small delta = reliable.

Linear distribution

A distribution with D([0,x)) = x for x in its support Omega. Uniform U on [0,1] is linear. Geometric-like G_infty on {2^-k} with Pr=2^-k is also linear.

Chernoff bound

A math bound for coin flips: the number of heads stays close to its mean Np. Paper's Fact 4.1: tail probabilities EP+, EP- <= exp(-Np epsilon^2 /3) or /2.
