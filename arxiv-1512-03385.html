001Lesson 1 — The mystery~4 min

Why did deeper networks suddenly fail?

Imagine you study for a test with flashcards. You learn 20 cards well. Then someone gives you the same 20 cards plus 14 blank cards you can leave blank — you should do at least as well, right? You could just ignore the blanks.

That is what researchers expected when they made image-recognition networks deeper. A network is a stack of layers that transforms an image step by step into a guess: "cat", "dog", "car" — one of 1000 classes in the ImageNet dataset (1.28 million training images). More layers was supposed to mean richer features, from edges to shapes to whole objects. VGG showed it worked: going from 16 to 19 layers won big in 2014.

So teams kept stacking. They fixed early training problems with smart starting weights and with batch normalization — a trick that keeps signals in a healthy range after each layer. Now even fairly deep nets could start learning. But a new mystery appeared.

Deeper suddenly meant worse — even on the training images it had just studied. The paper calls this the degradation problem.

That last part matters. We measure two scores. Training error = mistakes on images it learns from. Validation/test error = mistakes on new images it has never seen. If training error is low but test error is high, it memorized — that is overfitting. Here the opposite happened.

On CIFAR-10 (a smaller test with 50,000 tiny images), a plain 20-layer net learned fine. A plain 56-layer net — same kind of layers, just deeper — had higher training error the whole time, and higher test error too (paper's Fig. 1). On ImageNet, a plain 34-layer net had 28.54% top-1 error (its #1 guess was wrong 28.54% of the time) while a shallower plain 18-layer net had 27.94%. Deeper was worse by about 0.6 points on training data, so it was not overfitting.

Think again about the blank cards. A deeper network could just copy the shallow one and set extra layers to do nothing — an identity mapping where output = input. If it did, its training error could never be worse. The fact it is worse means the optimizer — the step-by-step nudging that teaches the network — cannot find that simple "do nothing" solution.

This is a clue, not just a complaint. If we know deeper should not be worse, and it is, the problem is not the power of the network but how we ask it to learn. The next lessons show the one-line rewiring that made the "do nothing" solution easy to find.

Try it: Drag depth from 6 to 56 layers. The orange curve is a plain net's training error — watch it rise. The green curve is the same net with the paper's skip added — watch it fall. Toggle each on and off, pause at 18 and 34 layers to see the paper's ImageNet numbers, and notice you are looking at training error, not overfitting.

002Lesson 2 — How a network learns~5 min

A neural network in plain English

Think of an image network like an assembly line. A photo of a cat comes in on the left as a grid of pixels — we’ll call it x. Each station along the line transforms it a little: find edges, then textures, then ears and whiskers, then finally vote: “tabby cat, 92%.” In the paper that line ends with an average pool and a 1000-way choice for ImageNet’s 1000 classes.

Each station is a layer with its own knobs, called weights $W_i$. For ImageNet the authors mostly use tiny $3\times3$ filters that slide over the picture. When the picture is the same size, they keep the same number of filters; when they shrink the picture by half with a stride-2 step, they use twice as many filters. That’s why the sizes in the real ResNet go $56\times56 \to 28\times28 \to 14\times14 \to 7\times7$.

Between layers there are two small but important helpers. After each convolution the paper adds batch normalization — like straightening papers on the line so the next worker gets them neat — and then a ReLU activation $\sigma$: $$ \sigma(z) = \max(0, z) $$ which simply keeps positives and turns negatives to zero. It’s the bend that lets the line learn curves, not just straight lines.

Learning means nudging all those $W_i$ so the whole chain’s guess, which the paper calls the underlying mapping $\mathcal{H}(x)$, gets closer to the right answer. Training does this with SGD on mini-batches of 256 images, starting with a learning rate of 0.1, momentum 0.9 and a tiny weight decay of 0.0001. Every image is also cleaned to per-pixel mean zero and randomly cropped, flipped and re-scaled — the same recipe is used for every plain and residual net, so comparisons are fair.

A plain network just stacks those helpers and hopes the stack directly fits $\mathcal{H}(x)$. More stations should mean a smarter line, but as Lesson 1 showed, that hope breaks.

Key idea: a network = chain of transformations $x \to \dots \to \mathcal{H}(x)$ built from weights $W_i$ plus BN + ReLU. Depth = how many $3\times3$ steps you add.

The paper goes further than this lesson — it also tests many exact depths, filter counts and FLOPs in Table 1 — but the intuition is the same. Try the lab below: build your own plain line, add $3\times3$ layers stage by stage, and watch the picture shrink, the filter counts change, and the parameter count climb.

003Lesson 3 — The one-line fix~6 min

Don't learn the whole thing — learn the difference

Think of every block of a neural network as trying to transform an input x into a desired output. Call that desired output H(x). In a plain network you stack layers and hope they learn H(x) directly.

The ResNet paper asks: what if we make the job easier? Instead of learning the whole thing, learn only the difference from the input.

$$ \mathbf{y}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x},\{W_i\}) + \mathbf{x} \quad\text{where}\quad \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) := \mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{x} $$

Here x is what goes into the block, y is what comes out, and Wi are the adjustable weights inside the block. F(x) is called the residual — literally "what's left over" after you subtract x. The original wish H(x) is then rebuilt as F(x) + x.

💡 The one-line fix: add a wire that skips the layers and adds x back. The layers now only have to learn the small leftover F(x).

Concretely, a classic 2-layer block does this: first layer transforms x to W1·x, passes it through ReLU σ which keeps positives and zeroes negatives, then second layer transforms again. In symbols simplified from the paper:

$$ \mathcal{F}=W_2\,\sigma(W_1\mathbf{x}) \qquad\text{and after adding the skip, }\; \sigma(\mathbf{y}) \text{ is applied} $$

That extra + x is element-by-element addition, channel by channel, and it never closes. It adds no extra learnable parameters and almost no computation. The whole network is still trained end-to-end with the usual SGD and backpropagation.

Why is this easier? Imagine the best thing for a block to do is nothing — just copy x to y (an identity mapping). A plain stack would have to carefully tune its non-linear layers to mimic y = x. With the shortcut, the solver can just push the weights toward zero so F(x) → 0, and y = 0 + x = x happens automatically. Pushing toward zero is far simpler.

In real life the optimum isn't exactly identity, but it's often close to it. The paper argues identity is a good precondition — a better starting guess. Evidence: on CIFAR-10 the learned residuals have small size (small standard deviation after batch normalization), and deeper ResNets make even smaller tweaks per layer. Each layer learns a tiny nudge, not a whole new picture.

Try it below: pick a target curve. In "learn H directly" the model must build the whole curve from a single ReLU bend. In "learn F then add x" it starts already sitting on the line y=x and only learns the leftover. Move W1 and W2 or hit auto-tune, and watch the error. Notice how identity is free for the residual mode.

004Lesson 4 — Wires that skip~5 min

The shortcut that costs nothing

Here's the clever part of ResNet: the skip wire itself learns nothing. It's just a copper wire that adds the input back.

Remember the building block from last time? The stacked layers learn the leftover:

$$y = F(x,\{W_i\}) + x$$

Here $x$ is what goes in, $y$ is what comes out, and $F(x,\{W_i\})$ is what the two $3\times3$ convolutions learn. The $\{W_i\}$ are just their weights. After the addition we do a ReLU, $\sigma(y)=\max(0,y)$. The $+x$ part is done by the shortcut — an element-wise addition, channel by channel. It adds no weights and almost no math.

That only works when $x$ and $F$ have the same shape — same width, height, and number of channels. In the paper these are the solid-line shortcuts. For example, inside a stage where everything stays $56\times56$ with 64 channels, you just add.

But networks need to shrink the image and grow channels — say from $56\times56\times64$ to $28\times28\times128$ with a stride-2 convolution. Dotted-line shortcuts cross that boundary, so $x$ (64 channels) can't be added to $F$ (128 channels).

🟩

Option A: free (zero-pad)

Keep the identity for the channels that exist, and fill new channels with zeros. Still zero extra parameters. The padded part learns no residual, but it works.

🟨

Option B: small projection

Use a tiny $1\times1$ convolution $W_s$ to resize $x$: $y = F(x,\{W_i\}) + W_s x$. Only when dimensions change — 3 times in ResNet-34.

🟧

Option C: all projections

Put a $W_s$ on every shortcut — 13 of them. Slightly best, but much heavier. The paper says don't bother.

The crucial number is in Table 3. All three beat the degraded plain net by a lot:

Plain-34: 28.54% top-1 error → ResNet-34 A: 25.03% → B: 24.52% → C: 24.19%

So even Option A — completely free — fixes degradation by about 3.5 percentage points. The gap between A, B, and C is small, which tells us identity shortcuts are already sufficient and economical.

That economy matters a lot next lesson. When we make the network really deep with bottleneck blocks, a projection would connect two high-dimensional ends and double the cost. Identity keeps the 152-layer giant lighter than VGG-19.

💡 Key insight: the shortcut is not a new learner. It's a default path that always lets information through. The convolutions only need to learn the tiny tweak $F$ on top.

005Lesson 5 — Going really deep without getting heavy~6 min

How to make a 152-layer network lighter than a 19-layer one

It sounds impossible: a network with 152 layers that is still lighter than one with 19. But that is exactly what the ResNet paper pulled off — and it is why depth finally became useful instead of painful.

First, the starting point. The authors built a simple plain baseline inspired by VGG. Two rules: if the image size stays the same, use the same number of filters; if you halve the size with a stride-2 convolution, double the filters to keep the work per layer steady. Finish with global average pooling — average each final feature map to one number — and a 1000-way softmax to pick the class.

Their 34-layer plain net needed only 3.6 billion FLOPs. FLOPs just means multiply-add operations — a measure of how heavy the math is. VGG-19 needed 19.6 billion FLOPs. So the 34-layer plain ResNet baseline was already only about 18% of VGG-19's work, even though it was deeper. Being deep does not have to mean being heavy, if you use fewer filters and smarter structure.

The real trick for going really deep is the bottleneck block. A basic block from Fig. 5 left is simple: 3×3, 64 → 3×3, 64 — two big filters that both see 64 channels. A bottleneck block from Fig. 5 right does 1×1, 64 → 3×3, 64 → 1×1, 256. The first 1×1 squeezes channels down, the middle 3×3 does the real work on the small squeezed layer, and the last 1×1 expands back up.

Think of it like a hallway: instead of carrying a huge 256-channel suitcase through a wide 3×3 door, you squeeze it to 64, walk through the small door, then expand again on the other side. Both designs take roughly the same time, but the bottleneck can handle much wider networks without blowing up. Inside each block the skip is still free: $y = \mathcal{F}(x,\{W_i\}) + x$, where $x$ is the input to the block and $\mathcal{F}$ is the 2 or 3 layers inside. The addition is element-wise, channel by channel, followed by ReLU $\sigma(y)=\max(0,y)$.

This is why identity shortcuts matter so much here. The paper notes that if the shortcut in a bottleneck had to use a projection $W_s$ — a 1×1 convolution to match dimensions via $y = \mathcal{F}(x)+W_s x$ — it would have to connect the two high-dimensional ends (256 channels). That would roughly double the cost and size. With identity $+x$, the block stays cheap.

With bottlenecks, Table 1 builds a family from the same recipe. ResNet-50 replaces each 2-layer basic block with a 3-layer bottleneck: 3.8B FLOPs. ResNet-101 adds many more blocks in the middle: 7.6B FLOPs. ResNet-152 adds even more: 11.3B FLOPs. Even the deepest — 152 layers, 8× deeper than VGG — is still lighter than VGG-16 at 15.3B and far lighter than VGG-19 at 19.6B.

And depth finally helped. On ImageNet validation with 10-crop testing, Table 3 shows top-1 error falling as depth grows: ResNet-34 A 25.03% → ResNet-50 22.85% → ResNet-101 21.75% → ResNet-152 21.43%. No extra magic, just more bottleneck blocks with free skips.

Try it in the lab: swap basic for bottleneck, add blocks to conv4_x, and watch the depth climb while the FLOPs bar stays below VGG. See if you can build the 152-layer model that is still lighter than a 19-layer VGG.

006Lesson 6 — Does it actually work?~6 min

152 layers and a 3.57% winning score

This is the moment the paper stops arguing and starts proving. After lessons of "it should work," the authors ask: on real ImageNet, does a skip actually fix training, and does depth finally pay off?

They set up the fairest fight possible. Take an 18-layer plain net and a 34-layer plain net. Then build the exact same two nets, but add free identity skips — no new knobs, no new heavy math, same number of weights. Everything else is equal: 1.28 million training images, batch size 256, same start learning rate 0.1. If the skip helps, only the skip can take credit.

The plain result looks wrong at first glance:

  • Plain-18: 27.94% top-1 error on validation (so its #1 guess is wrong 27.94% of the time).
  • Plain-34: 28.54% top-1 error — worse with more layers.

And it's not bad luck on new images. In Fig.4 left, the thin training-error curve for plain-34 sits above plain-18 for the whole training run. It does worse even on the images it is learning from. That means it's not overfitting (memorizing). It's under-optimizing — the trainer can't find a good path when the stack gets deep.

Now add the skip:

  • ResNet-18: 27.88% — about the same as plain-18 when shallow.
  • ResNet-34: 25.03% — a 3.5-point jump ahead of plain-34.

In Fig.4 right, the ResNet-34 training curve dives well below ResNet-18. Deeper now means better, because training error finally goes down as it should. The bold validation curves follow.

Was it just vanishing gradients? No. With batch normalization after each conv, the authors checked: signals going forward keep healthy size, and gradients coming backward keep healthy size too. Nothing vanished to zero. Their guess: very deep plain nets converge exponentially slowly — like taking tiny, wobbly steps in a maze — while the $y = F(x)+x$ skip gives a straight hallway that keeps you moving.

Once the fix works, the team can go tall without getting heavy. Remember from last lesson a 34-layer ResNet costs 3.6 billion FLOPs vs VGG-19's 19.6 billion. With bottleneck blocks the paper builds:

  • ResNet-50: 22.85% top-1, 6.71% top-5 (10-crop)
  • ResNet-101: 21.75% / 6.05%
  • ResNet-152: 21.43% / 5.71% — still only 11.3 billion FLOPs, lighter than VGG-16's 15.3 billion

With the full test recipe — fully-convolutional, averaging 5 scales (short side 224,256,384,480,640) — single-model ResNet-152 reaches 19.38% top-1 and 4.49% top-5 on validation. That one model already beats every previous ensemble.

The headline score: An ensemble of 6 ResNets (including two 152-layer) gets 3.57% top-5 error on the hidden test set. The previous best ensembles were VGG 7.32%, GoogLeNet 6.66%, PReLU-net 4.94%. ResNet won 1st place in ILSVRC 2015 classification — and the same deep features later won detection, localization, and COCO too.

007Lesson 7 — 100, 1000 layers~5 min

What 1202 layers taught us

ImageNet was huge — 1.28 million images, 1000 classes. Would the weird failure we saw there happen on a tiny dataset too? The paper checked on CIFAR-10: just 50,000 training images, 10,000 test images, 10 classes (like cat, truck, frog), each image only 32 by 32 pixels.

They built a simple family of networks for CIFAR-10 on purpose. Start with one 3 by 3 convolution, then add 6n more 3 by 3 layers in three groups: 2n layers with 16 filters on 32 by 32 maps, 2n with 32 filters on 16 by 16 maps, 2n with 64 filters on 8 by 8 maps. Total weighted layers = 6n + 2. Downsampling happens with a stride of 2. No fancy tricks — and for a fair test, plain and ResNet versions have exactly the same depth, width, and number of parameters. When ResNets are used, it's only identity shortcuts (option A from last lesson).

Plain degraded again. ResNet didn't. In Fig.6 left, plain-44 and plain-56 have higher training error than plain-20. Plain-110 shoots above 60% error and disappears off the chart. In Fig.6 middle, ResNets do the opposite: ResNet-20 8.75% test error, ResNet-56 6.97%, ResNet-110 6.43% best (6.61 ±0.16 averaged over 5 runs) with only 1.7M parameters — beating much larger thin networks like Highway 19-layer at 7.54%.

So this isn't an ImageNet quirk. It's a general optimization problem: the solver can't find the identity solution for plain nets when they get deep.

What are the ResNets actually learning? Fig.7 looks at the strength of each 3 by 3 layer's output after Batch Normalization and before the ReLU — measured as standard deviation (how spread out the numbers are). ResNets have smaller responses than plain nets. And deeper ResNets have even smaller responses per layer: ResNet-110's layers are weaker than ResNet-56's, which are weaker than ResNet-20's.

Think of it like a relay team carrying a tray of water. Plain layers each try to rebuild the whole tray carry from scratch and wobble a lot. Residual layers just say "I'm already holding it, I'll nudge it a tiny bit." That's exactly the idea from Lesson 3: if the best thing is close to doing nothing ( F(x) = H(x) - x near zero), the solver just pushes F toward zero, layer by layer.

Where 1,000+ layers hit a different limit. With n=200 the network becomes 1202 layers. It still trains: training error <0.1% in Fig.6 right — no optimization difficulty at all. But test error is 7.93%, worse than the 110-layer's 6.43%. With 19.4M parameters on a small 50k-image dataset and no dropout or maxout used on purpose, it memorizes a bit too well. That's classic overfitting: training error tiny, test error goes back up. Deeper isn't always better once the model is too big for the data.

008Lesson 8 — Beyond one dataset~4 min

One idea, five trophies — and a new foundation

A network that is great at naming 1,000 kinds of images should also be great at finding where things are. That is the idea of transfer: you train deep features once on ImageNet, then reuse those same features to do new jobs — drawing boxes around objects and saying what they are.

The paper tested this by keeping everything else the same. They took the standard detector at the time, Faster R-CNN, and only swapped its backbone — the part that turns pixels into features. In one run the backbone was VGG-16 (16 layers). In the next run it was ResNet-101 (101 layers). Same proposals, same training steps, same extra layers for boxes.

The jump was striking, and it was measured on public test sets:

  • PASCAL VOC 2007: 73.2% mAP with VGG-16 → 76.4% with ResNet-101
  • PASCAL VOC 2012: 70.4% → 73.8%
  • MS COCO (harder, 80 classes): mAP@0.5 41.5% → 48.4% (+6.9 points). And the strict COCO average mAP@[.5,.95] — average over many overlap thresholds — 21.2% → 27.2% (+6.0 points).

That COCO gain is about 28% relative: (27.2 - 21.2) / 21.2 × 100 ≈ 28%. The paper stresses it came solely from better features, not from a new detector.

Better features = better boxes. COCO also measures how tight your boxes are. The fact that the strict mAP@[.5,.95] rose by almost as much as mAP@0.5 means ResNet helped both recognition (what is it?) and localization (exactly where?).

For the competitions the team added three tricks on top — box refinement (re-score the box you just predicted), global context (look at the whole image too), and multi-scale testing (run at several sizes). Then a single ResNet-101 model reached 55.7% at 0.5 and 34.9% strict on COCO test-dev, and an ensemble of three nets reached 59.0% / 37.4%. Those numbers powered the wins:

  • ImageNet detection: 62.1% mAP with ensemble (8.5 points ahead of 2nd place)
  • ImageNet localization: 9.0% top-5 error (down from 25.3% for VGG and 26.7% for GoogLeNet)
  • COCO detection and COCO segmentation
  • Plus ImageNet classification (the 3.57% ensemble from Lesson 6)

That's five first places in ILSVRC & COCO 2015 from one idea. The paper's final lesson is about limits, too: on tiny CIFAR-10 a 1202-layer ResNet could still drive training error below 0.1%, but it did worse on the test set (7.93% vs 6.43% for 110 layers) because 19.4 million parameters overfit that small dataset when no extra regularization like dropout was used. Residuals fixed optimization of depth, not every other problem.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

neural network

A computer system made of stacked layers that learns to recognize patterns in images. Each layer transforms the input a little.

convolutional layer

A type of layer especially good for images. It looks at small 3x3 patches to find edges, textures, and shapes.

depth

How many layers a network has. More layers = deeper = can learn more complex ideas, in theory.

training error

How many mistakes the network makes on the images it is learning from. Lower is better.

validation / test error

How many mistakes on new images it has never seen before. This tests real learning vs memorizing.

overfitting

When a network memorizes training images but does badly on new ones. The paper shows degradation is NOT this.

degradation problem

The problem the paper found: adding more layers to a plain network made training error worse, even though a deeper network could just copy the shallow one.

underlying mapping

What a group of layers is trying to learn. The paper writes it as H(x), where x is input and H(x) is desired output.

residual function

The paper's trick: instead of learning H(x) directly, learn the difference F(x) = H(x) - x. Called residual because it's what's left over.

shortcut / identity connection

A wire that skips one or more layers and adds the input x directly to the output: y = F(x) + x. Also called skip connection. No extra learning needed.

identity mapping

A simple shortcut where output = input, unchanged. The network can push the other path to zero to do nothing.

projection shortcut

When input and output sizes don't match, the shortcut uses a small matrix Ws to resize: y = F(x) + Ws*x. Paper calls this projection.

activation (ReLU)

ReLU, a simple rule: keep positive numbers, turn negative numbers to zero. It lets networks learn curves, not just straight lines.

SGD (stochastic gradient descent)

Method to train networks: show small batches of images, guess, measure error, nudge weights a little to be less wrong, repeat thousands of times.

batch normalization

A trick that steadies training by keeping layer outputs in a healthy range. Let plain nets with Batch Normalization still degrade.

FLOPs

Number of multiply-and-add operations. Measures how heavy a model is. Paper's 34-layer ResNet is 3.6 billion FLOPs vs VGG-19's 19.6 billion.

ImageNet

A dataset of 1.28 million images in 1000 categories (dogs, cars, etc). The standard hard test for image recognition.

top-1 / top-5 error

Scoring: top-1 = is your #1 guess correct? top-5 = is the right answer in your top 5 guesses? Lower error % is better.

bottleneck block

A block design that saves computation: 1x1 layer to shrink channels, 3x3 layer to process, 1x1 layer to restore. Used for 50/101/152-layer nets.
