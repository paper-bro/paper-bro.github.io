001The problem~8 min

Robots that can't feel physics — yet

A modern robot can look at a kitchen counter, listen to "pick up the spoon," and reach out. Models that do this are called Vision-Language-Action models, or VLAs: vision for the camera image, language for the instruction, action for the motor commands.

That is useful, but it is not enough. Imagine you need to lift a full mug. Knowing what the mug looks like is not the same as knowing how heavy it feels, how it will tilt, or whether your grip will slip. The PDRVLA paper points to a recent analysis that found many VLA policies weakly encode physical properties like mass and do not reliably change their motion when mass changes. In other words, they see and they act, but they do not really feel physics yet.

One way to feel physics is to imagine what happens next. If you push the spoon, will it slide or tip? If you pour, where will the liquid go? Predicting the next moments — how appearance, shape, and motion evolve — gives the robot context for choosing an action.

Key trick: Video world models are good at this imagination. They learn from lots of video to predict future frames. But generating whole future videos inside the control loop is slow. Every step would have to wait for the video to be rendered — too much delay for a fast, reactive robot.

PDRVLA takes a different path. During training it learns to predict three kinds of future: RGB (what things will look like), depth (how far away each part is — geometry), and optical flow (which way each pixel is moving). It also gets help from a frozen, pretrained video world model that acts like a teacher. All of this gets squeezed into just 50 tiny vectors called physical tokens — a compact memory of the future.

At deployment, the robot does not run the video model and does not generate videos. It just uses those 50 tokens, together with normal vision-language features, to choose actions quickly. Training imagines a lot; acting stays fast.

In the lab below, try catching a falling block two ways. First, let the robot use instant tokens. Then make it generate 5 video frames before every decision — drag the latency slider higher and watch the loop frequency drop and misses increase. Look for the point where imagination helps, but waiting for imagination hurts.

002Background you need~8 min

How Vision-Language-Action works

Think of a Vision-Language-Action model — a VLA — like a robot's translator. Inputs: what its cameras see right now, plus a sentence like "pick up the spoon." Output: the next tiny moves for its hand. See, read, act — on a loop.

PDRVLA builds on a Vision-Language Model called Qwen3.5-4B. That's a large model that already understands images and words, with 32 decoder blocks (think of them as 32 floors of processing). At each step it gets several camera views — wrist camera, third-person camera — and one line of text that always starts the same way:

embodiment <robot>, arm <arm>, hand <hand>. control <rate> Hz. <instruction>
Example: embodiment panda, arm franka_panda, hand parallel gripper. control 30 Hz. pick up the spoon. That prefix tells the brain which body it's driving.

To teach that translator well, you need huge, varied examples — and the paper uses only public data. The core VLA data is:

  • 1.26M real-robot trajectories (about 14,893 hours) — real arms in real kitchens and labs.
  • 1.22M UMI trajectories (10,259 hours) — not a robot at all, but a person holding a gripper with a wrist camera. There's no robot joint data here, just where the hand-gripper was in space.
  • 1.87M simulated trajectories (8,868 hours) — in simulation you can reset the world and get perfect success labels.

Plus a lot of language-looking data to help it reason: 8.05M general image/video-language examples and 23.63M spatial & embodied examples (pointing, grounding, 3D relations). On top of that, the authors created extra derived data without collecting new robots: 0.90M human-to-robot trajectories remade from human hand videos, 1.79B frames labeled with subtasks (turned into 1.1M language examples), and 2.56M keyframes with chain-of-thought notes about scene and progress. We'll meet those in the next lesson.

Here's the catch: every robot records differently. One logs inches, another meters. One calls forward +z, another +x. One has a 7-joint arm, another has two arms plus dexterous fingers. A UMI demo has no joints at all. If you don't fix that, your model learns gibberish.

So the paper standardizes everything to one end-effector delta — how the hand's position and orientation should change:

$$\Delta a^{\mathrm{eef}}_{t} = (\Delta \mathbf{p}_{r,t},\, \Delta R_{r,t}),\quad \Delta \mathbf{p}_{r,t}=\mathbf{p}^{*}_{r,t}-\mathbf{p}_{r,t},\quad \Delta R_{r,t}=R^{*}_{re,t}R^{\mathsf{T}}_{re,t}$$

In plain words: $\mathbf{p}$ is the hand's current position, $\mathbf{p}^{*}$ is where you want it to go (both in meters, in the same reference frame $r$). $\Delta \mathbf{p}$ is just the difference — how far forward/left/up to move. $R$ is the hand's current orientation as a rotation matrix, $R^{*}$ the target orientation. $\Delta R = R^{*}R^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the twist needed to get from one orientation to the other. Everything is converted to one right-handed rule: +x forward, +y left, +z up.

That lets one model learn from pandas, WidowXs, bimanual Alohas, and even human hand demos — all speaking the same action language. Now try the lab: build the chat message yourself by picking a robot and views, then drag a target pose and watch $\Delta \mathbf{p}$ and $\Delta R$ be computed in that shared frame.

003PDRVLA core~10 min

50 tiny tokens that remember the future

Imagine you push a toy car. To catch it, you don't need a full movie of the future in your head — just a few sticky notes: "it will roll forward, turn a bit left, slow down." PDRVLA does something similar for robots.

Its "sticky notes" are called physical tokens, written $C_t$. The $t$ means "at this moment." There are exactly 50 of them, each a list of 2,560 numbers. They don't contain actions themselves — they contain a compact memory of how the scene is about to change, which the robot then uses to choose actions.

Where do 50 notes come from?

PDRVLA's main brain is a Vision-Language Model, Qwen3.5-4B — 32 Transformer blocks tall. It reads your camera views and instruction as one chat message. Most robots would only use the very last block's output, called $H_t^{\text{last}}$, to decide what to do.

PDRVLA does something clever: halfway up, at block 8, it taps the features $H_t^{(8)}$ — the 8th layer's view of the world. Why not the top? Later layers get busy turning images into words and plans, and studies show they actually suppress fine visual detail. Layer 8 still remembers shapes, edges, and motion patterns well.

A tiny extra module reads $H_t^{(8)}$. It starts with 50 learnable queries — think of them as 50 blank question cards that learn what to ask. Over 4 Transformer blocks (each with hidden width 1024, 4 attention heads, and a feed-forward layer), those cards:

  • do cross-attention to $H_t^{(8)}$ — each card looks at the image features and pulls out what it needs,
  • do self-attention to each other — the cards compare notes,
  • then get projected to width 2560.

The result is $C_t$ — your 50 physical tokens. In a simplified form, the robot's condition for acting is then

$$X_t = [H_t^{\text{last}} ;\, C_t]$$

meaning "stick the final-layer tokens and the 50 physics tokens together into one long list." That list $X_t$, plus the robot's own body sense $q_t$ (3 numbers for where its hand is and 6 numbers for how it's rotated), goes to the action expert.

Why 50? Why layer 8?
The paper tried other choices and measured how well the tokens could predict the future. Layer 8 scored 0.3617 vs only 0.2432 for the final layer — much better. For number of queries, 50 scored 0.3844, about the same as 128 (0.3815) or 256 (0.3834). So 50 was enough, and much cheaper.
Go deeper: the module adds about 57.3M parameters — only about 1.4% on top of the 4B backbone. Cross-attention in every one of the 4 blocks was slightly better than only in the first or last block. The design keeps deployment fast: no video model runs at test time, just these 50 tokens.

Try it below: pick which VLM layer the module reads and how many query cards it uses. Watch how sharply the cards attend to the image, how much "future-prediction skill" changes, and how cost grows. Look for the sweet spot the paper found.

004Data at scale~8 min

Millions of examples from public data + human hands

Collecting robot data the old way is slow: you need a real robot, a human teleoperating it, and hours of resets. The PDRVLA team asked — can we get millions of examples without buying millions of robot hours?

The answer is yes, and they did it two ways, using only public data.

🖐️

1. Borrow human hands

Human-to-robot synthesis. Take egocentric videos of people doing tasks (from EgoDex). Convert 3D hand keypoints into a robot gripper path, solve inverse kinematics — the math that turns "put hand here" into joint angles — for 12 different arms (Panda, UR5e, UR10e, FR3, Kinova Gen3, IIWA, ARX-L5, xArm7, ViperX, WidowX, Piper, YAM). That alone gave 896,832 new trajectories, about 2,646 hours, from just 74,813 human demos — each human demo becomes ~12 robot variants.

✍️

2. Auto-label what happened

Annotation. Don't record new video — add richer language to existing video. A vision-language model (Qwen3.8-27B) watches 1 frame per second in windows of up to 128 frames and writes subtask labels ("grasp spoon" → "lift spoon" → "done") and ECoT notes — short embodied chain-of-thought describing the scene, whether the step is finished, and what the hand does next.

Both steps need a clean-up studio. For the human videos: SAM3 finds and masks the human arm and hand. ProPainter inpaints the missing background — it copies nearby pixels across frames so the table looks untouched. Then the target robot arm is rendered from its joint angles and pasted in. DA3 depth estimates decide occlusion: if a cereal box is in front, it should cover the robot, not float behind it. The paper filters aggressively: too-short, misaligned, or low-confidence keypoint demos are dropped, and frames with non-finite values or spikes are excluded from loss but kept in place so images and actions stay aligned. At most 4 views are kept, capped at 30 Hz and 640 px on the longest side.

Subtasks = chapter titles, ECoT = director's commentary. Subtask annotation covers ~1.88 million episodes and 1.79 billion frames, turned into 1.1 million VL question-answer examples of 6 types (e.g., "what's the current step?", "what's next?", "is it done?" with or without history). ECoT picks keyframes — the first and last valid frame of each subtask plus frames where the image-hash changes a lot — and writes scene + progress + next motion. Next motion comes directly from the logged gripper poses 0.5 seconds ahead, turned into phrases like "moves forward, tilts down." The pipeline produced 2.56 million ECoT keyframes across AgiBot World, ABC-130K, RoboCOIN, RoboMIND, HiFi-UMI-2K and FastUMI-100K.

In short: instead of new robots, PDRVLA re-uses human video and re-describes old robot video. That is how the training corpus grew to millions of trajectories plus tens of millions of VL examples, all from public sources — and how the physical tokens in the next lesson get enough future to learn from.

Slide depth to see DA3 occlusion — does the box correctly cover the robot? Notice which frames get filtered for bad keypoints.

005Training step 1~10 min

Looking ahead in RGB, depth and flow

A robot about to stack a block needs three guesses at once: what will it look like, how far everything will be, and which way things will move. PDRVLA teaches its 50 physical tokens to predict exactly those three futures, without ever drawing a video at test time.

For each training example the paper takes a short 5-frame clip that starts now and spans the next 30 action steps. It encodes the clip with a frozen video VAE — the Wan VAE from LingBot-Video — into compact latent codes instead of raw pixels. That gives three targets $z_{future}^{m}$ for $m$ in $\{r,d,f\}$: $r$ = RGB appearance (color image), $d$ = depth geometry, $f$ = optical flow motion. Depth comes from a depth estimator called DA3, clipped and turned to grayscale; flow comes from an estimator called DPFlow, color-encoded so direction becomes color. RGB tells "is the cup still there?", depth tells "how tall is the stack?", flow tells "which way is the hand sliding?"

The head that predicts those futures is tiny on purpose. First it pools the VAE latents to a coarser grid:

$$ y^{m} = \text{Pool}(z_{future}^{m}) $$

so the head only has to output a rough layout, not full resolution. Then comes standardization, Eq. 4 in the paper. For each modality, channel and spatial position the paper subtracts the average $\mu^{m}$ and divides by the spread $s^{m}$:

$$ \tilde{y}^{m} = \frac{y^{m} - \mu^{m}}{s^{m}} $$

Here $y^{m}$ is the pooled target, $\mu^{m}$ is its mean over training examples, and $s^{m}$ is its standard deviation (with a floor so quiet features aren't blown up). Without this, flow numbers are naturally much bigger than depth numbers and would hog the learning. With it, all three speak at the same volume.

The paper's prediction $g_{\eta}^{m}(C_t)$ is made from the 50 physical tokens $C_t$ with LayerNorm + a linear layer + attention-pooling with three separate readouts. The loss is the average mismatch over the three worlds (Eq. 5):
$$ \mathcal{L}_{direct} = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{m \in \{r,d,f\}} \text{MSE}\left(g_{\eta}^{m}(C_t), \tilde{y}^{m}\right) $$
MSE means "mean squared error" — square each difference between prediction and $\tilde{y}^{m}$ and average. Lower means the tokens imagined better.

During this first stage the VLM backbone and the big video model stay frozen. Only the Physical Dynamics Module and its small prediction heads learn. The full pretraining loss is $\mathcal{L}_{pre}=\mathcal{L}_{WM}+\lambda(k)\mathcal{L}_{direct}$ where $\lambda(k)=0.5\cdot \min(k/2000,1)$ warms up from 0 to 0.5 over the first 2,000 updates. Early on the module learns mostly from the world-model teacher (next lesson); the direct loss is faded in gradually so its scale doesn't swamp early learning. The tiny detached path labeled "sg" means gradients from this loss update the module but don't flow back into the VLM.

In the lab below, play teacher: move the warm-up slider to see $\lambda(k)$ ramp up, coarsen the pooling to see the trade-off between detail and head size, and flip standardization on and off to watch RGB, depth and flow MSEs fall onto the same scale.

006Training step 1b~9 min

Learning faster with a frozen teacher

Imagine learning to cook by watching a chef who never comes to your kitchen — they just left notes while you practiced. At test time you cook alone, but the notes made you faster. That's the trick in this lesson.

The "chef" is a big pretrained video model called LingBot-Video-Dense-1.3B. It already knows how videos evolve. PDRVLA keeps it completely frozen — its weights never change — and only uses it during training to coach a tiny module that makes 50 physical tokens. When the robot actually runs, the video model is not loaded at all, so there's no extra video-generation lag.

The coaching signal in plain words: give the frozen teacher a clean present and a noisy future, plus your 50 tokens as a hint. If the tokens are good hints, the teacher can denoise the future well. We train the tokens to be good hints.

What the teacher sees

For each example the team encodes a 5-frame clip (current + next 30 steps) into compact latent codes with a frozen Wan video VAE. Call the clean current latent $z^r_{\text{current}}$ and the clean future latent $z^r_{\text{future}}$ for the RGB video. During coaching they mix the future with random noise:

$$z^{r}_{\sigma,\text{future}} = (1-\sigma)\,z^{r}_{\text{future}} + \sigma\,\epsilon, \quad \sigma \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),\; \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$$

Here $\sigma$ is just a number between 0 and 1 that says how noisy to make the future (0 = clean, 1 = pure noise), and $\epsilon$ is random Gaussian noise. The current part stays clean: $z^{r}_{\sigma,\text{current}} = z^{r}_{\text{current}}$. The teacher gets this noisy clip plus your physical tokens $C_t$ and has to predict how to undo the noise.

Its training target is a velocity — literally noise minus clean future. For the three kinds of future we care about — $m=r$ for RGB (appearance), $m=d$ for depth (shape), $m=f$ for optical flow (motion) — the auxiliary loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{WM}} = \sum_{m\in\{r,d,f\}} \text{MSE}\left(\hat{v}^m_{\text{future}},\; \epsilon - z^m_{\text{future}}\right)$$

$\hat{v}^m_{\text{future}}$ is what the teacher's heads predict, and $\epsilon - z^m_{\text{future}}$ is the true velocity for a straight line from clean to noise. The same $\epsilon$ is used for RGB, depth and flow, so the three heads agree. Gradients from this loss flow through the frozen teacher backbone back to your physical-token module (and its new depth/flow heads), but the teacher itself and its RGB head are never updated.

The full pretraining objective adds this to the direct future-prediction loss you saw last lesson:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{pre}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{WM}} + \lambda(k)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{direct}}, \quad \lambda(k)=0.5\,\min(k/2000,1)$$

$k$ is the training step. $\lambda(k)$ gently ramps the direct loss from 0 to 0.5 over the first 2,000 steps — a warm-up so the module first learns from the teacher, then adds its own direct RGB/depth/flow prediction.

Did the coach actually help?

The paper ran a fair comparison: same Qwen3.5-4B backbone, same budget of 35,082 updates and about 4.49 million examples, same direct loss schedule — one run with the frozen teacher, one without. They watched raw training error before weighting, so we see the real prediction quality.

Early in training, RGB and depth errors fall noticeably faster with the teacher (the solid vs dashed gap in the figure insets for steps 1,000–6,000). Flow improves much less, and later in training the gaps shrink — the teacher speeds up learning, it doesn't change the final ceiling by a lot.

The honest test is on held-out data the model never trained on: 828 RoboCOIN clips from 100 fresh episodes, scored with the original direct heads. Mean error across the three types goes from 0.6439 to 0.6354:

  • Mean: −1.32% with a 95% interval [0.71, 1.89] — a small but reliable win
  • RGB: −4.06% [3.04, 4.99] — clear
  • Depth: −2.12% [0.93, 3.31] — clear
  • Flow: −0.62% [−0.07, 1.28] — the interval includes zero, so we can't be confident it helped motion prediction

The intervals come from paired episode-bootstrap — they tell us how much the result would wobble if we picked different test episodes. When an interval crosses zero, like flow's, it means "might be zero, might be slightly positive."

Go a bit deeper — the same story shows up in a probe test (Appendix B). With the VLM and physical module frozen, a simple linear probe tries to predict future latents. No direct supervision scores 0.2683; adding direct supervision warmed to 0.5 over 2,000 steps scores 0.3834, the best of the tested mixtures. That probe is why the team kept the warm-up you saw above.

In short: a frozen video brain can coach compact tokens to imagine appearance and shape faster, without ever paying for video generation at deployment. Next, try the coach yourself — flip the teacher on and off, scrub the noise level $\sigma$, and watch the curves and held-out bars move.

007Training step 2~10 min

Turning imagination into motion

Imagination is useless if it never becomes motion. PDRVLA’s second training stage plugs its imagination straight into its hands — without ever rendering a video at test time.

Remember the two streams from the backbone: final-layer features $H_t^{\text{last}}$ (good at instructions and overall scene meaning) and the 50 physical tokens $C_t$ from layer 8 (good at what will change). For action, PDRVLA simply glues them together along the token dimension:

$$X_t = [H_t^{\text{last}};\, C_t]$$

In plain words, $X_t$ is one long list: first all the meaning tokens, then the 50 future-predicting tokens. The action expert — a Diffusion Transformer with 16 blocks and about 150M parameters — looks at $X_t$ through cross-attention while it also looks at itself. It also gets one extra token for the robot’s current body state $q_t$ (3 numbers for hand position plus 6 numbers for rotation).

How does it learn to move? Not by copying positions directly, but by learning a velocity field. Think of a smooth cartoon morph from TV static to a graceful arm movement.

Flow matching in one sentence: start from pure noise $\xi$, blend it with the real 8-step chunk $\mathbf{A}_t$, and teach the network to predict the straight-line velocity that would carry the blend toward the real motion.

The paper’s formulas make this precise. Pick a flow time $\tau$ between 0 (all noise) and 1 (all real):

$$x_{\tau} = (1-\tau)\xi + \tau \mathbf{A}_t,\quad v^* = \mathbf{A}_t - \xi$$

$x_{\tau}$ is the blended noisy chunk you show the network; $v^*$ is the true direction to the target. The $\tau$ itself isn’t uniform — it’s drawn from a $\text{Beta}(1.5,1)$ distribution and mapped through $\tau = 1-\min(b,s)/s$ with $s=0.999$, so the network sees many different blend levels. Each training example is repeated 8 times with different $\tau$ and noise.

Robots come in many shapes, so PDRVLA uses one fixed 58-number action vector: for each arm (left and right) 7 arm joints + 9 end-effector pose slots (only 6 used, 3 padded) + 12 dexterous-hand joints + 1 gripper. That’s 29 per arm, 58 total. If your robot has no dexterous hand, those numbers are just ignored in the loss. End-effector moves are always relative to where the hand was at the start of the chunk: $\mathbf{a}^{\text{eef}}_{t,k}=[\mathbf{p}^*_{t+k}-\mathbf{p}_t,\,\text{Log}(R^*_{t+k}R_t^{\mathsf{T}})]$ — a 3-number shift plus a 3-number twist.

Not every frame is trustworthy. PDRVLA builds a mask $M_i$ — a grid with one row per chunk step and one column per action number — that is 1 only when that step is valid and that joint exists on this robot. The action loss then averages only the allowed elements:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{act}} = \frac{\sum_i \|M_i \odot (\hat{v}_i - v_i^*)\|_F^2}{\sum_i \|M_i\|_1 + 10^{-6}}$$

$\hat{v}_i$ is what the network guessed, $\odot$ means multiply entry-by-entry with the mask, and the denominator counts how many 1s are in all masks.

The full policy loss is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{policy}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{act}} + 0.1\mathcal{L}_{\text{VL}} + 0.1\mathcal{L}_{\text{direct}}$. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{VL}}$ is next-token cross-entropy on language answers and only updates the VLM. $\mathcal{L}_{\text{direct}}$ is the same RGB/depth/flow future loss from the last lesson, kept at weight 0.1. A detached wire stops action and direct gradients from flowing back into the VLM through the layer-8 path — the VLM learns about actions only via $H_t^{\text{last}}$.

At deployment there’s no video model, no direct heads. The expert starts from Gaussian noise and takes 4 Euler steps of size 0.25 from flow time 0 to 1, producing $K=8$ commands that are executed before replanning. Training frames are sampled at about 8 Hz plus any frame where the gripper or hand command changes, with at least 0.01 s between kept frames.

008Results~9 min

State-of-the-art on four simulators — and what's still hard

After learning to imagine the future as 50 tokens, how good is PDRVLA in practice? The paper tests one trained brain on four simulation benchmarks that look like tabletop chores. The headline is simple: PDRVLA gets the highest average score in each benchmark, but not the highest score on every single task inside them.

Four numbers to remember, exactly as reported: LIBERO 99.3% average over 2,000 trials, LIBERO-Plus 89.9% over 10,030 trials with perturbations, RoboTwin 2.0 93.6% average (93.9% clean, 93.3% randomized), and SimplerEnv 81.5% average on WidowX visual-matching. In each case that beats the listed baselines like $π0$, $π0.5$, InternVLA-A1.5 and Qwen-RobotManip, but only by a few tenths to a couple points.

Inside those averages are trade-offs. On LIBERO, PDRVLA is perfect on Spatial (100%) and near-perfect on Object and Goal (99.8% each), yet on the hardest Long-horizon suite it gets 97.6%, a bit below LingBot-VA's 98.5%. On SimplerEnv its best task is Eggplant $→$ Basket at 97.6%, but Carrot $→$ Plate is only 64.6% while InternVLA-A1.5 gets 85.4% there. Averages hide the hard spots.

Small gains that depend on the test: On LIBERO-Plus, adding self-generated subtask text like Current step: grasp spoon lifts overall success from 89.93% to 90.23% — just +0.30. It helps most when the robot starts in a new place (+1.16) or the camera moves (+1.00), but hurts slightly under new lighting, language, or background. Adding object-pose prediction during training gives +0.19 overall (89.93% → 90.12%) with +1.38 on camera changes but drops in four other categories.

How often should the robot rewrite that subtask hint? On eight RoboCasa tasks, refreshing every chunk (every step) gives 32.25% and every 8 chunks gives almost the same, 32.13%, both well above the 26.25% baseline without subtasks. Waiting 32 chunks collapses to 20.63%. Between refreshes the prediction mostly repeats anyway — only 11.6% of consecutive predictions differ when refreshing every chunk.

And what about asking the model to explain itself with ECoT (scene + progress + next motion)? Training with ECoT helps a lot on long tasks even if you never generate it at test time: LIBERO Long jumps 92.8% → 96.0% and average 97.1% → 98.2%; LIBERO-Plus Long jumps 65.18% → 69.87%. Turning generation on at test time adds no consistent extra benefit (98.3% LIBERO average but Long down again). The paper's future work points beyond RGB, depth and flow to object-centric geometry, contact and materials — the parts of physics it does not yet imagine well.

A note on "including zero": For the world-model help earlier, the flow improvement was -0.62% with a 95% interval [-0.07, 1.28]. Because that interval crosses zero, we can't be confident flow really improved — unlike RGB and depth, where intervals stay above zero.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Vision-Language-Action (VLA)

A model that takes a camera image + a language instruction ("pick up the spoon") and outputs robot actions. It reuses a Vision-Language Model (VLM) that already understands images and words.

Physical Dynamics Module

A small added network (about 57.3M parameters, 1.4% of the 4B backbone) that reads intermediate features from the VLM and produces 50 special vectors called physical tokens.

physical tokens

50 vectors of width 2560 created by learnable queries. They are trained to summarize future appearance, geometry and motion, and are given to the action expert without ever generating a video at test time.

learnable queries

Learnable starting vectors that "ask" for information via cross-attention. PDRVLA uses 50 of them across 4 Transformer blocks to pull dynamics info from VLM layer 8.

video world model

A pretrained video generator (here LingBot-Video-Dense-1.3B with a Wan VAE) that can predict future frames. PDRVLA keeps it frozen and uses it only during training as an auxiliary teacher; it is not run at deployment.

RGB / depth / optical flow

Color image (what things look like), depth image (how far each pixel is, encoded to grayscale via DA3), and optical flow image (which way each pixel moves, color-encoded via DPFlow). PDRVLA predicts all three as latent features.

latent features

Compact code produced by a video VAE (variational autoencoder) instead of raw pixels. PDRVLA predicts these latents, pooled to a coarse spatial layout, after per-channel standardization.

direct future prediction

The future part of a 5-frame clip (sampled from current time + next 30 steps) after pooling and standardization: y~ = (y - mu)/s. Loss = MSE(g(Ct), y~) averaged over the 3 modalities.

conditional flow matching

Way to train the action generator: start from Gaussian noise ξ, interpolate x_tau = (1-tau)ξ + tau*A, target velocity v* = A - ξ, and learn to predict v*. At inference 4 Euler steps turn noise into an 8-step action chunk.

Diffusion Transformer (DiT) action expert

The action expert network: 16 blocks, alternating self-attention on action tokens and cross-attention to [H_last ; C_t], 12 heads width 64, ~150M params. Input = noisy chunk + proprioceptive token + conditioning sequence Xt.

end-effector delta action

End-effector action as translation delta + rotation delta: Delta_p = p* - p, Delta_R = R* R^T, and for Cartesian chunks a^eef = [p*_{t+k}-p_t , Log(R*_{t+k} R_t^T)] in R6. Plus absolute gripper/hand joints in a fixed 58-D vector (7+9+12+1 per arm x2).

subtask conditioning

Short phrase describing the current stage, e.g., "Current step: grasp spoon". PDRVLA can generate it from observation+instruction and append it to the instruction to improve robustness.

ECoT annotation

Embodied Chain-of-Thought: training annotations that describe scene, whether subtask is complete, and next motion ("moves forward, tilts down"). Used to train the VLM; PDRVLA finds training helps but generating it at inference adds no consistent gain.

human-to-robot synthesis

Converting human egocentric hand data (EgoDex 3D hand keypoints) to robot trajectories via IK, then inpainting human arms out (SAM3 + ProPainter) and rendering 12 robot arms with DA3 depth occlusion. Yielded 0.90M trajectories / 2,646 hours.

world-model-assisted supervision (result)

Ablation holding training budget equal (35,082 updates, 4.49M exposures) comparing Direct only vs Direct+WM. Mean normalized MSE 0.6439 -> 0.6354 (-1.32%). RGB -4.06%, depth -2.12%, flow -0.62%.
