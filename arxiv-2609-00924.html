001Lesson 1 — The hook~5 min

Why tracking from a flat video is so confusing

Imagine you take a photo of two friends dancing, one right in front of you and one further back. In real life they are metres apart. In the photo? They can land on the exact same pixels.

That is what a normal video camera does. We live and move in 3D — left-right, up-down, and near-far (depth). A single camera squashes all of that onto a flat image plane, like pressing a shadow onto a wall. The depth — who is in front — is simply lost.

The paper calls this beyond the image plane for a reason. Trackers today mostly look only at that flat picture: what something looks like and where it is in 2D, maybe how its 2D position moves. When depth is hidden:

  • Two different people can overlap in the picture and look like one.
  • The same real motion — say, walking straight away — can look tiny in 2D if far away, huge if nearby.
  • When someone goes behind someone else, the picture loses them completely (occlusion), and the camera's own shake adds more confusion.
Key insight: the problem is not sloppy code. A flat projection throws away information. If two dancers project to the same spot, no amount of staring at the flat pixels can tell for sure who is who — unless you somehow bring depth back.

And this hits everyone. The older style, tracking-by-detection (find boxes first, then link them with hand-made rules), and the newer end-to-end style that learns detection and linking together, both inherit the same 2D ambiguity if they learn only from flat cues. They are guessing in a world they cannot fully see.

Planet's whole idea is to give the tracker back a sense of depth — not from a fancy depth sensor, but by estimating it from the flat video itself — so it can reason in the world, not just on the photo.

Try it yourself: use the lab to put two dancers at different depths but line up their flat projections. Watch the camera view lie while the top-down view tells the truth.

002Lesson 2 — Background you need~7 min

From detect-then-link to learn-to-track together

Most trackers work in two separate steps. First they detect: “there is a person here, and here, and here.” Then they link: “the box in this frame is the same person as that box in the next frame.” That is called tracking-by-detection.

Linking usually uses hand-made rules: match the most similar shirt color, the closest 2-D position, or the biggest box overlap. It can work, but there is a seam between the two steps. The detector does not know what the linker will need, and the linker cannot fix what the detector missed. Both steps look only at how things appear on the flat image.

Recent trackers try a different deal: learn detection and linking together with the same building block. Planet builds on one called MOTIP, which uses a Transformer detector named Deformable DETR.

Think of an object query as a question slot that asks “is there something here?” For each frame It, the detector makes a set of query vectors:

$$\mathcal{Q}_t = \{\mathbf{q}_t^i\}_{i=1}^{N_t},\;\; \mathbf{q}_t^i \in \mathbb{R}^d$$

Each q is just a list of d numbers the network learned. From that same q, two tiny heads predict two things at once: a 2-D box b̂it ∈ ℝ4 (center x, y, width, height) and an identity label ŷit ∈ Y (“this is person #7”). Predictions with the same identity across frames become one tracklet — one continuous trail.

The important idea: detection and identity share the query. If you make q richer, both tasks get better. That is the hook Planet will use to sneak 3-D information in later.

Why history length matters. A Transformer ID predictor can look back at past queries to re-find someone after an occlusion. But keeping that history is expensive — every extra frame costs memory and spreads attention thinner. So many recent end-to-end trackers train on only 2 to 10 frames of context. With such short memory, evidence from far back gets diluted or dropped, and a dancer who disappears for a second is forgotten. Planet will need to fix this too.
How we keep score — in plain words. Three numbers you will see later:
HOTA is the main overall score. At each box-overlap threshold it takes the geometric mean (multiply then square-root) of detection accuracy DetA (did we find the boxes?) and association accuracy AssA (did we link them to the right IDs?), then averages over thresholds. Higher is better.
IDF1 cares about identity keeping: after matching predicted trails to true trails one-to-one, it is the harmonic mean of identification precision and recall.
MOTA counts detection mistakes: missed boxes + false boxes + ID switches, divided by the number of true boxes, then subtracted from 1. It punishes detection errors most.

So we have the pieces: two-stage trackers that split detect and link, and end-to-end query trackers that unify them — but with a short fuse of memory.

The other adds a simple motion guess — where should each ID be given its recent velocity? Watch when IDs flip, and how much harder a short memory makes re-identification.

003Lesson 3 — Paper's step 1~6 min

Turning old 2D videos into 3D-enriched worlds

To teach a tracker about depth, you first need depth to learn from. But the big tracking datasets — DanceTrack, SportsMOT, and BFT — were made from ordinary single-camera video. They have only flat 2D boxes and IDs. No depth sensor, no 3D boxes.

Planet's first step is to give those old datasets a 3D upgrade. It uses a recent kind of feed-forward geometry model — Depth Anything 3, with its long-video cousin called DA3-Streaming — that can guess dense 3D structure straight from the images, without a slow optimization.

Think of it like this: for every single pixel in every frame, the model tries to answer "where would this pixel be in the real world?" That answer for one pixel is three numbers $(x, y, z)$ in world coordinates — like an address in 3D space. Do that for all pixels and you get a dense point map $P$. Planet calls $P$ the reconstructed scene geometry.

How does $P$ get made? DA3-Streaming slides overlapping windows over the long video, like laying transparent sheets that overlap. Each window guesses depth, camera intrinsics (how the lens bends light) and camera pose (where the camera was). Overlapping lets it glue windows into one single global coordinate system, and a trick called loop closure fixes drift that builds up over time. Then it back-projects: aligned depth + intrinsics + poses become the world-coordinate point map $P$. Planet recovers camera parameters too, but does not use them directly for tracking — it keeps $P$.

A point map alone is not enough. A tracker also needs to know which 3D point belongs to which person or bird. So Planet adds a second kind of label: a pseudo-3D object center for each 2D box.

For a fully visible box, the rule is simple: take all the 3D points inside that 2D box and average them. That average is the estimated center — not the size, not the orientation, just $(x,y,z)$ of the middle. Planet did this for three datasets: DanceTrack with 100 group-dance videos, SportsMOT with 240 sequences and more than 150,000 frames and 1.6 million boxes, and BFT with 106 clips of 22 bird species in 14 scene types.

Occlusion makes this tricky. If one dancer stands in front of another, the box of the person behind is filled mostly with the person in front. Averaging would put the center in the wrong place. Planet is conservative: for possibly occluded boxes it checks temporal depth (what was behind there before?), current-frame depth, and image-plane position to find the true foreground. It also looks along the identity's trajectory history to keep things consistent over time. If the estimate is still uncertain, Planet marks it invalid and simply skips 3D supervision for that box. No guess is better than a bad guess.

So after lifting, each dataset has dense scene geometry $P$ plus, where safe, a 3D center per box. That's the new training signal for the next two lessons. To feel how it works, try the lab: paint depth onto pixels, lift them into a point cloud, and watch when averaging gives a good center — and when Planet wisely throws it away.

004Lesson 4 — Paper's step 2a~6 min

World-Grounded Queries, Part 1: Decorating features with geometry

Planet already has the 3D world in hand: a dense point map P where every pixel knows its world-coordinate (x,y,z). Lesson 3 made those maps. Now the question is: how do you actually let a 2D tracker feel that depth?

You could just guess 3D later, after the tracker has already decided what it sees. Planet does the opposite. It decorates what the tracker sees before it decides. The team calls this geometric query lifting — lifting the queries beyond the image plane during their formation, not as an afterthought.

It works in two parallel moves on P. Think of P as a bumpy 3D blanket laid over the image.

🎨

What it looks like

F3D = LF(P)
A small learnable head LF reads P and makes geometric feature embeddings F3D — "what the local 3D shape looks like here".

📍

Where it is

E3D = LP(P)
A second head LP reads the same P and makes geometric position embeddings E3D — "where in 3D this pixel lives".

Next, Planet fuses those into the tracker's normal 2D ingredients. For appearance features:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{F}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{2D}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}$$

That plus sign matters. F2D comes from a ResNet-50 backbone pretrained on COCO to recognize objects in flat images. If you replaced it, you'd throw that learning away. Adding F3D as a residual keeps all that pretraining and just layers 3D shape on top.

For position, the fusion is a little richer:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \mathcal{M}([\mathbf{E}_{\text{2D}} \,\|\, \mathbf{E}_{\text{3D}}])$$

E2D is the standard sine-cosine code for "where in the 2D picture". [ \| ] means stick the 2D and 3D codes together side-by-side, and M is a learned mixer that learns how to blend them into a new, 3D-aware position code E-tilde.

Deformable attention — the engine that updates each object query — now runs on F-tilde and E-tilde instead of the flat versions. Every query literally carries dense scene-level geometry before it ever predicts a box or an identity. This is why the paper says lifting "conditions query formation," not just post-processing.

This is still scene-level help: the whole blanket of geometry, not yet "that dancer's exact 3D center." The object-level part comes in Lesson 5. But even this blanket alone helps both jobs: Base 63.0 HOTA / 53.2 AssA / 75.0 DetA becomes 64.0 / 54.3 / 75.9 with lifting alone — a +1.0 HOTA jump where both detection and association improve.

005Lesson 5 — Paper's step 2b~7 min

World-Grounded Queries, Part 2: Learning to point in 3D

Lesson 4 decorated the tracker's eyes with scene geometry — every query could now see depth. But seeing depth isn't the same as remembering where each person is in 3D. This lesson makes the queries learn to point.

In Deformable DETR, each query has a tiny anchor it looks around — a learned 2D reference point, call it $r_{2D}$. Think of it like a finger pointing on the photo. Across decoder layers that finger is nudged until it lands on a 2D box. Because the whole backbone was pretrained on COCO photos, $r_{2D}$ already knows a lot about where objects tend to be.

To also predict a 3D position, the obvious idea is to give each query a second, brand-new finger that learns to point in 3D from scratch, with its own prediction head. The paper calls this Direct 3D Localization (D3DL). It sounds clean, but it throws away all that 2D pretraining. In Table 3, with the same 3D teaching signal, D3DL actually hurts: HOTA falls from 64.0 to 63.8, and every metric slips a little.

Planet's fix is Anchored 3D Localization (A3DL): lift first, then nudge. It takes the existing $r_{2D}$ and lifts it into 3D using the dense point map $P$ to get $r_{3D}$. A tiny extra head then reads the query $q$ and predicts only a small correction $\Delta$, so the final 3D center is $$\hat{c}_{3D} = r_{3D} + \Delta(q)$$ It's anchor-relative — building on what the detector already knows instead of starting over.

How does that correction get learned? After the usual detector matching, each matched query that has a valid pseudo-3D label gets extra supervision: an $L_1$ loss $\mathcal{L}_{\ell_1}^{3D} = |\hat{c}_{3D} - c_{3D}^{pseudo}|$ added on top of the normal detection and ID losses. Remember from Lesson 3: if a box was too occluded and its pseudo-center is uncertain, Planet simply marks it invalid and skips this loss — no noisy signal. This auxiliary task doesn't replace detection; it shapes the same shared queries so they have to keep object-level 3D position inside them.

The payoff is in what improves. From Base 63.0 HOTA / 53.2 AssA / 75.0 DetA, adding geometric lifting alone reached 64.0 / 54.3 / 75.9 (Lesson 4). Adding anchored localization on top pushes to 64.4 HOTA and 54.9 AssA in Table 1 — a further +0.4 HOTA, but with a bigger jump in association (AssA) than detection (DetA). In other words, learning to point in 3D helps the tracker keep identities through crossovers and occlusions, not just find boxes. And versus the naive direct version, anchored wins by +0.6 HOTA (64.4 vs 63.8).

Try it below: place a 2D anchor, then switch between building a fresh 3D point from nothing and nudging a lifted anchor. Watch how the $L_1$ error shrinks with the anchored residual, and see when an occluded label is correctly ignored. That's the object-level half of World-Grounded Queries.

006Lesson 6 — Paper's step 3~6 min

Keeping 3D Evidence Alive Over Time

Remember that MOTIP-style tracker from Lesson 2? Its brain has a waiting room with a fixed number of seats — call that budget M. It can only think about M past observations at once, because looking at more costs too much compute and drowns attention in old, repetitive frames. In practice MOTIP's horizon was about 30 frames. If a dancer disappears behind someone for 40 frames and comes back on frame 41, the evidence is already gone — the tracker has to guess.

Planet fixes this without buying more seats. It splits the same waiting room in two. Think of it as dense now, sparse long ago:

$$M = \underbrace{M_{dense}}_{\text{recent, every frame}} + \underbrace{M_{sparse}}_{\text{older, every }S\text{-th frame}}$$

About half the seats — roughly M/2 — keep the most recent consecutive frames. That dense short-term memory covers brief occlusions, missed detections, and fast motion where every frame matters. The other half keeps older frames sampled with a stride S — every 2nd, 6th, or 12th frame. Same cost, much longer reach.

The expand trick: If you keep 15 recent frames + 15 older frames every 6th frame, your horizon is $15 + 15\times 6 = 105$ frames, not 30. With the settings in the paper, Planet stretches from ~30 to ~200 frames under the same budget. It does not store raw video — it stores the tracker's final world-grounded query embeddings, so the 3D evidence travels with you.

Training also learns to handle this. Planet varies the sampling rate during training, so the ID decoder gets used to both tight gaps and far-apart reappearings. At test time no extra training is needed — just this smarter allocation.

What does it actually improve? Table 1 in the paper adds this dual-resolution temporal memory (DRTM) on top of the world-grounded queries: HOTA goes 64.4 → 65.6 (+1.2), AssA 54.9 → 56.9, IDF1 67.8 → 70.3, while DetA stays flat at 76.1. That pattern tells you something important: detection didn't change — the tracker didn't find more boxes. Association did. It kept the right who is who over longer gaps.

And 3D makes time even more powerful. Table 2 shows the same memory added to a 2D-only tracker gives +0.8 HOTA / +1.5 IDF1, but added to a 3D-aware tracker gives +1.2 HOTA / +2.5 IDF1 and even a small MOTA bump. Why? Depth is stable when appearance isn't — two dancers in matching costumes look the same in 2D but sit at different depths. If you can remember that depth for 100+ frames, a long-occluded return becomes obvious.

Try it below: fix your budget M and grow the stride S to see your horizon stretch. Then drag the occlusion gap longer and watch when a 30-frame MOTIP-style memory loses the ID, but Planet's dense+sparse memory still reaches it. Notice how the gain concentrates in association, not detection — exactly what the paper found.

007Lesson 7 — So what?~6 min

From lab to leaderboards: Planet's results

So does all that 3D work actually translate into wins on real videos? Planet was tested on three very different playgrounds: DanceTrack (100 group-dance videos where everyone looks alike), SportsMOT (240 sports sequences, over 150,000 frames and 1.6 million boxes, with fast sprints), and BFT (106 clips of dense bird flocks from 22 species). Same strong but ordinary detector for all: Deformable DETR with a ResNet-50 backbone, started from COCO pretraining, trained with AdamW.

Here is the important rule the authors set for themselves: they trained only on each dataset, with no extra help from outside data. Many competitors add extra training by turning still images from CrowdHuman into fake videos. Planet skipped that, so its numbers are not boosted by more data. It also runs about as fast as the baseline it builds on, MOTIP — about 12 FPS in FP32 and 20 FPS in FP16. In other words, the win is from design, not from more data or a bigger model.

Read a leaderboard like this: HOTA is the overall score that mixes detection (DetA — did you find the box?) and association (AssA — did you keep the right ID?). IDF1 cares most about keeping IDs consistent over time. MOTA counts misses, false alarms, and ID switches — it rewards good detection. Planet's gains sit mostly in AssA and IDF1.

Start with the small, clean lab test the paper uses to measure each piece. Base model: 63.0 HOTA. Add geometric query lifting (mixing 3D geometry into features and positions): 64.0 — +1.0, and both AssA and DetA rise. Add anchored 3D localization (teach queries to predict their 3D centre with a small correction around a lifted 2D anchor): 64.4 — another +0.4, but AssA jumps more than DetA, so it really helps re-connect identities after occlusion. Add dual-resolution memory (same budget M, about half dense recent frames and half sparse older ones with stride S, stretching from ~30 to ~200 frames): 65.6 — +1.2 more, while DetA stays flat at 76.1. The message: longer memory did not find more boxes, it kept IDs alive.

The opposite designs show why the details matter. Simply injecting raw 3D foundation features without Planet's structure only moved HOTA 63.0 → 63.1. And learning 3D from scratch (direct 3D prediction, D3DL) actually hurt, 64.0 → 63.8, while anchored A3DL beat it by 0.6 points to 64.4. Memory also multiplied 3D's value: the same memory trick gave +0.8 HOTA to a 2D-only model but +1.2 HOTA and +2.5 IDF1 to the 3D-aware model.

On the final public test sets, Planet is state-of-art everywhere, still without external data:

  • DanceTrack test: Planet 72.1 HOTA vs MOTIP 69.6 (+2.5), AssA 63.9 (60.4), DetA 81.4, IDF1 78.0 (74.7), MOTA 91.6. That beats the best tracking-by-detection scores in the table like Hybrid-SORT 62.2 and DiffMOT 62.3, and end-to-end rivals like CO-MOT 65.3.
  • SportsMOT test: Planet 73.7 HOTA vs 72.6 (+1.1), DetA 86.0 (83.5), IDF1 78.7, MOTA 95.2.
  • BFT test: Planet 72.6 HOTA vs 70.5 (+2.1), AssA 73.9, IDF1 84.6, MOTA 80.4 — even in dense flocks with rapid deformation.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Monocular video

A video from one camera with one viewpoint. No stereo depth sensor. The tracker must guess depth from flat images.

2D projection / Image plane

Squashing 3D world points onto a 2D picture, like a shadow on a wall. Depth is lost, and distant and close objects can land on the same pixel.

Object query

A learnable slot in a Transformer detector (like DETR) that asks 'is there an object here?' Each query predicts a box and, in MOTIP/Planet, an identity.

Point map

A dense 3D grid recovered from video where every pixel gets a 3D world coordinate (x,y,z). Planet gets this from Depth Anything 3 and DA3-Streaming.

Positional encoding

Numbers added to features to tell the Transformer where something is. 2D version uses image x,y. Planet also adds 3D geometry: E3D = L_P(P) and fuses to E-tilde = M([E2D || E3D]).

Reference point (anchored)

Predicted 2D point inside a query that guides where to look. Planet lifts the pretrained r_2D into r_3D and then predicts a small 3D correction to get the object's 3D center.

Temporal memory / Context budget

The set of past query embeddings the ID predictor can attend to. Planet's budget is M slots: ~M/2 dense recent frames + ~M/2 sparse older frames with stride S, stretching horizon from 30 to ~200 frames.

HOTA

Main tracking score. At each threshold, HOTA is the geometric mean of detection accuracy (DetA) and association accuracy (AssA), then averaged over thresholds. Higher is better.

IDF1

Emphasizes keeping identities consistent over time. Harmonic mean of identification precision and recall after matching predicted and true IDs.

MOTA

1 minus errors over truth: counts missed detections, false alarms, and ID switches, divided by number of true boxes. More about detection coverage.
