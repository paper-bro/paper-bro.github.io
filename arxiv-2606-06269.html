A Wasp and an Albatross Fly Differently. Or Do They?

A mosquito and a wandering albatross both fly, but they seem to live in different universes. The mosquito's wings are about 5 millimeters across and beat roughly 600 times a second. The albatross spans about 3 meters and flaps only a few times a second. How can one piece of physics describe both?

This paper tries exactly that. The authors collected published measurements for over 700 flights from 423 species — birds, bats, and insects in 25 orders — and put them on the same picture. The surprise is the range they cover:

📏

Wingspan b

from 250 micrometers — thinner than a hair — to 1.4 meters. That is more than a 5,000-fold difference, about 4 orders of magnitude.

🔄

Flapping frequency f

from 0.5 Hz (one flap every 2 seconds) to 850 Hz. More than a 1,000-fold difference, about 3 orders of magnitude.

Figure 1 shows this zoo: each bar is how wide b and how fast f can be for a group. A wasp and an albatross are not outliers — the whole kingdom stretches across those bars.

So why might one rule still work? When any wing flaps, it makes a swirl on its front edge called a leading-edge vortex. That swirl helps create lift, and biologists have found it in everything from fruit flies to pigeons. Oddly, how fast that swirl forms does not seem to depend much on the Reynolds number — a number written Re = U b / ν that normally tells you if air flow is smooth or messy. U is flight speed and ν (nu) is how sticky the air is. For flight, the leading-edge vortex looks universal.

The puzzle: if the lift trick is universal, why haven't we found a universal formula for b and f together? Most earlier studies looked only at the animal — its weight, wing area, muscles — and ignored the air around it.

That is the gap this paper fills. The air itself is not still. Turbulence is a tangle of swirls from hundreds of kilometers across (cyclones) down to a few hundred micrometers — exactly the size of the smallest flyers. Even a tiny insect with Re near 1, where air would seem calm, still lives inside turbulence. And turbulence is stronger in some habitats than others.

The authors ask: what if we include the turbulence of the atmosphere when we describe flight? Their goal is a physics-informed yardstick that links the animal's size and beat to the energy in the surrounding swirls — and lets that tiny wasp and that huge albatross sit on the same line.

Try it below: drag wingspan from wasp to albatross and flapping speed from slow to ultra-fast. Watch how many times bigger one animal is than another, and see your dot move across the kingdom-wide b vs f map the paper had to explain.

002~8 min

Air Itself Has a Hidden Pattern

Air looks empty, but it is full of swirls. A breeze is not one smooth push. It is vortices inside vortices: a cyclone hundreds of kilometers wide carries smaller eddies, which carry even smaller ones, down to swirls only a few hundred micrometers across. The paper points out that this goes from bigger than any bird to smaller than the tiniest wasp. Every flyer lives inside turbulence.

Turbulence has a rule for how energy is shared across sizes. Big eddies feed smaller eddies, which feed smaller ones still, until the energy finally turns into heat. In the middle range where this handing-down happens, the energy at each size follows a famous pattern called the Kolmogorov–Richardson cascade.

$$E_{\rm turb}(k) = C_K\, \varepsilon^{2/3}\, k^{-5/3}$$

Here is what each part means, in plain words:

  • k is the wavenumber, just $k = 1/b$ where $b$ is a size like a wingspan. Big $k$ means small vortex. Small $k$ means huge vortex. The paper uses $k = 1/b$ so an animal's size can be placed directly on the turbulence scale.
  • $E_{\rm turb}(k)$ is how much turbulent energy sits at that size $k$.
  • $\varepsilon$ (say "epsilon") measures how strongly turbulence is being stirred. Think of it as the power of the weather at that place and time.
  • $C_K \approx 0.5$ is the Kolmogorov constant, just a small number close to one half.

The important part is $k^{-5/3}$. That is a power law: energy is proportional to $k$ to the $\,-5/3$ power. On a log-log plot — where both axes count powers of ten — a power law looks like a straight line, and the slope of that line is the exponent. So $\,-5/3 \approx -1.67$ is just the steepness of that line.

What $\varepsilon$ does: It does not change the slope. It slides the whole line up or down. A calm night has $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-4}\,{\rm m^2/s^3}$. A sunny, windy day can reach $\varepsilon \approx 5\times 10^{-1}\,{\rm m^2/s^3}$ — thousands of times stronger — depending on habitat and altitude. That is why the paper says animals in different climates may feel very different turbulence.

Why does dimensions force $\,-5/3$? If the only things that matter are $\varepsilon$ (power per mass) and the size $k$, there is only one way to combine them to make an energy density. No extra length is involved, so the $k^{-5/3}$ shape is locked in. If you add a fourth ingredient — like body mass or wing area — you could get a different slope. That idea will matter in the next lessons.

Try it below. Drag $\varepsilon$ from a calm night to a stormy afternoon and watch the whole $\,-5/3$ line lift — same slope, more energy everywhere. Flip between log-log and linear to see why the paper lives in log-log, then drop a bee ($k \approx 100/{\rm m}$) and an albatross ($k \approx 0.7/{\rm m}$) onto the same cascade.

The Paper's New Yardstick: E_sp = b^3 f^2

Turbulence researchers draw spectra: how much energy lives at each size of swirl, from a cyclone down to a millimeter eddy. The paper asks: can we put a flying animal on that same kind of graph?

The trick is a new yardstick for the animal, called animal turbulent energy density and written $E_{\rm sp}$.

$$E_{\rm sp}(k) = b^3 f^2 = f^2 k^{-3}$$

Here $b$ is wingspan in meters, $f$ is flapping frequency in Hertz (beats per second), and $k = 1/b$ is the wavenumber. Bigger $k$ just means a smaller animal. So $k$ is the paper's way of putting wingspan on a turbulence scale.

Where does $b^3 f^2$ come from? In a simplified version, the air right at the wingtip moves roughly as fast as the tip itself, about $b \times f$. Kinetic energy per unit mass goes like $(b f)^2$. That energy is not spread everywhere — the animal makes its wake at its own size. The math way to say "concentrated at $k = 1/b$" is a Dirac spike written $\delta(k - 1/b) = b\,\delta(kb - 1)$, which is zero except at the animal's own scale. Multiply them and you get $b^3 f^2$ with units $\text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$, designed to be directly comparable to the turbulence spectrum $E_{\rm turb}(k)$ from lesson 2.

Intuition: to avoid being shoved around by a vortex its own size, and to keep its own leading-edge vortex useful before it fades, the animal must dump at least this much energy into the air at its own scale. It's a minimal estimate, mass-specific (per kilogram) and per-wavenumber, not the full biology.

What does the $k^{-3}$ part mean? Look at the formula as $E_{\rm sp}(k) = f^2 \times k^{-3}$. If you kept $f$ fixed and just changed $k$, $E_{\rm sp}$ would fall very steeply with $k$ — on a log-log plot that's a straight line with slope $-3$. In real life $f$ also depends on $b$, so the overall slope for many species together becomes less steep, like $-5/3$ or $-2$, which you will test next time.

With that yardstick, a 250-micrometer wasp flapping at 200 Hz and a 1.4-meter bird flapping at 2 Hz both get one number and one position $k = 1/b$ — ready to plot on the same turbulence graph.

Watch how changing $b$ slides the sharp spike left or right (that's $k = 1/b$), while changing $f$ makes the spike taller (that's $b^3 f^2$). If Flights Followed One Rule, What Would the Slope Be?

Imagine every flying animal promised to obey one simple rule like “if you are twice as wide, you flap half as fast.” What would that mean for the energy they dump into the air?

The paper tests exactly that idea. Its yardstick is Esp = b3 f2 — wingspan cubed times flapping frequency squared. That comes from the wingtip’s kinetic energy per kilogram, (b f)2, thought to be concentrated at the animal’s own size scale k = 1/b. Bigger k means a smaller animal and smaller vortex.

Any rule of the form “f is proportional to b to some power” becomes a straight line on a log-log plot of Esp vs k:

$$E_{\rm sp}(k) \propto k^{\alpha}$$

Here α (alpha) is just the slope. Steeper negative slope = energy drops faster as you go to smaller animals.

⚖️

aero α = −2

f ∝ b−1/2 from balancing lift and weight in hover. Gives Esp ∝ k−2 (Eq. 3).

🔋

power α = −5/3

f ∝ b−2/3 from same power per kilogram. Gives Esp ∝ k−5/3 (Eq. 5) — same slope as air turbulence itself.

🦴

limits α = −1 & −3

f ∝ b−1 from wing-bone stress → α=−1 (Eq. 4). f = fmax from muscle speed → α=−3 (Eq. 6).

How n becomes α: If f ∝ bn, then Esp=b3f2 ∝ b3+2n ∝ k−(3+2n), so α = −3 − 2n. Plug in n=−1/2 → α=−2; n=−2/3 → α=−5/3; n=−1 → α=−1; n=0 → α=−3. Full power form is Esp= C Pm2/3 k−5/3 (Eq. 7).

The paper notes what each line does: the −1 and −3 lines act like walls that bound almost all 423 species. The −2 line hugs the very biggest (birds, bats) and very tiniest (feather-winged beetles) but sits too low for hoverers like hawkmoths — odd, since it was invented for hovering. The −5/3 line is the best middle road.

In the lab, turn the four theory slopes on and off. Then drag n yourself to see how any f(b) rule turns into a slope α — watch which of the four it lands closest to.

005~9 min

Putting 400 Species on One Graph

Imagine pinning every flying animal’s wingbeat on one page — a 250-micron fairy wasp that beats at 850 Hz next to a 1.4-meter albatross at 0.5 Hz. That’s what Figure 2 does: more than 700 dots from 423 species in 25 orders, collected from 33 earlier studies, all turned into the paper’s single number Esp = b3 f2.

Here b is wingspan in meters, f is flaps per second (Hz), and k = 1/b is how tightly you zoom in: big k means a tiny animal, small k means a huge one. On a log-log plot, a straight line means a power law: Esp ∝ kα, where α is just the slope.

Four slopes were waiting to be tested. The paper drew them all and asked: which one goes through the middle of the cloud?

  • Upper ceiling: α = −1 (bone and wing material can’t take more stress — dots rarely go above this).
  • Lower floor: α = −3 (muscles can’t flap faster than about fmax — dots rarely go below this). At one size, dots still spread over 1,000 times (3 decades) vertically, but they stay between those two bounds.
  • Middle candidates: α = −2 from balancing lift and weight, and α = −5/3 from assuming every animal puts out roughly the same power per kilogram.
Roughly, R2 is a score from 0 to 1 for “how close are the dots to the line?” Closer to 1 is tighter. Negative means even a flat average line would do better.

Checked with that score, the −5/3 line wins for the whole animal kingdom: R2 = 0.7572, versus R2 = 0.6915 for the aerodynamic −2 and only R2 = 0.4573 for −5/2 when forced on everyone. If you let the slope be anything, the absolute best free fit is α = −1.531 with R2 = 0.7632 — barely better than −5/3, so the physics-inspired −5/3 is kept.

There is no insect-versus-vertebrate split at this zoomed-out level — birds, bats and insects overlap. The line also gives a physical payoff. Written out fully it is Esp = C Pm2/3 k−5/3, where Pm is mass-specific power (power per kilogram) and C is just a number near 1. Setting C = 1, the best-fit line implies Pm = 67 ± 50 W/kg, right in the range other labs have measured directly, even with all the simplifications.

One gap remains: insects around 1 mm wingspan are thinly sampled — probably because they’re hard to see and catch, not because they don’t exist. The paper flags this as observation bias.

For the curious: in logs this is log10Esp = α log10k + C. For each fixed α, the paper chooses the intercept C that minimizes squared vertical errors, then reports R2 and the fitted C. The full table of those fits per group is Table 1, which the course simplifies to the headline numbers above.

006~9 min

Look Closer: Insects Follow Their Own Rule

Remember the big picture from the last lesson: put every flyer from a 250-micrometer wasp to a 1.4-meter albatross on one log-log plot of Esp vs. k, and a single line with slope −5/3 fits pretty well. That slope means $E_{\rm sp} \propto k^{-5/3}$, where $k = 1/b$ is just 1 divided by wingspan and $E_{\rm sp}=b^{3}f^{2}=f^{2}k^{-3}$ is the paper's energy-density yardstick. But if you look carefully, insects are messier — at one size their $E_{\rm sp}$ can sprawl over a thousand-fold range.

So the authors asked: what if insects are not one cloud, but several clouds mixed together? They split the data by how wings are actually used in flight — not just family tree, but wing shape, how far wings swing, and what kind of muscle wiggles them. In the Appendix they make eight insect groups. For example: bees and wasps (Hymenoptera) whose front and hind wings hook together; flies split three ways — Brachycera (houseflies, hoverflies), craneflies, and mosquito-like flies; dragonflies and friends (Odonata, Mecoptera, Neuroptera, Orthoptera, Blattodea) with four separate wings flapped by synchronous muscles; true bugs plus beetles (Hemiptera + Coleoptera) whose hard forewings cover beating hindwings powered by asynchronous muscles; and butterflies (plus stick insects) versus moths (plus caddisflies and mayflies) who overlap their scaly wings differently.

Hidden second law: Once separated, every insect group stops following −5/3 and snaps onto a steeper line: $$E_{\rm sp} \propto k^{-5/2}$$ (Eq. 8). Same slope for all insects, but each group has its own height C, as if written $\log_{10}E_{\rm sp}= -2.5\,\log_{10}k + C$.

That is not a tweak. No previous rule predicted −5/2. The aerodynamic rule predicted −2, the stress limit −1, the constant-power idea −5/3, and the muscle-speed limit −3. For vertebrates, −5/3 stays king. Table 1 makes it concrete: birds (Aves) are $R^{2}=0.7919$ for −5/3 versus only $0.3389$ for −5/2; bats $0.9424$ versus $0.7731$. For insects it flips: Brachycera flies $0.9488$ for −5/2 versus $0.8011$ for −5/3, Hymenoptera $0.9296$ versus $0.8268$, Hemiptera $0.9688$ versus $0.9128$. A higher $R^{2}$ means the line explains more of the dots — closer to 1 is better.

A universal insect slope with a family-dependent offset suggests a missing physics piece tied to wing morphology and how flight muscles bend and store elastic energy, not just to weight or power. The paper does not claim to know the mechanism yet; it points to wing use as the clue worth testing with wing-stiffness and muscle measurements.

007~7 min

The Air Changes at the Wingtip

Earlier lessons made one number to compare all flyers: Esp = b3 f2. It worked surprisingly well — every animal from a 250-micrometer wasp to a 1.4-meter albatross landed near the same line. But insects hid a second, steeper line. Why would vertebrates and insects split?

The paper's clue is what the air itself is doing right at the wingtip. Even in still air, a flapping wing drags air with it. Is that little wingtip flow smooth or choppy? The paper estimates it with a classic fluid idea, the Reynolds number — but applied only to the tip:

$$\mathrm{Re}_{\rm wingtip}=\frac{b^{2} f}{\nu}$$

Here $b$ is wingspan in meters, $f$ is flaps per second, and $\nu$ (nu) is the air's stickiness, about $1.5\times 10^{-5}$ m2/s. You don't need the history of $\nu$ — just see what the formula says: bigger wing ($b^{2}$) and faster flap ($f$) both make the tip flow more turbulent.

Two regimes, two scalings: if $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm wingtip} < 10^{3}$ the tip vortex is laminar (smooth, blue in the paper's Figs. 2–3). If $\mathrm{Re}_{\rm wingtip} > 10^{4}$ it is spontaneously turbulent (choppy, yellow). Tiny insects sit in blue; birds and bats sit in yellow.

That color split almost exactly matches the scaling split. Groups that follow $E_{\rm sp} \propto k^{-5/3}$ — the same $-5/3$ slope as atmospheric turbulence — mostly live in yellow. Groups that follow the steeper $E_{\rm sp} \propto k^{-5/2}$ — all the insect orders when separated by wing use — mostly live in blue.

This matters because we used to think of one universal lift trick, the leading-edge vortex, working the same way at every size. The paper suggests the environment matters too. Turbulence itself exists from hundreds of kilometers down to a few hundred micrometers, so even a flyer with $\mathrm{Re} \sim 1$ feels turbulence around it. But whether its own tip makes laminar or turbulent air changes the physics it has to solve.

It's not magic — it's like rowing a canoe on glassy water versus whitewater. The $ -5/3 $ versus $ -5/2 $ may reflect different wing shapes and muscle types tuned to those two air regimes. And because turbulence strength $\varepsilon$ changes with habitat and weather, the same species might need different kinematics on a calm night ($\varepsilon \sim 10^{-4}$) versus a stormy day ($\varepsilon$ up to $5\times10^{-1}$).

008~8 min

What Turbulence Tells Us About Flying Animals

So what did the paper actually find, after putting more than 700 dots — 423 species from tiny feather-winged beetles to bats and albatrosses — on one picture?

Think of the picture as two sloping rulers. The first ruler is the air itself. In the part of the sky where animals fly, turbulent air has a very particular slope: if you plot its energy against vortex size, you get a straight line going down with a slope of -5/3. That's turbulence physics, written as $E_{\rm turb}(k)=C_K \varepsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3}$ with $C_K\sim 0.5$ and $\varepsilon$ saying how strongly the air is stirred.

The second ruler is the animals. For each animal the paper invents a simple number that can be put on the same kind of graph: $E_{\rm sp}=b^3 f^2$, where $b$ is wingspan and $f$ is how fast it flaps. Big $b$ and fast $f$ means more energy dumped into its own wake. Plotted against $k=1/b$ (so tiny animals are on the right), animals also fall on a straight line — and the best straight line through all animals together has the same slope the air has: $-5/3$.

Unified but steeper for insects: Across the whole animal kingdom the best fit is $E_{\rm sp} \propto k^{-5/3}$ with $R^2=0.7572$, better than the aerodynamic $-2$ idea ($R^2=0.6915$). If you split by how wings are used, vertebrates stay on $-5/3$ (birds $R^2=0.7919$, bats $R^2=0.9424$) but insect groups switch to a steeper $E_{\rm sp} \propto k^{-5/2}$ — for example flies $R^2=0.9488$ for $-5/2$ vs $0.8011$ for $-5/3$, bees and wasps $0.9296$ vs $0.8268$.

Why care that the slopes match? In turbulence $-5/3$ comes from “the power being put in stays the same as it flows to smaller vortices.” In animals the same $-5/3$ comes from “the power per kilogram $P_m$ needed to fly stays roughly the same.” The paper finds, if you pretend the constant in front is $1$, $P_m = 67 \pm 50$ W/kg — which is roughly what labs have measured directly. The hint is not that animals copy turbulence, but that the air’s turbulence may act like a quiet selective pressure: animals that can inject about as much energy as turbulence has at their size keep their leading-edge vortex before it is smeared out.

The insect twist is the puzzle. Once you group by wing use — bees/wasps together, three fly groups, four-wing dragonfly-like flyers, bugs+beetles, butterflies vs. moths and relatives — each insect group follows $-5/2$ with its own height $C$ on the graph (see the appendix groups). Vertebrates don’t. That steeper slope isn’t predicted by any of the old $f$ vs. $b$ rules, so the authors think there is a still-unknown physical trick tied to insect wings and how their flight muscles work.

It’s a first-order picture, and the authors are clear about the limits. $E_{\rm sp}$ ignores body mass, wing area and how far the wing actually sweeps. Insects around 1 mm are sparse in the data — we just don’t watch them much. And we still need what almost no one has measured together: the air’s $\varepsilon$ at that place and time plus the animal’s $b$ and $f$. Next step: go outside, measure habitat turbulence and watch who flies well in it.

Try it yourself in the lab: build a flyer, put it in calm night air versus a stormy day, and see whether its ruler sits above the air’s ruler — and how switching from the vertebrate $-5/3$ to the insect $-5/2$ changes the answer.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

wingspan (b)

Distance from one wingtip to the other. The paper writes this as b, measured in meters.

flapping frequency (f)

How many times an animal flaps its wings per second. The paper writes this as f, measured in Hz.

wavenumber (k)

1 divided by wingspan, written k = 1/b. Bigger k means smaller animal and smaller vortex size.

animal turbulent energy density (E_sp)

The paper's invention: E_sp = b cubed times f squared = f squared times k to the minus 3. It is the energy per unit mass the animal puts into the air at its own size scale.

turbulence energy density spectrum (E_turb)

How energy is spread across vortex sizes in turbulent air. In the inertial range it follows E_turb = C_K times epsilon to the two-thirds times k to the minus five-thirds.

Kolmogorov constant (C_K)

Tiny constant about 0.5 in the turbulence spectrum formula.

dissipation rate (epsilon)

Written epsilon. How strongly turbulence is stirred. Night values about 10 to the minus 4, day values 10 to the minus 4 to 5 times 10 to the minus 1 square meters per cubic second.

power law / scaling relation

A straight line on a log-log plot, meaning one quantity is proportional to another to a power: E_sp proportional to k to the alpha.

wingtip Reynolds number

Here Re_wingtip = b squared times f divided by nu, with nu the viscosity of air. Below about 1,000 the wingtip vortex is laminar, above about 10,000 it is spontaneously turbulent.

leading-edge vortex

A swirl that forms on the front edge of a flapping wing and boosts lift, seen from tiny insects to large birds.

mass-specific power output

Power divided by body mass, written P_m. The minus five-thirds animal law can be written E_sp = C times P_m to the two-thirds times k to the minus five-thirds.

Dirac delta

A math spike that is zero everywhere except at k = 1/b, used to say all the animal's injected energy is concentrated at its own size scale.
