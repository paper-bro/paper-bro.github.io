001Lesson 1~6 min

Cutting out anything with a tap and seeing the scale up front (promptable segmentation + SA-1B preview + paper thread F4, F5, F7, F10, F12, F14, F15)

Imagine you have a photo and a pair of digital scissors. Segmentation means cutting out exactly which pixels belong to one object — every pixel gets a yes/no answer. That's a mask. A box just draws a rectangle around it; a mask follows the wiggly edge of hair, a handle, a leaf.

Until recently, most cut-out tools were specialists: one model for people, one for cars, retrained each time. The paper asks a different question: what if one model could cut out anything you point to, with no retraining — like ChatGPT for images?

Promptable segmentation: you give an image + a prompt (a hint), the model gives back a mask. A prompt can be a point (“this spot”), a box (“inside here”), a rough mask (“clean this up”), or even text (“the hat”). The job is to return a valid mask for at least one object your hint could mean.

Why the word valid? Because hints are often vague. Tap the dot on a shirt and you could mean the shirt or the person wearing it. Tap a wheel and you could mean wheel or bike. The paper says the model must not panic or blur them together — it must pick one real object, like a language model giving a coherent answer to a vague question. That rule forces the model to understand “objects can be inside other objects.”

This is the same playbook that made foundation models work in language: train once on a broad, promptable task, then solve new tasks by prompt engineering — phrasing your request cleverly. Here, instead of writing text prompts, you place points, drag boxes, or combine hints to get edges, proposals, instance masks, and more, without ever retraining SAM.

To make that work, the authors had to build data at an unusual scale. Photos with masks aren't just lying around the internet, so they built a data engine that taught itself. The result is SA-1B: 11 million licensed, privacy-blurred high-resolution photos with 1.1 billion masks — about 100 cut-outs per image on average, about 400× more masks than the biggest previous dataset. The examples above show why that matters: from tiny screws to whole buildings, the diversity is what lets a single model generalize to new photos it never saw.

Try it below: place a point on the toy scene — especially right in the middle of the hat that sits on the person. Watch what counts as a “valid” answer, then add a background point or box to steer it, and try a text prompt. Notice how one vague dot can mean two right answers.

002Lesson 2~7 min

A brain that sees once, answers fast and shows the whole paper thread (how data leads to results: SA-1B creation → scaling tests → zero-shot tests → text-to-mask and limits)

Imagine asking a friend to cut things out of photos for you. You could hand them one photo at a time, wait while they stare at it, then point and say "that one." If they had to re-look at the whole photo for every point, it would be slow. SAM avoids that by looking once — carefully — and then answering many points instantly.

SAM has three parts that work together. First, a heavy image encoder looks at the photo once and turns it into a compact summary called an embedding. In the paper this encoder is a Vision Transformer called ViT-H/16 pre-trained with MAE. SAM resizes your photo to 1024×1024 and makes an embedding that is 16× smaller: 64×64 squares, each holding 256 numbers. Think of it as a small, rich map of the image.

Second, a flexible prompt encoder turns your hint into numbers. SAM handles two kinds of hints. Sparse hints are points and boxes (and even text). A point becomes its position plus a learned word that means "this is foreground" or "this is background"; a box becomes its two corners. Dense hints are masks themselves — they are squished with tiny convolutions and added straight onto the image map. Text hints use a CLIP text encoder, which you will see in a later lesson.

Third, a fast mask decoder mixes the two. It is a tiny 2-layer Transformer. It does self-attention among prompts, then cross-attention both ways: prompts look at the image, and the image looks at prompts. Then SAM upsamples that map 4× with learned up-scaling (transposed convolutions) and uses a small MLP to turn one special output token into a custom classifier that paints "foreground vs not" at each pixel.

Why can the encoder be heavy? Because its cost is amortized — paid once. The paper measures the prompt encoder + decoder at about ~50 milliseconds in a browser on CPU. So one slow look, then many fast answers.

What happens if you add a second point? In the paper's training, SAM is taught to treat every new point as a correction: it looks at where its last mask was wrong and you add a foreground point on a missing piece or a background point on extra spill. The mask shrinks, grows, or switches to the part you meant. That iterative training (11 rounds per mask) is why SAM feels interactive.

003Lesson 3~7 min

One dot, three honest answers where averaging masks fails

Tap a shirt that’s on a person. What did you mean — the shirt, or the whole person? Or tap the wheel on a car. One dot can honestly mean three different cut-outs: the tiny detail, the part, or the whole thing.

This is the core headache the paper solves. Most models are forced to give one answer. When one point matches several real objects, a one-answer model does the worst thing: it averages them. Imagine averaging a photo of a button and a photo of a whole shirt — you get a blurry smear that is neither. SAM was built to not smear.

SAM’s fix: for a single point, predict three masks at once — whole, part, and subpart — plus a confidence score for each.

That confidence is a predicted IoU. IoU just means "how much two cut-outs overlap." SAM predicts, "if you let me keep this mask, I think its IoU with the true object would be 0.86." The highest score is what SAM shows first, so you can rank without knowing the true mask.

How does it learn not to average? In training SAM uses a simple rule: compute the loss for all three masks, but only learn from the best one.

$$\text{loss} = \min_{k=1,2,3} \text{Loss}(\text{mask}_k, \text{true mask})$$

Only the winner gets gradient — the teaching signal. The other two are left alone, so each hypothesis can stay honest. Three is enough in practice because nesting is usually at most three deep: subpart inside part inside whole.

The paper’s losses are, in a simplified version, focal loss plus dice loss in a 20-to-1 mix for the mask, and mean-squared error for that IoU score. Training also copies how people click: SAM starts with one point or box, then gets up to 8 more points sampled from where it was wrong, plus 2 extra refinements where it just looks again at its own unthresholded mask logits. That’s 11 rounds per mask.

With more than one prompt, ambiguity mostly disappears. So when you have given 2+ points, SAM switches to predicting a single mask — using a special 4th output token reserved for the unambiguous case.

004Lesson 4~8 min

A data factory that teaches itself by putting a real SA-1B example at each major step (SA-1B samples → scaling tests → zero-shot tests → text-to-mask)

Text and photos are everywhere on the internet, so models like CLIP can learn from billions of image-text pairs for free. Masks — those pixel-perfect cut-outs that say exactly which pixels belong to a cat, a chair, or a crumb — are not. No one uploads them. That is the factory problem the paper had to solve: how do you get a billion masks when they don't exist?

The answer is a data engine: a loop where the model and the data teach each other. Think of a cookie factory that starts with a few hand-shaped cookies, uses them to train a cookie-cutting robot, then the robot helps humans cut faster, then humans teach the robot the tricky shapes, until finally the robot cuts alone.

Stage 1 was assisted-manual. SAM ran live in a browser. Professional annotators clicked foreground and background points and cleaned up with brush and eraser. No semantic limits — they cut "stuff" and "things" they could name. At first SAM was weak, trained on public datasets, and it took about 34 seconds per mask. After 6 retrains and scaling the image encoder from ViT-B to ViT-H, it dropped to about 14 seconds per mask — about 6.5 times faster than COCO masks and only twice as slow as drawing a box. They collected 4.3 million masks on 120 thousand images, and masks per image rose from about 20 to 44.

Stage 2 was semi-automatic to get more diversity. They trained a generic "object" box detector on all the stage 1 masks. That detector pre-filled confident masks automatically, and annotators focused only on what was missing — the less obvious objects. The easy stuff was now free, so the remaining work felt harder: about 34 seconds per mask again (not counting the automatic ones). In 180 thousand more images they added 5.9 million masks, for a total of 10.2 million. Masks per image went from 44 to about 72 after 5 more retrains. The model was learning rarer shapes because humans were now forced to look past the obvious.

Stage 3 could only run because SAM had become ambiguity-aware. Earlier you saw that one point can mean shirt or person — whole, part, or subpart. If you blindly prompt a grid of points, most points are ambiguous. Without three predictions per point, the model would average masks into blurs. With it, a point on the shirt can honestly propose shirt, person, and sweater separately.

So stage 3 was fully automatic. No humans. They prompted ambiguity-aware SAM with a regular 32 by 32 grid of points on every image — that's 1,024 points times 3 masks, often hundreds of masks per image. They also ran the same grid on zoomed-in crops (2 by 2 and 4 by 4 overlapping windows) to recover small masks. Then they aggressively filtered:

  • Predicted IoU filter: keep only masks SAM thinks are confident, with predicted IoU at least 88.0.
  • Stability filter: threshold the soft mask at $0.5-\delta$ and $0.5+\delta$ — if those two binary masks overlap with IoU at least 95.0, the mask is stable.
  • NMS at 0.7: if two boxes overlap too much, keep the more confident one and delete the duplicate.
  • Tiny-piece cleanup: remove connected components and holes smaller than 100 pixels, and drop masks covering more than 95% of the image.

Run that on all 11 million licensed, high-resolution photos and you get 1.1 billion masks — about 100 per image on average, with 99.1% made fully automatically. That's the dataset SA-1B that powers everything else.

Key idea: the factory bootstraps itself — human help makes the model better, a better model makes human help faster, until humans can step away and the ambiguity-aware model plus strict filters can mass-produce.

005Lesson 5~7 min

A billion cut-outs and learning to read mask-property plots (mask-per-image, size, concavity)

Imagine you are asked to cut out everything in a photo — not just the dog, but every leaf, every button, every crumb on the table. That is what the Segment Anything team did, 1.1 billion times.

Their dataset is called SA-1B: 11 million licensed, high-resolution photos (about 3300 × 4950 pixels on average, shared at 1500 pixels on the short side) and 1.1 billion masks. A mask is just a black-and-white cut-out that says which pixels belong to one object. Faces and license plates were blurred to protect privacy, and 99.1% of the masks were made fully automatically by SAM itself.

How big is that? The next-biggest segmentation dataset, Open Images, has about 400 times fewer masks and 11 times fewer images. On average SA-1B has ~100 masks per image — about 36 times more than Open Images and 3.5 times more than ADE20K. If you look at the left histogram in Figure 6, SA-1B's curve is pushed far to the right: many images have dozens or hundreds of cut-outs. The middle histogram shows SA-1B also has more small and medium sized masks — which makes sense when you cut out so much — and the right histogram shows shape complexity (concavity, or 1 minus area divided by area of its convex hull) looks broadly similar to other datasets once you control for size.

Key numbers to remember: 11M images, 1.1B masks, ~100 per image. SA-1B is a simplified training set — the paper trains its released SAM only on the fully-automatic masks because adding the earlier manual masks improves its score by only ~0.5 mIoU.

But are a billion automatic cut-outs any good? The team checked on 500 random images (~50,000 masks) by asking professional annotators to fix every auto mask with precise brush and eraser tools, then measuring overlap with IoU — intersection over union, or shared area divided by total area, where 1.0 is perfect. They found 94% of pairs were above 0.90 IoU and 97% above 0.75. For context, two humans drawing the same object usually agree at 0.85 to 0.91. That means the auto masks are inside normal human agreement, and human raters and extra training tests backed this up.

What makes SA-1B more diverse, and what bias remains? On image layout, SA-1B has less of the strong center bias seen in COCO and Open Images — there is more coverage in the corners — but photographer bias does not disappear. On geography, SA-1B is broader than its predecessors: by the paper's caption-based estimates, Europe ~49.8% (540M masks), Asia & Oceania ~36.2% (423M), North America ~7.7% (80M), Latin America & Caribbean ~3.5% (36M), Africa ~2.8% (28M), and by income high 54%, middle 45%, low 0.9%. Every region still has at least 28 million masks — more than 10 times the total of any prior dataset — yet Africa and low-income countries remain underrepresented. That is the diversity gain and the remaining gap.

006Lesson 6~8 min

Tested everywhere without studying — starting the zero-shot thread (23 unseen datasets, single-point test, metrics)

Imagine you studied only family photos, then someone tests you on underwater dolphins, X-ray luggage, microscopes full of cells, and drone roofs — without letting you study first. That's SAM's test.

The paper calls this zero-shot transfer: SAM was trained only on SA-1B, then evaluated on 23 completely unseen datasets covering plants, cells, boulders, timber logs, underwater, traffic cameras, recycling, irregular shapes, occlusions, simulation, parking lots, egocentric kitchens, dolphins, wood, driving, X-ray, paintings, trash and more. Table 7 in the paper lists them; none of their images or labels were seen during training.

The core check is the hardest prompt: a single foreground point. Point to one spot and name one object. The problem is ambiguity — one dot on a hat that is on a person could mean hat, person, or person-plus-hat. To make the test fair and repeatable, the paper samples the first point as the "center" — the pixel farthest from the object's boundary (the peak of the distance transform). That's the standard interactive-segmentation rule. Later they also try a random point inside the object, which is even harder because you might click near the edge.

How is quality measured? Two ways. First, $mIoU$ — mean Intersection over Union. For one mask, $IoU = \frac{\text{area of overlap}}{\text{area of both together}}$. Average that over all objects in a dataset and you get $mIoU$, from $0\%$ (no overlap) to $100\%$ (perfect). Second, a human study where paid annotators rated masks $1$ (nonsense) to $10$ (pixel-perfect) after inspecting boundaries.

A trick to handle ambiguity fairly: oracle evaluation. SAM can predict 3 masks with confidence scores (predicted IoU) for one point. By default the paper scores SAM's top-ranked mask. For the oracle score, they let SAM pick the best of its 3 by comparing to ground truth — revealing how much of the "error" was just picking a different valid object.

Result in one sentence: on these 23 tough, unseen sets, SAM beats the strongest single-point baseline RITM (HRNet32 trained on COCO+LVIS) on 16 of 23 datasets by up to about 47 $mIoU$ points, and with oracle it beats RITM on all 23. On the 7 datasets where humans rated quality, SAM averaged $7$ to $9$ out of $10$ — "identifiable, errors small and rare" — significantly higher than RITM and higher than a single-output SAM. On datasets like DRAM (paintings) and IBD (drone roofs) SAM actually loses on $mIoU$ but wins on human ratings: $mIoU$ punished a valid answer (e.g., whole building vs. roof), humans didn't.

Two more clues: give more points ($2,3,5,9$) and the gap shrinks — everyone improves when ambiguity disappears. And switch from center to random first point, and SAM's lead grows — SAM is more robust to sloppy clicks.

007Lesson 7~9 min

One tool, many jobs — continuing the zero-shot thread (edges → proposals → instances → text-to-mask) and why SAM's masks can look better even when scores are lower

Lesson 6 left us with a thread: SAM learned one skill — take any prompt and return a valid mask — and it already works on 23 datasets it never saw. Now follow that thread. What if no human is clicking at all? Can the same weights do edges, find everything, act as a detector's paintbrush, even listen to words?

The trick is prompt engineering. You do not retrain SAM. You change how you prompt it.

▣

Edges: grid trick

Prompt a 16×16 grid of points → 768 masks (3 per point). Throw away duplicates with NMS, run a Sobel filter on the soft mask edges, thin them. That is it.

📦

Proposals: everything

Prompt a dense 64×64 grid → about 900 masks after NMS at 0.9. Use those masks as object proposals. No detector needed.

🎯

Instances: detector + SAM

Take an off-the-shelf detector's boxes, feed each box to SAM, feed SAM's own mask back once for refinement. Detector finds where, SAM draws how.

On BSDS500 edge detection — a dataset SAM never saw — SAM gets ODS 0.768, OIS 0.786, AP 0.794 and R50 0.928. HED, a classic deep edge model trained on BSDS, gets 0.788 / 0.808 / 0.840. SAM is close even though it was never taught what an "edge" label means. Why not higher? SAM predicts more sensible edges (so recall R50 is huge at 0.928), but BSDS only labels a subset, so extra edges count as "wrong" and lower precision. A Sobel filter alone is 0.539.

For object proposals on LVIS v1 (1203 categories, high-quality masks) SAM gets AR@1000 59.3%. A single-output version drops to 54.9% — proof that 3 masks per point helps recall. A strong ViTDet-H detector used as a proposal trick gets 63.0% overall. Look closer: SAM actually beats the detector on medium and large objects, and on rare and common categories. It only loses on small and frequent objects, where the detector has memorized LVIS quirks from training.

Same story for instance segmentation. Prompt SAM with ViTDet boxes: COCO AP 46.5 vs ViTDet 51.0, LVIS 44.7 vs 46.6. Lower score — but in a human study trained raters scored SAM's masks higher (crisper boundaries). The score drop is about dataset bias: COCO and LVIS masks are simple polygons with no holes by construction. A plate with food on it is labeled filled-in. ViTDet learns to copy that; SAM, zero-shot, correctly cuts the hole out — and gets punished by the metric. The mask can look better while the number is lower.

Key idea: Zero-shot scores can be misleading. When the ground truth has biases, a generalist that has not learned the biases can make a better-looking mask and still lose on AP or mIoU.

Last, text-to-mask. There were no text labels for SA-1B. The proof-of-concept does a swap: during training, take a mask bigger than 100×100 pixels, crop around it, and extract a CLIP image embedding — a number vector trained to line up images and text. Sometimes zero out pixels outside the mask and use masked attention so the vector focuses on the object. Train SAM to accept that vector as the first prompt. At test time, run your sentence through CLIP's text encoder and give that vector to SAM instead. Because CLIP's image and text spaces already line up, it works. Ask for "a wheel" or the nuanced "beaver tooth grille" and SAM cuts it. When it picks the wrong thing, one extra point fixes it, just like in Fig. 12.

Try all four jobs yourself. Switch tabs, change the grid and NMS, loosen the detector box, and type your own text prompts — watch when the metric goes down while the mask looks better.

008Lesson 8~7 min

What it can't do — and why it matters — tying scale to zero-shot outcomes and what remains open (fine structure, text-to-mask, domain bias)

So far SAM looks like magic: tap anything, get a clean cut-out, even on photos it never saw before. But magic has edges. If you want to use SAM well — and use it fairly — it helps to see where it wobbles, how scale actually paid off, and what the paper chose to share so others can push further.

First, the honest limits. SAM can miss hair-thin structure — think antenna wires, bike spokes, lace — and sometimes it invents tiny disconnected blobs. Its borders are not as razor-crisp as slower methods that zoom in and polish. And while a click feels instant (~50ms for the light decoder in a browser), the full pipeline with the big ViT-H image encoder is not real-time end-to-end. Give a dedicated interactive tool many points and it can beat SAM on very high IoU. The paper's text-to-mask demo is also exploratory: it works for "a wheel" or "beaver tooth grille" but can pick the wrong object until you add a point.

Second, what the ablations teach about scale. Each data-engine stage improved accuracy on the 23-dataset zero-shot suite. Yet training only on the final automatic masks was almost as good — only about 0.5 mIoU lower than training on all human + auto masks combined — so the released default trains on auto-only for simplicity. Data volume mattered up to a point: 0.1M images was much worse, but 1M images (about 10% of SA-1B, still ~100M masks) was comparable to the full 11M. Encoder size showed the same flattening: ViT-H is clearly better than ViT-B, but only a little better than ViT-L. In short, billion-scale helped a lot, then returns shrank.

Third, responsibility. The paper traced captions to countries and used Flickr geo-tags for COCO and Open Images. SA-1B is more diverse — Europe 49.8% (540M masks), Asia & Oceania 36.2% (423M), North America 7.7%, Latin America 3.5%, Africa 2.8% (still 28M masks, more than 10× any prior dataset's total) — but Africa and low-income countries (0.9%) remain underrepresented, as in all datasets. For fairness segmenting people, 1-point mIoU was similar across perceived gender presentation (feminine 54.4±1.7 vs masculine 55.7±1.7), age (older 62.9±6.7 looks higher but the interval is wide), and Fitzpatrick skin tone 1–6 (all ~51–57 with overlapping 95% confidence intervals). The exception was clothing segmentation with one point: masculine 81.0±1.2 vs feminine 76.3±1.1, a real gap that closes with more points. The takeaway the authors stress: test your own use case, because biases can appear when SAM is plugged into a larger system.

Why release everything? SAM is Apache 2.0, SA-1B is available for research (faces and plates blurred), and there is a demo at segment-anything.com. The goal is to lift segmentation toward a foundation-model era where one promptable model is reused via composition — for example, MCC using SAM for single-image 3D reconstruction, or gaze points from glasses. The paper also reports the cost of that start: 256 A100 GPUs for 68 hours, about 6,963 kWh and an estimated 2.8 metric tons of CO₂, so sharing weights saves others from retraining.

In the lab, hunt the limits yourself: compare a solid shape to a thin antenna, watch the ViT-B → L → H curve flatten, and open the fairness dashboard to see which confidence intervals overlap — and which one for clothing does not. Try writing a "do everything" prompt and notice why SAM, which returns one object per prompt, is not built for semantic or panoptic labeling.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

segmentation

Picking out which pixels belong to an object, like cutting it out with scissors on a photo.

mask

A black-and-white image the same size as the photo where white means "this pixel is part of the object".

prompt

A hint you give the computer about what to cut out: a point, box, rough mask, or text.

promptable segmentation

The task introduced in the paper: return a valid mask for any prompt, even if the prompt is ambiguous.

foundation model

A large model trained on very broad data that can be reused for many new tasks without retraining, often by prompting it.

zero-shot transfer

Using a model on a completely new dataset or task it was never trained on, just by prompting it.

image encoder

Part of SAM that looks at the image once and turns it into a compact numerical summary (an embedding). Heavy and slow, but run only once per image.

prompt encoder + mask decoder

Part of SAM that turns your point/box/mask/text into numbers and mixes them with the image summary to predict a mask. Lightweight, runs in ~50ms per prompt.

IoU

Intersection over Union: area of overlap between predicted and true mask divided by area of both together. 0 = no overlap, 1 = perfect. The paper reports it as % or 0-100.

NMS

Non-Maximum Suppression: a cleanup step that removes duplicate masks that overlap too much, keeping only the most confident one.

ambiguity

When one prompt could mean several valid objects, e.g., a point on a shirt could be shirt or person. SAM handles this by predicting 3 masks.

data engine

The paper's loop where SAM helps humans label, then retrains on those labels, then helps more, until it can label alone.

SA-1B

Dataset built by the data engine: 11M licensed, privacy-protected photos and 1.1B masks (~100 per image on average).

ViT

Vision Transformer: an image model that chops an image into patches and uses attention to understand it. SAM uses ViT-H/16 pretrained with MAE.

CLIP

A model that learns matching numbers for images and text. SAM is trained to accept CLIP image embeddings so at test time it can accept CLIP text embeddings.
