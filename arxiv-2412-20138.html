0011 | The Big Idea~8 min

Why One Super-AI Trader Isn't Enough

Imagine betting your allowance on a stock. To guess right, you'd need to read the company's report card, scan the news, see what crowds are saying on Reddit, and squint at wiggly price charts — all at once, in minutes, while the price keeps moving. That's trading.

For years computers tried two separate paths. Old trading bots used rigid math formulas: "if this line crosses that line, buy." They can't read news. Meanwhile, newer helpers like PIXIU/FinMA (fine-tuned on 136,000 finance instructions), FinGPT (tuned with LoRA on ~50,000 samples), or BloombergGPT (trained from scratch on finance text) are good at answering finance questions, but they weren't built to actually trade.

More recently, researchers turned big language models into solo traders. Some were news-driven — read headlines, guess up or down. Some added memory and reflection, like FinMem and FinAgent, or tried to teach the model with rewards, like SEP. Others became alpha-miners like QuantAgent and AlphaGPT that write little code formulas instead of trading directly. Clever, but still mostly one AI doing everything.

The paper says that leaves two holes. 1) No realistic firm structure. Real Wall Street firms don't have one genius — they have specialists who each own a piece and talk to each other. 2) The "telephone effect." When AIs just keep a long chat history and pass messages, details get lost or twisted over time, like the game of telephone — and context limits make it worse.

TradingAgents copies a real firm. It builds seven specialized AIs organized into teams: four analysts who each look at one lens (company health, news, crowd mood, charts), a research team where a bull and a bear debate, a trader who decides, a risk team with three different attitudes to risk, and a fund manager who gives the final okay. Instead of one endless chat, they mostly write short, structured reports into a shared state that others can query, and only use brief debates when they need to argue.

In the lab below, try being the market. Move the sliders to make news messy or hype loud, then flip between a Solo AI and the AI Firm. Watch how steady the daily buy/sell decisions stay — and see for yourself why splitting the work beats asking one super-AI to juggle it all.

0022 | The Scoreboard~8 min

How Do You Know If a Trader Is Actually Good?

Imagine two friends both say “I made $10.” One started with $20, the other with $1,000. Same $10, very different success. To judge a trader, you need fair scores — not just bragging.

The TradingAgents paper uses four standard Wall Street scores. They tested on 5 big tech stocks (Apple, Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, Google, with detailed tables for AAPL, GOOGL, AMZN) from Jan 1 to Mar 29, 2024, day by day, with one strict rule: no peeking into the future. Each day's decision could only use data up to that day, then the team computed the day's scores before moving on. That's called avoiding look-ahead bias.

The scoreboard: how much? (CR, AR) vs how nicely? (Sharpe) vs how bad was the worst dip? (MDD). You need all four.

Score 1 & 2: How much did you make?

Cumulative Return (CR) is total profit over the whole test:

$$\text{CR}=\frac{V_{\text{end}}-V_{\text{start}}}{V_{\text{start}}}\times 100\%$$

$V_{\text{start}}$ is what you started with, $V_{\text{end}}$ is what you finished with. Turn $100 into $126.62? That's a CR of $26.62\%$ — exactly what TradingAgents got on AAPL.

Annualized Return (AR) stretches that to a yearly rate so you can compare a 3-month test to a 1-year test:

$$\text{AR}=\left(\left(\frac{V_{\text{end}}}{V_{\text{start}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{N}}-1\right)\times 100\%$$

$N$ is years tested (here $N\approx 0.24$ years). A $26.62\%$ in ~3 months annualizes to about $30.5\%$ AR — huge, but only because the period was short and strong.

Score 3: Was it smooth or scary?

Sharpe Ratio (SR) is return per unit of wobble:

$$\text{SR}=\frac{\bar{R}-R_{f}}{\sigma}$$

$\bar{R}$ is your average daily return, $R_{f}$ is the safe "risk-free" return (like a U.S. Treasury bill), and $\sigma$ (sigma) is how much your daily returns wobbled (standard deviation). High SR = you earned well without wild swings. Negative SR = you would have done better just holding safe cash.

Score 4: What's the worst dip you had to stomach?

Maximum Drawdown (MDD) is the biggest fall from a peak to a later trough:

$$\text{MDD}=\max_{t}\frac{\text{Peak}_{t}-\text{Trough}_{t}}{\text{Peak}_{t}}\times 100\%$$

If you peaked at $120 then fell to $108, that's a $10\%$ drawdown. Even if you later recovered, that dip is what might make you panic-sell.

Why you need the balance: in the paper, cautious rule-based strategies often had tiny MDD (they barely traded) but missed profits. High return with huge MDD is also unsustainable — most people quit during the dip.

Who do you have to beat?

The paper benchmarks against five old-school strategies: Buy and Hold (just buy and wait), MACD (buy when a fast trend line crosses above a slow one), KDJ+RSI (momentum: buy when not "overbought" or "oversold"), ZMR (mean reversion: bet prices snap back to average), and SMA (short moving average vs long moving average crossover). If your AI firm can't beat those on all scores, it's not really smarter.

Try the lab below: design a 60-day portfolio by moving Trend, Wobble, and Crash. Watch CR/AR climb with Trend, Sharpe collapse when you add Wobble, and MDD spike when you add a Crash. Can you make a path that beats the cautious baselines without scaring yourself?

0033 | Meet the Analysts~9 min

The Analyst Squad: Four Ways to Look at One Stock

Imagine your school nurse tried to do every job in a hospital — heart surgery, X-rays, and pharmacy — alone. Mistakes would pile up. A real trading firm doesn't work like that either, and neither does TradingAgents.

The paper builds a mini Wall Street firm. As Figure 1 shows, the day flows in five stages: I Analysts gather info in parallel → II Researchers debate → III the Trader decides → IV Risk guardians check → V the Fund Manager approves and trades. Lesson 3 is all about stage I: the Analyst Squad.

📊

Fundamental

Studies the business itself: financial statements, earnings reports, balance sheets, insider trades from filings and SEDI, plus cash flow and debt. Goal: what is the stock really worth?

💬

Sentiment

Listens to the crowd: Reddit posts, X/Twitter chatter, and sentiment scores calculated by an extra helper model. Goal: will hype or fear move the price tomorrow?

📰

News

Reads the world: Bloomberg, Yahoo, EODHD, FinnHub news on company changes, politics, and macro numbers like inflation. Goal: catch shocks early.

📈

Technical

Does math on charts: runs code to compute 60 indicators like RSI (are buyers over-excited?), MACD (where is the trend going?), Bollinger Bands, ATR for volatility, ADX for trend strength, plus volume tools. Goal: time the entry and exit.

Why four? In the paper's example for Apple (AAPL) from Nov 4–18, 2024, the Market Analyst didn't just guess. It called get_YFin_data and saw the price rise from about 220.75 dollars to 228.02 dollars, volume spike to 54.5 million shares on Nov 6, and a 0.25 dollar dividend on Nov 8. Then it called get_stockstats_indicators_report eight times — for RSI, ADX, Bollinger Bands, MACD, VWMA, ATR, Supertrend and CCI — and wrote a short summary of momentum, volatility and volume signals. The Fundamental, Sentiment and News analysts did the same in parallel with their own tools.

Key trick: each analyst writes a concise structured report — key numbers plus a recommendation — and drops it into a shared global state. The next team doesn't wade through a long chat; they query only what they need. That keeps the relational integrity of the data and avoids the "telephone effect" you'll meet in Lesson 5.

One generalist trying to juggle filings, memes, macro news and 60 chart formulas would overload and blur details. Four specialists with the right tools get a true 360° view, and their reports feed straight into the bull-vs-bear debate next.

0044 | Let Them Argue~8 min

Bulls vs Bears: Let Them Argue It Out

One analyst says "Apple looks great!" Another says "Apple looks expensive and risky!" Who do you listen to? In a real Wall Street firm, you don't pick one — you make them argue, and a referee decides.

That is exactly what TradingAgents does with its Researcher Team. Think of it like a debate club with two members: a Bull (the optimist who thinks the price will go up) and a Bear (the pessimist who thinks it will go down). Neither of them trades. Their job is to fight it out with facts and hand the Trader a balanced summary.

🐂

The Bull

Finds reasons to buy: growth, strong brand, fat profits. "Apple can win new markets."

🐻

The Bear

Finds risks to warn about: high price, competition, debt. "What if it drops?"

Here is how the debate works. Both researchers first query the global state — they pull the concise analyst reports (not a long chat history). Then they debate in natural language for n rounds, guided by a facilitator agent. When they finish, the facilitator reads the whole debate history, picks the prevailing side, and saves it as a short structured entry. That entry — not the messy chat — is what the Trader actually uses.

Why bother? The paper points to earlier studies showing that short, focused debates create deeper reasoning than one model thinking alone. Two opposing views catch blind spots.

See it in the paper's AAPL example (Nov 2024):
The Bull argued: Apple is pushing a new AI-powered smart-home device that fits its ecosystem, plus stellar numbers — 46.21% gross margin, 23.97% net margin, 164.59% ROE — strong cash flow, and big investment firms adding to their holdings.
The Bear shot back: Amazon and Google already dominate smart home, US-China tensions could hit Apple's supply chain and China sales, valuation looks stretched at P/E 37.8 and P/B 62.2, liquidity is tight with current ratio 0.867 < 1, insiders sold a lot (Kondo 4,130 shares, Levinson 150,000 shares over two trades), and social sentiment turned negative on Nov 12 (−0.201) and Nov 4 (−0.141).

The Trader never sees the shouting match — only the facilitator's final structured note: "bullish, bearish, or mixed, with reasons." The same debate trick is used later by the Risk Team, but with three voices (aggressive, neutral, safe) instead of two.

In the lab below, you get to be the facilitator. Set how strong each side's evidence is, add rounds, and watch how n rounds turn a noisy argument into a clear recommendation.

Try this: start with the "Close call" tie — confidence stays low even after 4 rounds. Then drag to "Strong bull" or "Strong bear" and add rounds — notice confidence climbs and the final BUY / SELL label flips. That is the debate doing its job.

0055 | From Ideas to Action — Safely~9 min

Don't Play Telephone With Millions of Dollars

Pass a whispered number down a long line of people and it comes out wrong — that's the children's game of telephone. Now imagine that whispered number is “Apple earned $2.15 per share, beating expectations” and millions of dollars ride on it. A single garbled detail can turn a good trade into a bad one.

Earlier AI traders often worked like that long line: every agent just appended messages to one giant chat history. The paper calls the result the telephone effect. Over many turns, old facts get buried, twisted, or forgotten because of context limits. TradingAgents fixes it with a simple rule: structured reports first, short chats second.

Analysts write concise, structured reports with key numbers and recommendations into a shared global state. Other agents query only the fields they need — like calling get_YFin_data or get_EODHD_news — instead of re-reading the whole chat. Natural language is saved for short, focused debates (n rounds, guided by a facilitator) and the result is logged back as a structured entry. That keeps relational details intact.

After the analysts and bull-vs-bear researchers have filed their work, the Trader steps in — this is Figure 4. The Trader reviews all four analyst reports, the debate winner, and price history, then decides the practical question: buy, sell, or hold? how much? when? It can place orders and adjust the portfolio. Crucially, it uses the ReAct pattern — Reason + Act — writing a thought, calling a tool, reading the result, thinking again — so every decision leaves a readable trace.

Before any money moves, the Risk Management Team challenges the Trader. Three characters debate the same plan from different risk appetites:

🚀

Aggressive

Sees overbought RSI/CCI as strong momentum. “Ride it — higher return justifies more risk.” Wants bigger position, looser stops.

⚖️

Neutral

Balanced. “Take some upside but hedge.” Suggests stop-loss orders, options hedges, dollar-cost averaging, diversification.

🛡️

Safe

Conservative. Worries about high valuation, volatility (ATR), and drawdown. “Wait for a clearer signal, protect capital first.”

After its n-round debate, a facilitator synthesizes the views. Then the Fund Manager reviews the risk discussion, sets the final risk adjustment, updates the Trader's decision in the protocol, and approves execution. Because everything was done with structured fields plus ReAct logs, a human can later audit why the trade happened — what data was fetched, what argument won, and what risk guardrail was applied — unlike a black-box deep-learning model.

In the lab below, see both halves: first, drag the chat chain longer and watch a whispered earnings number mutate — then flip to structured reports and watch fidelity stay high. Then switch between Aggressive, Neutral and Safe to see how position size and stop-loss change the same trade.

0066 | Fast Brain, Slow Brain~7 min

Fast Brains and Slow Brains

Think of a real trading desk. An intern is great at fetching things fast. A senior analyst is slower, more expensive, but much better at connecting the dots. TradingAgents uses AI the same way — with two kinds of brains.

The paper calls them quick-thinking and deep-thinking models. Quick-thinking means models like gpt-4o-mini and gpt-4o. They are fast and cheap at shallow jobs: summarizing a news article, calling an API like get_YFin_data, or turning a table of prices into a sentence. Deep-thinking means models like o1-preview. They are slower and cost more, but they can do multi-step reasoning, write evidence-based reports, and weigh a bull case against a bear case.

⚡

Fast Brain

For retrieval & summarizing. Example: fetch Reddit posts, fetch prices, turn numbers into text. Cheap and quick.

🧠

Slow Brain

For analysis & decisions. Example: write the fundamentals report, run the n-round bull-bear debate, choose buy/sell/hold timing and size.

So where does each brain work? In TradingAgents all analyst nodes use deep models to do the actual analysis, but they hand off the fetching to quick models to save time. Researchers and Traders use deep models for their insights and final decisions. There are also small auxiliary expert models just for scoring sentiment.

Why split it? You get the best of both: speed where you can, depth where it counts. And you don't need a GPU farm — the whole firm runs on API credits. If a better model appears next year, you just swap it in for any agent. The paper calls this plug-and-play and future-proof.

In a simplified way, a job on the wrong brain hurts. Put a quick brain in charge of the debate and it misses nuance. Put a slow brain on fetching prices and you just burn money and time for no extra quality.

Try it in the lab: assign each of the 6 jobs to a Fast or Slow brain and watch cost, time, and decision quality change. Can you find the paper's sweet spot — fast for fetch, slow for thinking?

0077 | Beating the Market~9 min

Did the AI Firm Beat the Market?

How do you prove an AI trading firm isn't just lucky? You put it in a time machine. The paper rewinds to Jan 1 – Mar 29, 2024 and makes TradingAgents trade day by day on big tech stocks — Apple, Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, Google — with a strict rule: on each trading day it can only see data up to that day. No peeking ahead. That's called avoiding look-ahead bias. After it decides to buy, sell, or hold, the researchers calculate its scores, then move to the next day.

For each stock the dataset is intentionally messy and realistic: daily open/high/low/close prices and volume, daily news from Bloomberg, Yahoo, EODHD, FinnHub and Reddit, social posts and sentiment scores, insider transactions from filings, company financial statements, and 60 technical indicators like RSI, MACD and Bollinger Bands. If a human would have to juggle it, the AI firm has to juggle it too.

Then they compare TradingAgents to the same old playbook everyone knows: Buy & Hold (just buy and wait), MACD and SMA (follow trend crossovers), KDJ+RSI (buy oversold, sell overbought), and ZMR (bet prices snap back to average). Same prices, same dates, same four scoreboard metrics you learned.

The headline numbers come from Table 1 on three stocks the paper shows in detail. Roughly in plain words: TradingAgents made at least 23.2% total profit in under 3 months with a yearly pace of at least 24.9%, beating the best baseline on each stock by ≥6.1%.

Zoom in on Apple (AAPL) — the hardest test because prices were choppy. TradingAgents: CR 26.62%, ARR 30.50%, Sharpe 8.21, MDD 0.91%. The best baseline on AAPL was KDJ&RSI with only 2.05% CR, Sharpe 1.64, MDD 1.09%, while Buy & Hold actually lost –5.23%. That's a +24.57% improvement in cumulative return. On Google (GOOGL) TradingAgents made 24.36% (vs 7.78% for Buy & Hold, +16.58% lead) with Sharpe 6.39 and MDD 1.69%. On Amazon (AMZN) it made 23.21% (vs 17.10% for Buy & Hold, +6.10% lead) with Sharpe 5.60 and MDD 2.11%. In all three, Sharpe was roughly double or more the next best — meaning more profit per wobble.

What about risk? Some rule-based baselines had tiny maximum drawdowns — for example ZMR on AAPL only fell 0.86% from a peak vs 0.91% for TradingAgents — but they did it by barely making money. The paper's point is the trade-off: high return usually means you lived through scary dips, but TradingAgents kept its worst dip at or below about 2% on these stocks despite those high returns. The authors credit the Risk Management Team debates: the aggressive, neutral and safe agents argue and the fund manager trims the bet before it executes.

Beyond the numbers, there's an audit trail. Unlike a black-box deep network, TradingAgents logs its ReAct thoughts and tool calls — "I fetched prices, computed RSI, read this news, here's why I vote buy" — so a human can debug it. The code is released at github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents, and future work aims at live trading and real-time feeds. The paper is careful: this is still a backtest on history, not proof it will always win tomorrow.

Now try it yourself in the lab below: pick AAPL, GOOGL or AMZN and toggle the five baselines on and off. Watch three things: where the cumulative-return line ends (who made more), how tall the Sharpe bars are (who was smoother), and how small the drawdown dip needs to be to stay profitable.

008Review~6 min

Recap: Your Turn to Build the Firm

Imagine you want to open a tiny Wall Street firm with robots. You learned the recipe in pieces — now let's put the whole assembly line together and see why it matters.

Think of it like a relay race with five handoffs:

  1. I. Four analysts run at once. Fundamental reads earnings and insider sales, Sentiment reads Reddit/X mood, News reads Bloomberg/Yahoo macro news, Technical runs code to compute 60 chart signals like RSI and MACD. Each writes a short structured report.
  2. II. Researchers debate. A bull robot argues growth and moat, a bear robot argues overvaluation, China risk, and leverage. A facilitator listens for n rounds and saves the winning view as a clean note.
  3. III. Trader decides — buy, sell, or hold, and how much — by weighing those reports plus past history.
  4. IV. Risk guardians argue again from three lenses — aggressive, neutral, and safe — and suggest stop-losses or smaller size.
  5. V. Fund manager checks the risk notes, updates the trader's ticket, and executes.
The paper's big fix wasn't just more data. It was structure + short debate. Long chat-only histories cause a "telephone effect" where facts warp over many turns. Structured reports that agents can query keep the signal clean, while brief natural-language debates add judgment where it counts.

How do you know the firm worked? The same scoreboard from Lesson 2: raw profit, profit per wobble, and worst dip.

$$\text{CR}=\frac{V_{end}-V_{start}}{V_{start}}\times 100\%\quad\;\;\text{SR}=\frac{\bar R-R_f}{\sigma}\quad\;\;\text{MDD}=\max\frac{\text{Peak}-\text{Trough}}{\text{Peak}}\times 100\%$$

Cumulative Return (CR) is total gain, Annual Return stretches it to a yearly rate, Sharpe (SR) is return per unit of bumpiness, and Maximum Drawdown (MDD) is the deepest fall you'd have to stomach. In the paper's 3-month backtest (Jan 1 to Mar 29, 2024, on Apple, Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, Google — no peeking at the future), this firm scored roughly 23% to 27% CR with Sharpe around 5 to 8 and MDD under 2%, well above the best single-signal rules like Buy-and-Hold, MACD, KDJ+RSI, ZMR, and SMA. For Apple alone it was 26.62% versus 2.05% for the next-best baseline.

Beyond the numbers, there's a human win: because every agent uses ReAct — write a thought, call a tool like get_YFin_data, see the result, think again — the trade comes with a readable log traders can audit and tune, not a black box.

🔄

When specialization helps

More analysts help when signals conflict — earnings say buy but news says risk. Alone, one bot overreacts. Together, they balance.

⚖️

When debate and risk help

A few bull-vs-bear rounds surface blind spots; three risk views stop reckless size. Too little debate misses risk, too much just adds noise and cost.

Fast brains versus slow brains seal the deal: quick models fetch and summarize cheaply, deep models do the heavy reasoning. You can swap in better LLMs later — no GPU needed, just API credits. Future work in the paper points to live trading, more roles, and real-time feeds. ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Large Language Model (LLM)

A large AI trained on huge amounts of text that can read, write, summarize, and reason with language.

Agent

An AI with a specific job, goal, tools, and rules — like a character in a company (e.g., news analyst, trader).

Fundamental analysis

Studying a company's real business health: earnings reports, sales, profits, debt, insider trades to estimate its true value.

Sentiment analysis

Measuring how optimistic or pessimistic people sound online — in news, Reddit, X/Twitter — to guess short-term price moves.

Technical indicator

Math formulas from past prices and volume that hint at momentum or trend, like MACD, RSI, Bollinger Bands, ATR, ADX, CCI.

Backtesting

A way to test a trading idea on old data, day by day, without peeking ahead. The paper uses Jan 1 - Mar 29, 2024.

Look-ahead bias

Cheating by using future information you wouldn't have had at the time. TradingAgents avoids this by only using data up to each trading day.

Cumulative Return (CR)

((End value - Start value) / Start value) ×100%. Total profit over the whole test.

Annualized Return (AR)

Cumulative return stretched or shrunk to a yearly rate: ((Vend/Vstart)^(1/years)-1)×100%. Lets you compare short and long tests.

Sharpe Ratio (SR)

(Average return - risk-free return) / volatility. High Sharpe = good profit for the risk taken. Low/negative = risky or unrewarding.

Maximum Drawdown (MDD)

The biggest fall from a peak to a later trough, as %. E.g., 2% means you at most lost 2% from a high before recovering.

Bull vs Bear

Optimists who argue a stock will go up vs pessimists who argue it will go down. TradingAgents makes them debate.

Debate / dialectical process

The group's process of proposing evidence, countering, and letting a facilitator pick the stronger side after n rounds.

Telephone effect

Like the game of telephone: when agents only pass long chat histories, details get lost or twisted over time.

ReAct framework

A prompting trick (Reason + Act) where the agent writes its thought, calls a tool (search, code, price API), sees the result, and thinks again.

Risk Management Team

In TradingAgents: three debaters — risk-seeking (aggressive), neutral, and risk-conservative (safe) — plus a fund manager who approves the final trade.
