001The problem~6 min

Why making pictures from pure pixels is so hard

Imagine you have to copy a photo dot-by-dot onto graph paper. Now imagine a shortcut: someone first shrinks the photo to a tiny sticky note that says “orange cat on couch,” and you only have to copy the note. The second job is much easier — but you can never get the whiskers back.

Most modern image generators, like those built on a VAE latent space (Rombach et al. 2022), take that shortcut. A separate compressor squeezes raw RGB pixels into a small, tidy “latent” world, and diffusion happens there. It is efficient, but the compressor decides what detail to throw away.

This paper asks: what if you are not allowed to cheat? In pixel diffusion transformers (pDiTs), the model starts from pure noise and must predict real pixels end-to-end. Representation learning — how the model turns those noisy patches into internal features — and pixel synthesis happen together, in one network.

The authors use a beautifully simple baseline called JiT (Li & He 2026): just cut the noisy image into patches, turn each patch into a token, and run a plain Transformer — the same kind of blocks you find in language models, with no special pixel gadgets. JiT showed this plain setup can already be competitive for pixel generation. That makes it the perfect clean lab bench: if the picture gets better or worse, we know it is because of the representation, not a fancy new part.

Why this is hard: recognition can throw away detail once it knows “that is a cat.” Generation cannot. It must do both — organize global meaning and spatial layout and rebuild every single pixel perfectly.

So the paper tracks one question through the whole course:

What makes a representation actually useful for making pictures — not just good at understanding them?

It is not enough for the middle of the network to look smart. That smartness has to survive all the way to the final pixel, and the network has to actually use it. We will see in the next lessons that it often does not.

002How models learn~7 min

How a flow model learns to undo noise

Imagine you have a perfect photo and a TV full of static. Now slide a fader between them. That's exactly what a flow model trains on.

Take any clean image x — every pixel's real color — and a sheet of random noise ε drawn from N(0,I), which just means each pixel is a random number centered at zero. Pick a time t between 0 and 1. The model never sees the clean image alone. It only sees the mix:

$$ \mathbf{z}_t = t\,\mathbf{x} + (1-t)\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon} $$

When t=0, zt = ε — pure noise. When t=1, zt = x — the original photo. In between, say t=0.5, you get a 50/50 blend. During training t is sampled from a logit-normal distribution (just a way to spend more training time on the interesting middle values, not only the extremes).

Which way should the model move to clean it up? The answer is the straight arrow from noise to image:

$$ \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon} $$

This v is called the velocity. If you're at the noisy mix, adding a little bit of v pushes you toward the clean image.

The learning task is simple to state: see the mix zt, guess the arrow v that would undo the noise.

The network doesn't output v directly. Following JiT, it predicts the clean image xθ(zt, t, c) — "θ" just means "the network's weights", and c is an optional condition like a class label "cat". That guess is turned into a velocity prediction:

$$ \mathbf{v}_{\theta} = \frac{\mathbf{x}_{\theta} - \mathbf{z}_t}{1-t} $$

Training compares this guess to the true arrow with a squared error, averaged over many images, noises, and times:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \mathbb{E}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{v}\|_2^2 $$

In practice this is the same as penalizing xθ vs x, but weighted by 1/(1-t)² (clipped so it doesn't explode near t=1). Smaller loss means the predicted arrow points more like the true arrow.

At sampling time — when we actually want a new picture — we start from pure noise and repeatedly follow the model's predicted vθ toward clean. To make images match the prompt better, people use classifier-free guidance (CFG). They ask the model twice: once without the condition (∅, "no label") and once with it (c), then exaggerate the difference:

$$ \mathbf{v}^{w}_{\theta} = \mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\varnothing) + w\,(\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\mathbf{c})-\mathbf{v}_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_t,t,\varnothing)) $$

With w > 1 this samples from a sharpened distribution p(zt)p(c|zt)w — "stronger cat-ness". Crucially, CFG only happens during sampling. The model was trained normally.

The paper's new idea, Representation Guidance, flips this: instead of guiding only at sampling, it changes the training target v itself to force the model to rely on its own internal representation. You'll build that mechanism in the next lessons.

Try it: drag t to slide between noise and image and watch the true red arrow v. Adjust model skill to see how the orange guess vθ and loss change, then crank up w to feel how CFG stretches the arrow only at sampling — before we make training itself listen.

003Insight 1~7 min

Does a better 'understanding' make a better generator?

Imagine an assembly line that builds a picture pixel by pixel. In the middle of the line you send in a substitute teacher who is brilliant at understanding images — a model called DINOv2 that is great at spotting objects and layout. The question is: will the workers after the teacher actually keep using what the teacher taught?

The paper tests this with a trick called REPA. REPA adds an extra loss that forces one middle layer of the plain Transformer (called JiT-B/16) to look more like DINOv2. They try it at layer 4, layer 6, and layer 8, and train each version for 200 epochs on ImageNet at $256\times256$. To see if the understanding stuck, they measure two things at every layer: spatial structure (RMSC is how different patch tokens are from each other, LDS is how similar nearby patches are versus far patches) and global semantics (linear probing — freeze the layer, train a tiny classifier on top, check accuracy).

At the forced layer itself, it worked. Linear probing jumps way up, and RMSC/LDS get stronger — the model really does look more semantic right there. But the very next layers wash it away. As the network gets closer to spitting out pixels, those gains diminish immediately. The later layers have a different job: do dense, high-frequency pixel work, and they overwrite the fancy semantics to do it.

That fade shows up in the final pictures too. FID is the main quality score here — lower is better. After 200 epochs the plain baseline without any teacher is 4.56. With REPA at layer 4 it is worse at 4.80, at layer 6 it is 5.38, and at layer 8 — closest to the output — it collapses to 8.33. REPA-4 does learn faster at first, but after about 160 epochs the baseline overtakes it. Pushing the alignment deeper only hurts more.

Insight 1: Better representation quality does not automatically mean better generation. If later layers aren't forced to keep and use the understanding, they will just paint over it to make pixels. The trick is not only to make a good representation, but to make it stay useful all the way to the output.

The authors call this a kind of lazy shortcut: when every pixel gives you a loss signal, the network can satisfy it with local tricks and feels no pressure to keep high-level structure unless you make it.

Watch the probing-accuracy bump appear at your chosen layer and then fade in the layers after it, and watch the FID curves — notice how an early lead turns into a worse final score, especially when the tutor is placed near the end.

004Insight 2~6 min

What if we add a specialist painter for the pixels?

Lesson 3 showed a puzzle: you can make a middle layer super smart by copying a famous vision model like DINOv2, but later layers just paint over it when they go back to predicting raw pixels. So maybe the fix is to split the job — one team for understanding, another team for painting?

That’s exactly what recent pixel models tried. They kept the top 8 blocks as an encoder for understanding and bolted on a pixel-specialized decoder for the last 4 blocks:

  • Vanilla: plain Transformer blocks — the same patch-to-patch attention the paper uses as its baseline.
  • PixelDiT: patch attention plus extra per-pixel little networks.
  • DiP: a convolutional net with down-and-up sampling and skip connections.
  • DeCo: a tiny per-pixel MLP that works inside each patch alone.

All four were trained the same way: same data, same 200 epochs on ImageNet 256×256, same REPA help at encoder layer 3, same clean-image prediction. The only difference is the decoder.

Here’s what happened, from Table 1 in the paper:

DecoderParamsComputeFID ↓ (lower is better)
Vanilla131 M28.4 G4.17
PixelDiT203 M47.4 G4.26
DiP94 M33.7 G6.09
DeCo100 M25.8 G9.17

PixelDiT used 55% more parameters and 67% more compute than Vanilla for essentially the same score. DiP and DeCo were worse. No specialist consistently helped.

The key insight: the decoder doesn’t just use the encoder’s representation — it changes what the encoder has to keep. If your painter can already handle layout across patches (Vanilla, PixelDiT), the encoder can relax and keep more global meaning. If your painter works patch-by-patch alone (DiP, DeCo), the encoder is forced to memorize spatial layout and loses global meaning. It’s not improvement, it’s redistribution.

You can see that trade-off in the paper’s measurements. Vanilla and PixelDiT keep stronger global semantics — a linear probe on their last encoder layer classifies ImageNet better — but weaker spatial variety. DiP and DeCo are the opposite: stronger spatial scores (RMSC/LDS) but weaker semantics. Different decoders, different balance, same ceiling.

That’s Insight 2 from the paper: architectural separation mainly redistributes semantic and spatial modeling, without yielding a representation better suited to generation — and it breaks the simple, scalable plain-Transformer design that made JiT attractive.

So if neither adding external smarts nor hiring a specialist painter works, what will? The paper argues we need to fix both sides at the interface: force the model to form a compact, useful note, and force it to actually read it. 005Solution part 1~7 min

Forcing the model to write a tiny, useful note

Last time we saw that adding a fancy pixel specialist didn't help — it just shuffled where the model kept "what's this?" versus "where are the edges?". The paper tries a simpler idea: keep the plain Transformer, but force it to talk to itself through a keyhole.

Imagine an encoder that looks at the noisy image and a decoder that has to paint the clean one. In RG they are two separate stacks of normal Transformer blocks. The decoder gets its own patch embedding of the noisy image, so it can already see pixels. The only extra thing it gets from the encoder is a short note called r, used as a token-wise adaLN condition — basically a dial that nudges every decoder token.

That note is forced through a squeezebox:

$$ \mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D} \;\to\; \mathbf{u}=\mathbf{h}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d} \;\to\; \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{W}_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D},\quad d \ll D $$

Here $N=256$ patches for a $256\times256$ image with patch size 16, $D$ is the wide hidden size (768 for B, 1024 for L, 1152 for XL), and $d$ is the narrow width of the note. $\mathbf{h}$ are the encoder's output tokens, $\mathbf{W}_{\text{down}}$ squeezes them down to $d$ numbers per patch, and $\mathbf{W}_{\text{up}}$ stretches them back to $D$ so the decoder can read them. The space of $\mathbf{u}$ is what the paper calls an implicit latent space.

Think "VAE latent, but inside." A normal latent diffusion model uses a separate autoencoder to make a tiny image-like code. RG's $\mathbf{u}$ is also compact and spatial, but it's computed from the noisy input $\mathbf{z}_t$ at every step and trained end-to-end for generation, all still in pixel space.

How narrow should the keyhole be? The paper tests RG-B/16 trained 200 epochs on ImageNet $256\times256$:

  • Width $d$: At 200 epochs $d=512$ looks best (FID about 3.18 vs 3.28 for $d=128$). At 600 epochs the ranking flips: $d=128$ wins with FID 2.64 vs 2.89 for $d=512$. A tighter note forces the model to keep only generation-useful structure, which pays off with longer training.
  • Encoder vs decoder split: With 12 blocks total, 8 encoder + 4 decoder gives FID 3.28. A shallow encoder (4+8) worsens to 3.60, and a tiny 2-block decoder (10+2) jumps to 4.19. You need a deep encoder to form good notes, but enough decoder to do high-frequency painting.
  • Where to align to DINOv2: Aligning encoder layer 3 or 6 gives similar FID ~3.3, but aligning the very last encoder layer (layer 8, right before the bottleneck) collapses to FID 8.97. Pretrained features aren't meant to be the final pixel note.

On its own the bottleneck is not magic, but it's cheap and necessary. Adding just the bottleneck to a REPA-guided encoder drops FID from 4.17 to 3.79, and the whole RG block adds only 0.4M parameters and about 13% FLOPs. Next we need the second half: how to make the decoder actually use that tiny note.

Try the squeezebox below: slide $d$ from 16 to 512. Watch the 16×16 patch note — narrow means clean semantic blocks, wide means noisy speckles creep back in. Notice how "detail kept" rises with $d$ but "semantic clarity" peaks near 128, matching that 600-epoch FID win.

006Solution part 2~8 min

Nudging the target so the decoder must listen

Last time we squeezed the model's understanding through a keyhole — a tiny d=128 bottleneck — so it had to write a compact note to itself. But a note only helps if the decoder actually reads it. A lazy decoder could just look at the noisy pixels and ignore the note entirely.

This lesson is the second half of Representation Guidance: actively forcing the decoder to listen.

The trick is simple. During training, with 10% probability the paper randomly erases the note: set r = 0. The very same decoder then makes two predictions from the same noisy image zt:

  • vwith_rep = vθ(zt,t,r) — velocity prediction with the note
  • vwithout_rep = vθ(zt,t,∅) — velocity prediction without it (unconditional). The class label only reaches the decoder through r, so erasing r erases conditioning too.

Their difference isolates what the note actually contributed:

$$ \Delta v_{rep} = v_{with\_rep} - v_{without\_rep} $$

RG then nudges the training target a little further in that direction:

$$ v_{RG} = v + (s-1)\, \mathrm{sg}(\Delta v_{rep}) $$

Here v = x - ε is the normal flow-matching target (clean minus noise), s ≥ 1 is the RG scale (paper uses s=1.4), and sg(...) means stop-gradient — treat the offset as a fixed arrow, don't back-propagate through it. The model is trained to match the new target: LRG = E || vθ(zt,t,r) - vRG ||2. When s=1 this is just the normal loss.

What does the nudge do at convergence? The math (Appendix A.2, Eq. 7) shows the network learns to amplify the note's effect by 1/(2-s). For s=1.4, that's about 1.67×. Informally this is like sampling from a sharpened distribution p(zt) p(r|zt)w with w = 1/(2-s) — similar to classifier-free guidance, but where the learned representation is the condition. Gradients also flow through r into the encoder, so the note itself gets shaped to be more useful for denoising.

This only works if the note is well-formed. On B/16 at 200 epochs, RG alone without REPA and bottleneck raises FID to 7.85–8.99 (Table 8 — much worse than the 4.56 JiT baseline). With both REPA scaffold and bottleneck, adding guidance drops FID from 3.79 to 3.28, and to 2.64 at 600 epochs. In other words: guidance amplifies whatever is in the note — helpful structure or junk.

Two practical details make it stable:

  • Use a stable teacher: the offset is computed with the EMA (slow-moving average) network — two extra forward passes with no gradients, zero cost at inference.
  • Skip very noisy steps: guidance is only applied for t in (0.3, 1]. A representation extracted from almost pure noise is unreliable and just injects noise — applying RG at all noise levels raises FID to 5.28 (Fig. 9b). Warm-up of 20 epochs before turning RG on helps a little but barely matters.

Overall loss is L = LRG + 0.5 LREPA. The decoder now must rely on the note to hit its nudged target — and the encoder learns to write a better one.

007What changed inside?~7 min

Does the inside actually look better?

Earlier we saw that making an intermediate layer "smarter" doesn't guarantee a better picture. So how do we know if the inside of the RG model actually got better? The paper looks inside layer by layer, like lifting floors of a building.

Three simple rulers are used, all on ImageNet's 50K validation images. Think of an image cut into 16×16 = 256 little patch tokens:

  • RMSC — how distinct the patches are from each other. High means different spots look different.
  • LDS — local vs distant similarity: are neighbours similar but far-away patches different? High means good spatial layout.
  • Linear probing — freeze the features, train only a tiny classifier on top, and see how well you can name the object. Higher means stronger global semantics.

Scores are averaged over noisy inputs from $t=0.1$ to $0.9$ for RMSC/LDS, and over clean images for probing. At noisy $t=0.5$ the paper also paints each token's features with PCA colours: the first three principal directions become red, green and blue. A clear object silhouette in those colours means the layer has organized meaning; speckled snow means it is just doing fine pixel static.

What the pictures show: the baseline JiT-B is speckled everywhere, with unclear contours. JiT + REPA at layer 4 gets a sharp object map near that layer, but layers after it drift back to snow. RG-B is different: encoder layers 3–8 keep clear semantic contours and a clean foreground-background split, while decoder layers 9–12 turn clearly high-frequency. The network has split jobs.

Numbers make it concrete for the 131M base models at 200 epochs. At the last encoder layer (layer 8) RG-B reaches RMSC 0.858 versus JiT's 0.476, and LDS 0.39 versus 0.111. Its probing accuracy stays 52% to 55.2% across encoder layers 3–8, nearly double JiT's peak of 29.5%. JiT + REPA does spike to 60.7% at its aligned layer 4, but falls all the way to 30.8% by layer 12. In RG-B the decoder deliberately drops to about 22.7% to 29.2% — it no longer needs to remember "cat", it just paints fur.

That split is no accident. Removing the bottleneck hurts spatial diversity at layer 8; adding it back restores it. Adding Representation Guidance on top then lifts global semantics and sharpens the encoder-decoder divide. The payoff in training speed is large: RG-B matches the 600-epoch quality of JiT-B (FID 3.66) in only 150 epochs — about a 4× speedup — and keeps improving to FID 2.64 at 600 epochs.

Watch the synthetic feature map switch from object shape to static, and watch the three bars rise, spike, or fade — that's the difference between a representation that looks smart for a moment and one the generator actually keeps until it has to use it.

008Payoff~6 min

So did it actually work?

So the model learned a compact note and was nudged to read it. Did the pictures actually get better — not just prettier inside, but on the scoreboard?

On ImageNet 256×256 the answer is yes, and the scaling is the clue. RG-XL/16 with 725M parameters got FID 1.55 at 600 epochs — the best FID among all pixel-space models in the paper. More striking: RG-L/16 with only 459M got FID 1.79 and beat much larger models like JiT-G/16 (2,000M, FID 1.82) and PixelREPA-H/16 (953M, FID 1.81). In other words, a smaller plain Transformer with the right note beat giants with fancier decoders.

That held when the image got bigger too. At 512×512 (same patch size 16, so now 32×32 tokens per image, 4× more work) RG-XL/16 reached FID 1.74 — again best in pixel-space. For comparison, latent-space models that get help from a separate VAE compressor still lead overall (RAE-XL 1.13, DDT-XL 1.26), but RG narrows that gap without using a VAE at all.

The same recipe worked for text prompts. RG-T2I-L/16 kept the same plain blocks, swapped in MM-JiT blocks for text, and used a frozen FLAN-T5-Large text encoder (341M) — much smaller than the 2.6B and 1.7B encoders in PixelDiT-T2I and DeCo. After pre-training on CC12M and fine-tuning on a 120K mix, it scored 0.89 on GenEval and 87.1 on DPG-Bench at 512×512, outperforming larger latent models like SD3 (8B) and FLUX.1-dev (12B) in the paper's table. The team even fine-tuned it to 1024×1024 (samples in the figure below).

The takeaway the paper wants you to remember: a representation helps generation only when it is both properly formed — squeezed through a bottleneck into a compact, implicit latent, with REPA as a scaffold — and actively exploited, by shifting the flow target along its own offset ($v_{RG} = v + (s-1)\,\Delta v_{rep}$). Neither alone worked (guidance without bottleneck hit FID 7.85–8.99). No specialist pixel decoder needed.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Pixel-space generation

Generating images directly from raw RGB pixels instead of from a compressed latent made by a separate autoencoder. Harder because every tiny detail must be predicted.

Diffusion Transformer (pDiT / JiT)

A plain Transformer that works on image patches. It turns a noisy image into tokens, processes them, and predicts the clean image.

Flow matching

Training idea used in the paper: start from noise ε, mix with clean image x to get z_t = t·x + (1-t)·ε, and teach the model to predict the direction v = x - ε that moves noise toward the image.

Representation

The internal patch-features the model builds. Measured in the paper with RMSC/LDS (spatial structure) and linear probing accuracy (semantics).

REPA alignment

Method that forces an intermediate layer to look similar to features from a pretrained vision model like DINOv2. In the paper REPA improves the aligned layer but its benefit fades in later layers.

Bottleneck

A low-dimensional layer that squeezes features h (size D) to u (size d << D) and back to r. Forces the model to keep only the most useful information.

Representation Guidance (RG)

The paper's second trick: compare prediction with representation v_with_rep and without v_without_rep, get offset Δv_rep, and push the training target a bit further in that direction: v_RG = v + (s-1)·Δv_rep with s=1.4.

FID

Féchet Inception Distance: a score for generated images vs real images. Lower is better. The paper reports FID-50K. IS (Inception Score) higher is better.

Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

At sampling time, mixing conditional and unconditional predictions to make images match the class or text prompt more strongly: v_w = v_uncond + w·(v_cond - v_uncond).

RMSC / LDS / Linear probing

Ways to measure representations: RMSC = how distinct different patch tokens are; LDS = how similar nearby patches are vs distant patches; linear probing = train a simple classifier on frozen features to test semantics.
