001The bottleneck~6 min

Why 5G towers need constant feedback to aim.

Imagine trying to chat with a friend across a noisy gymnasium. Shouting in every direction wastes your voice, while aiming a narrow megaphone straight at them makes every word clear. Modern 5G cell towers—called base stations (BS)—do the same thing with radio waves. By packing dozens of small antennas onto one panel, a technique called large-scale MIMO (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output), a tower can aim focused beams straight at your phone and even send multiple independent data streams at the same time.

There is a catch: radio waves bounce off walls, buildings, and furniture before reaching you. In frequency division duplex (FDD) systems like 5G New Radio (NR), the tower sends data down to your phone on one frequency, and your phone replies on a different frequency. Because the airwaves behave differently at those two frequencies, the tower cannot guess the path your signal took. Your phone—called the user equipment (UE)—must measure the incoming signal to capture Channel State Information (CSI) and send steering instructions back up to the tower.

Instead of mailing back a giant raw map of every signal bounce across every frequency, 5G phones condense those measurements into a compact table of numbers called a precoding matrix, written as $\mathbf{W}$:

$$\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \dots, \mathbf{w}_{N_{\text{s}}}]$$

Each column $\mathbf{w}_s$ is a list of weights—one for each of the tower's $N_{\text{t}}$ transmitting antennas—that steers the $s$-th data stream (out of $N_{\text{s}}$ total streams) toward the phone's $N_{\text{r}}$ receiving antennas. Think of $\mathbf{W}$ as a timing and volume recipe so all the tower's antennas add up cleanly right where you are standing.

To send $\mathbf{W}$ without clogging the airwaves, standard 5G NR uses a Type II codebook—essentially a shared menu of preset beam patterns. Rather than describing a custom shape from scratch, the phone picks up to $N = 4$ preset beams from the menu and reports how to blend them. In the paper's real-world 5G measurements, accuracy is measured by cosine similarity ($\rho$), where $1.00$ means a perfect match to ideal steering:

📡

2 beams (41 bits)

Lightest menu option, but real-world match drops to $\rho = 0.72$.

🎯

3 beams (58 bits)

Stronger aiming ($\rho = 0.86$), at a moderate bit cost.

💎

4 beams (80 bits)

Near-ideal aiming ($\rho = 0.96$), but costs 80 bits every report.

The bottleneck: As 5G and future 6G towers add more antennas, beams get narrower and preset menus get heavier—forcing a painful tradeoff between blurry aiming and bloated feedback. Deep learning promises to compress $\mathbf{W}$ into far fewer bits, raising the central question of this study: how do you train that AI without disrupting live networks to gather massive real-world datasets?

002AI compression~7 min

Squeezing beam instructions into 32 bits.

Sending back every raw radio measurement across all 1,620 frequency slices (subcarriers) would flood the wireless uplink. Instead, 5G systems use implicit feedback: the phone extracts only the strongest steering directions—called dominant eigenvectors—and packs them into a precoding matrix $\mathbf{W}$:

$$\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_1, \mathbf{w}_2, \dots, \mathbf{w}_{N_{\text{s}}}]$$

Here $N_{\text{s}}$ is the number of simultaneous data streams ($N_{\text{s}} = 2$ in this study), and each column $\mathbf{w}_s$ is the list of antenna weights aiming stream $s$.

Even $\mathbf{W}$ is too bulky to send raw, so the researchers use an AI model called EVCsiNet. It is an autoencoder—two neural networks trained as a team, split right across the air between your phone and the base station. First, the phone separates the complex antenna numbers into their regular (real) and sideways (imaginary) parts, then runs three steps:

📱

Encoder at the phone

Network $f_{\text{e}}$ squeezes $\mathbf{W}$ down into a short list of $V$ numbers.

🪜

Quantizer in the middle

Step $Q$ rounds each number onto a staircase of $B$-bit digital levels.

📡

Decoder at the tower

Network $f_{\text{d}}$ rebuilds the steering matrix $\mathbf{W}$ from those bits.

Putting all three steps together gives the paper's autoencoder equation:

$$f_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{W};\Theta) = f_{\text{d}}\big(Q(f_{\text{e}}(\mathbf{W};\Theta_{\mathrm{E}}));\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}\big)$$

In this formula, $\Theta_{\mathrm{E}}$ and $\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}$ stand for the learned internal weights of the encoder and decoder, grouped as $\Theta = (\Theta_{\mathrm{E}}, \Theta_{\mathrm{D}})$. During training, the AI adjusts $\Theta$ to shrink the mean square error (MSE)—the average squared gap between the original $\mathbf{W}$ and the rebuilt output $f_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{W};\Theta)$.

Because each of the $V$ compressed numbers is rounded to $B$ bits ($2^B$ possible levels), the total feedback size is simply $V \times B$ bits. In this paper, EVCsiNet uses a compression ratio of 0.5 to pack $\mathbf{W}$ into a 16-number codeword ($V = 16$) at 2 bits per number ($B = 2$). That is $16 \times 2 = 32$ bits total—far leaner than the 41 to 80 bits required by 5G Type II codebooks.

Why 2 bits still works: A 2-bit quantizer allows only four discrete values per number. Because the encoder learns to pack the most important directional clues into those 16 numbers before rounding, the decoder can still aim the beam accurately.

Try the lab below to push an 8-antenna steering signal through this encoder, quantizer, and decoder yourself. Start at the paper's 32-bit setting ($V = 16$, $B = 2$), then drop $B$ down to 1 bit or switch to the multi-bounce hallway to see how aggressive rounding and extra reflections affect the rebuilt beam and similarity score $\rho$.

003Virtual worlds~7 min

Building a virtual twin of a real hallway.

Training an AI autoencoder like EVCsiNet takes thousands of channel examples from all over a building. Shutting down a live 5G hallway to walk a receiver into every corner is slow and disrupts real users. Instead, the researchers built a Digital Twin (DT)—a virtual 3D replica of their real test site where radio waves can be simulated safely on a computer.

Their real-world testbed was a hallway at National Sun Yat-sen University (NSYSU), measuring $19.7\text{ m}$ long, $5.93\text{ m}$ wide, and $2.8\text{ m}$ high. Across this corridor, a base station transmitted a $3.8\text{ GHz}$ 5G signal spread over $100\text{ MHz}$ of bandwidth—sliced into $N_{\text{c}} = 1{,}620$ narrow frequency channels called subcarriers. Any real wireless channel $\mathcal{H}$ depends on three things working together: the physical environment $\mathcal{E}$ (walls and obstacles), the antenna hardware $\mathcal{A}$, and the rules of wave physics $g$:

$$\mathcal{H} = g(\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{A})$$

Because capturing every real-world detail is hard, the team approximated $\mathcal{H}$ with a virtual channel $\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}$ built from three matching ingredients:

📐

3D Room Model ($\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}$)

A scaled 3D hallway recording exact wall positions, shapes, and material traits like conductivity that decide how strongly surfaces bounce waves.

📡

Measured Antennas ($\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}$)

The real $15\text{ cm} \times 15\text{ cm}$ base-station and $7\text{ cm} \times 15\text{ cm}$ user patch antennas were measured in an echo-free chamber to capture their exact beam patterns.

🔦

Ray Tracing ($\tilde{g}$)

Using Wireless InSite software, virtual rays are shot from transmitter to receiver to track direct paths and wall reflections across $0.5\text{ m}$ grid spots and $100$ random phone orientations.

Why does a single hallway look different at each of the $1{,}620$ subcarriers? Because radio waves bounce. Suppose $L_{\text{h}}$ distinct rays reach the phone. Each path $l$ leaves the base station at an Angle of Departure ($\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{AoD}}_l$), arrives at an Angle of Arrival ($\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{AoA}}_l$), picks up a strength matrix $\mathbf{G}$ from the antennas and walls, and takes a travel time (delay) $\tau_l$. Summing every path gives the virtual channel at subcarrier $n$:

$$\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{n} = \sum_{l=1}^{L_{\text{h}}} \mathbf{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{l}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{l})\, e^{-j2\pi \frac{n}{N_{\text{c}}} \tau_{l}}$$

Here, the factor $e^{-j2\pi \frac{n}{N_{\text{c}}} \tau_l}$ is a compact wave tracker: it tells us whether path $l$ lands on a wave crest or a trough at frequency slice $n$. Longer wall bounces have larger delays $\tau_l$, so as you step across the $1{,}620$ subcarriers, two paths can line up and boost the signal on one frequency, then cancel each other out a few subcarriers later.

Going further: Alongside the indoor corridor, the team also built an outdoor NSYSU campus twin with the base station $50\text{ m}$ above a building and users spaced every $2\text{ m}$. Even with chamber-measured antennas, ray tracing simplifies messy real-world details like tiny wall roughness and ambient noise—which becomes crucial when testing the twin against reality.

004Reality check~6 min

Does the digital twin actually match reality?

Building a 3D digital twin is only useful if its virtual radio waves behave like real waves in the actual hallway. Before trusting their twin to train an AI, the researchers ran a reality check at five spots inside the NSYSU corridor: three in strong signal areas (A4, D4, and F4) and two in weaker corners (G and H).

To measure which way real waves arrived, the team swapped in direction-finding antennas—a vertical dipole transmitter and a 7-patch receiver array—and used signal phase differences to estimate each wave's Angle of Arrival (AoA). Every 3D arrival direction has two angles: a left-right compass angle called azimuth ($\theta$) and an up-down tilt called elevation ($\phi$). Turning those angles into a unit-length 3D arrow $\mathbf{u}(\phi, \theta)$, they compared the twin's predicted arrow against the real measured arrow $\mathbf{u}(\hat{\phi}, \hat{\theta})$ with an inner-product alignment score $\eta$:

$$\eta = \langle \mathbf{u}(\phi, \theta),\ \mathbf{u}(\hat{\phi}, \hat{\theta}) \rangle, \quad -1 \le \eta \le 1$$

Think of $\eta$ as how closely two arrows point the same way: $\eta = 1$ means a perfect match, $0$ means perpendicular, and $-1$ means opposite directions. Across all five corridor spots, the strongest (primary) wave path scored between $\eta = 0.95$ and $1.00$. Even the second-strongest bounced path scored between $0.85$ and $0.99$, proving the ray tracer captured the real walls and geometry accurately.

Because the AI compresses precoding matrices $\mathbf{W}$ rather than raw angles, the team also compared precoder cosine similarity across the entire corridor. In the maps below, both the digital twin and real measurements show the same diagonal bands of high similarity: spots lying along the same direction from the base station share similar steering instructions.

Close cousins, not clones: Even when main arrival angles align near $\eta = 1.0$, real hardware still introduces subtle antenna quirks, tiny surface bumps, and background noise that a 3D model misses. Those small mismatches mean virtual and real channels drift apart slightly.

005Closing the gap~7 min

Teaching the base station to adapt on the fly.

A digital twin can be remarkably accurate and still fall short in the real world. When the researchers tested their Indoor EVCsiNet model inside the virtual hallway it trained on, its beam-reconstruction similarity hit $\rho = 0.93$. When they dropped that exact same model into the real NSYSU corridor without any adjustments, accuracy fell to $\rho = 0.67$. Tiny differences in real walls, cables, and antenna hardware add up.

Throwing away the virtual training and starting from scratch with real measurements would defeat the whole point of a digital twin. Instead, the team used online learning (OL): fine-tuning the already-trained AI on a small trickle of real-world measurements so it adapts on the fly.

📱

Phone encoder: frozen

Keeping the encoder $f_{\text{e}}$ locked saves battery and computing power on the phone, and avoids sending new model weights back and forth over the air.

📡

Base station decoder: updated

Plugged into wall power with plenty of compute, the base station updates only its decoder $f_{\text{d}}$—learning how to translate the same 32-bit codes for real-world quirks.

How the base station fine-tunes itself

Starting from the digital-twin weights $\dot{\Theta}$, the base station holds the encoder fixed and searches for updated decoder weights $\ddot{\Theta}_{\mathrm{D}}$ that minimize the average reconstruction error $L(\cdot)$ on real precoding matrices $\mathbf{W}$:

$$\ddot{\Theta}_{\mathrm{D}} = \arg\min_{\Theta_{\mathrm{D}}} \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}\!\left\{L\!\left(\mathbf{W}, f_{\text{a}}(\mathbf{W}; \dot{\Theta})\right)\right\}$$

Here $\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{H}}$ means averaging over real-world channel measurements, and $f_{\text{a}}$ is the full encode-quantize-decode pipeline.

Gathering real measurements across a whole building takes time, so the team sampled only 30% of the 78 corridor points. To stretch that tiny dataset further, they split the signal's 1,620 subcarriers into 60 subbands of 27 consecutive frequency slices each. Because radio waves interfere differently at different frequencies, each subband produces its own precoding matrix $\mathbf{W}$—turning every single measurement spot into 60 distinct training examples.

That combination—decoder-only online learning plus subband splitting—lifted real-world similarity from $\rho = 0.67$ up to $\rho = 0.82$, without changing a single line of code on the phone. 006Stress testing~6 min

Why a dedicated twin still matters after fine-tuning.

Online learning at the base station is powerful, so a natural question comes up: do we really need to build a detailed 3D twin of every hallway and room? Couldn't we just train the AI on any wireless simulation—say, an outdoor campus or a generic textbook channel—and let online learning patch the rest?

To find out, the researchers tested three versions of EVCsiNet inside the real indoor corridor, each using the same 32-bit feedback budget:

🏢

Indoor DT

Trained on the custom 3D ray-traced model of the actual NSYSU hallway.

🌳

Outdoor DT

Trained on a 3D twin of the open NSYSU campus with the base station 50 m up.

📘

Generic CDL

Trained on a 3GPP Cluster Delay Line (CDL) model—a generic statistical recipe with no floorplan.

When tested in the real corridor without online learning, Indoor DT scored a cosine similarity of $\rho = 0.67$, Outdoor DT scored $0.64$, and generic CDL trailed at $0.44$. After fine-tuning the base station's decoder on 30% of the real-world measurement points, Indoor DT climbed to $\rho = 0.82$. Outdoor DT stalled at $0.75$ and generic CDL stalled at $0.74$.

Why online learning cannot save the wrong twin: Remember that the phone's encoder stays frozen to save battery. If the AI was pretrained on an outdoor campus or a generic statistical recipe, its frozen encoder discards hallway-specific clues before the 32 bits ever leave the phone—and the base station's decoder cannot invent lost information back.

Next, the team isolated hardware changes inside the indoor twin, swapping either the phone's antenna pattern or the base station's antenna pattern. Swapping the phone's antenna barely budged reconstruction accuracy ($\rho$ moved from $0.93$ to $0.94$). Swapping the base station's antenna, however, crashed $\rho$ down to $0.57$—a sharper drop than moving from simulation into the real room.

Why the huge difference? The precoding matrix $\mathbf{W}$ is a set of steering instructions for the base station's antennas, so it depends heavily on which directions those tower antennas push energy. Change the phone's antenna and the tower still aims along the same paths; change the tower's antenna pattern and every steering instruction shifts. Encouragingly, fine-tuning the decoder with just $5\%$ of data from the new base station antenna restored $\rho$ back to $0.84$—and the team also found that time-varying interference and user movement left accuracy stable without needing extra twin modeling.

007The payoff~6 min

Fewer feedback bits, faster real-world downloads.

Cosine similarity ($\rho$) tells you how closely the base station's reconstructed beams match reality. But when you are streaming video on a phone, what really matters is download speed in megabits per second (Mbps) and how much uplink airtime your phone spends sending feedback.

In the indoor corridor testbed at 3.8 GHz—sending two simultaneous data streams ($N_{\text{s}} = 2$) with 1024-QAM modulation, which packs 10 bits into every radio symbol—adding online learning (OL) boosts Indoor EVCsiNet's real-world download speed by 17 Mbps. That jump brings a tiny 32-bit AI codeword ($\rho = 0.82$) up to roughly the same real-world throughput as the standard 5G Type II codebook with 3 beams ($\rho = 0.86$), while cutting feedback overhead from 58 bits down to 32 bits—a 45% reduction.

⚡

32 bits: EVCsiNet + OL

Reaches $\rho = 0.82$ in the real corridor and matches 3-beam Type II speed—saving 26 bits on every report.

📡

58 bits: Type II (3 beams)

Scores $\rho = 0.86$ and delivers comparable speed, but needs nearly twice as many feedback bits.

🏋️

80 bits: Type II (4 beams)

Hits the top score ($\rho = 0.96$) and highest speed, at 2.5 times the feedback cost of EVCsiNet.

The biggest surprise happens when online learning is turned off. Even though plain Indoor EVCsiNet scores slightly lower on average similarity ($\rho = 0.67$) than the 41-bit, 2-beam Type II codebook ($\rho = 0.72$), EVCsiNet still delivers faster real-world downloads.

Why does a lower average score win? With two spatial streams, the base station tries to send two independent data pipes at once along the channel's two strongest directions. Restricted to only 2 beams, the Type II codebook reconstructs those directions unevenly, starving one stream while favoring the other. When an expectation propagation (EP) detector separates both streams at the receiver, one poorly aimed stream leaves extra interference that drags down total speed. By contrast, EVCsiNet reconstructs both streams with balanced accuracy.

Where the full study goes further

In the real indoor testbed, throughput is measured across 1,620 subcarriers at $8.64 \times 10^7$ resource elements per second per stream using an EP MIMO detector. The authors also tested giving EVCsiNet more feedback bits and found only marginal gains—most of the beam shape already fits in 32 bits, while online learning is what fixes real-world mismatch.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

MIMO

Using multiple transmitting antennas at a base station and multiple receiving antennas on a device to send several data streams at once.

Channel State Information (CSI)

A snapshot of how radio waves bounce, fade, and shift phase between a base station's antennas and a user's device.

Precoding matrix (W)

A grid of numbers (weights) telling each base station antenna how to adjust its signal strength and timing to aim beams cleanly at a user.

Codebook (Type II)

An agreed-upon menu of preset beam patterns shared by the phone and base station so the phone only has to send back menu choices.

Autoencoder

A pair of neural networks where an encoder squeezes data into a tiny code and a decoder reconstructs it back into the original shape.

Quantization

Rounding continuous numbers into a small set of discrete steps so they can be sent as a short string of bits.

Digital Twin (DT)

A 3D virtual replica of a real space, built with real material properties and antenna shapes, used to simulate radio waves.

Ray tracing

A simulation method that tracks individual radio signal paths as they travel straight, bounce off walls, and scatter around corners.

Subcarrier

One of many narrow frequency slices (1,620 in this study) that together make up a wide wireless signal.

Angle of Arrival (AoA)

The 3D direction a radio wave is coming from when it hits the receiving antenna.

Cosine similarity (ρ)

A score from -1 to 1 (or 0 to 1 for magnitudes) measuring how closely two vectors or beam patterns point in the same direction.

Online learning (OL)

Updating an already-trained AI model using a small trickle of fresh real-world measurements so it adapts to reality.
