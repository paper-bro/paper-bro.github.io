001~6 min

When AI Imagines, Why Does It Go Blurry?

Imagine you teach an AI to dream the future. You show it thousands of pendulum videos and ask: what comes next? The AI learns a shortcut: squish the image into a short idea-vector — a latent — then predict the next idea-vector, not the next pixels. To imagine 30 frames ahead, it feeds its own guess back in, again and again. That loop is a rollout.

Rollouts look fine for a step or two. But there is a quiet math trap. Most predictors learn to output the average of plausible futures, because averaging minimizes the one-step error. An average is always tamer than the options it averaged — so each step shrinks the cloud of possible futures just a little.

Call the shrink per step $s$, a number like 0.96. One step: area $\to s \times$ area. After $K$ steps: area $\to s^K \times$ area. In logs that's a straight line down. The paper tracks exactly this with

$$ \text{cumulative log-volume} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{K-1} \log |\det J(\mathbf{z}_i)| $$

Here $J(\mathbf{z}_i)$ is the tiny stretch-table (the Jacobian) that says how much a speck of latent volume around $\mathbf{z}_i$ grows or shrinks in one step, $\det J$ is its volume factor, and $n$ is the latent dimension. If $\det J = 1$, log-volume stays at $0$. If $\det J = 0.96$, after 20 steps log-volume is about $-0.8$ and area is less than half — all futures blurring together.

The three ways shrinking kills imagination
LeHami's Proposition 1 names three collapse modes that follow from that shrinking:
(i) Uniform contraction — the product of all stretch factors $\sigma_i$ drops below 1, so the cloud must shrink in every direction at once.
(ii) Settling to a dead dot — the rollout gets sucked into one asymptotically stable fixed point and stops moving.
(iii) Merging — two different starts $\mathbf{z}_1 \neq \mathbf{z}_2$ get mapped to the same future, so the model can no longer tell them apart.

Physics never does this — not because nature is clever, but because its geometry forbids it. A pendulum's full state needs both position $\mathbf{q}$ and momentum $\mathbf{p}$ (angle and how fast). Its motion comes from one energy function $H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})$, and the rule $\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \partial H/\partial \mathbf{p}$, $\dot{\mathbf{p}} = -\partial H/\partial \mathbf{q}$ always preserves a special oriented area in every $(q_i,p_i)$ plane, written $\Omega = \sum dq_i \wedge dp_i$. If area is preserved, volume $\Omega^n/n!$ is preserved — a cloud can shear and spin like taffy, but never get squeezed to a point. Training tricks that spread out the encoder can't give you this; they work on batches during training, not on every step during imagination.

LeHami borrows that guarantee. It keeps the same encoder but replaces the predictor with a learned Hamiltonian $H_{\phi}$ integrated with an exactly symplectic leapfrog step — a kick-drift-kick that has $\det J = 1$ by construction, for any step size, any learned weights, any action sequence.

Try it below: drag Shrink per step $s$ from 1.00 (no shrink) to 0.85 and move Steps K from 1 to 40. Watch the unconstrained cloud shrink and its log-volume dive in a straight line. Then switch to LeHami — same number of steps, but the cloud only shears and rotates. Area stays 1.00 and log-volume stays 0. That's the world that doesn't shrink.

Going deeper (optional) — A map with $\det J = 1$ everywhere is volume-preserving. A symplectic map preserves the finer $\Omega = \sum dq_i \wedge dp_i$, which implies volume preservation but also rules out the other two modes. LeHami's step is the composition $\Psi_h = A_{h/2} \circ B_h \circ A_{h/2}$, each a shear with matrix $\begin{pmatrix}I & 0\\ -h/2\,\text{Hess}V & I\end{pmatrix}$ or $\begin{pmatrix}I & h\,\text{Hess}K\\ 0 & I\end{pmatrix}$ — both determinant 1 by being triangular with ones on the diagonal.

From Pixels to Ideas: How JEPAs Learn Without Labels

Imagine you teach an AI by showing it millions of photos with no captions, no labels — just video frames one after another. How can it learn anything useful from that?

That's the idea behind a JEPA — a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture. Instead of asking the AI to repaint the next video frame pixel by pixel, you ask it to predict the next idea. It turns each frame x into a short list of numbers, a latent vector:

$$\mathbf{q}_t = \text{Enc}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{W}(t)})$$

Read it as: the encoder Enc with learned weights θ looks at a little window W(t) of frames and outputs qt. With W=0 it sees one frame; with W=3 it sees three frames at once. In this paper the total idea-vector is 64 numbers long — either kept whole (baseline LeWM) or split into two halves of 32 for LeHami.

The predictor then guesses what that idea will be next: (tilde qt+1, tilde pt+1) = HamFlow(qt, pt, ut). It rolls forward K=8 steps by feeding its own guess back in. Training compares each guess to what the encoder actually saw:

$$\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{A+j}-\mathbf{q}_{A+j}\|^2 + \lambda_p\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{A+j}-\mathbf{p}_{A+j}\|^2 + \lambda_{\mathcal{S}}\,\mathcal{S}$$

First term: how wrong is the position guess? Second term: how wrong is the momentum guess, weighted by λp=1.0. Third term: the regularizer S weighted by λS=0.25. S is SIGReg — it looks at a whole batch of encoder outputs during training and nudges them to spread out like a round bell curve. The anchor A=3 just means the first real state needs 4 frames to build momentum from.

Two different collapses, two different fixes. Encoder collapse is a training problem: the encoder learns to map every image to the same dot because that's an easy way to make predictions look close. SIGReg fixes this — but only in expectation over training batches. Predictor contraction is an inference problem: even with a nicely spread encoder, each rollout step can slightly squeeze the cloud of possible futures. After 30 steps, the cloud has shrunk to a dot (lesson 1's log-volume line). No training batch can see that — it happens when you imagine without new camera frames.

That's why the paper calls them complementary, not redundant (Remark 4). SIGReg shapes the encoder distribution during training. LeHami's symplectic predictor preserves that volume at every single inference step, for every input, forever — a guarantee built into the geometry, not learned from data.

You can see this concretely in the paper's regularizer experiment: putting SIGReg on the joint 64-dim vector [q||p] actually destroys the momentum half — the model inflates q to satisfy the bell-curve test and sets p ≈ 0. Only regularizing q alone keeps both halves alive, which is what LeHami uses.

003~8 min

What Physics Knows About the Future

A single photo of a pendulum tells you where it is, but not where it's going. That photo could be a pendulum frozen at the top of its swing, about to fall left — or falling right through the same spot. To predict the future you need two numbers: position and momentum.

Physicists call the pair (q, p) the mechanical state. For a simple pendulum, q is the angle θ (how far from hanging straight down) and p is proportional to angular velocity ω (how fast it's spinning). The space of all possible (q,p) pairs is called phase space — for one pendulum it's a flat plane, ℝ². Knowing one point in that plane is enough to roll the future forward; knowing only q is half the story. The paper leans on this: tasks like catching or throwing need full state, and a world model should carry it too.

$$ \dot{\mathbf{q}} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{p}}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{p}} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial \mathbf{q}} $$

This is Hamilton's equations. H(q,p) is one scalar number — the total energy. Its slopes tell the system how to move: the slope in p tells q how fast to change, and minus the slope in q tells p how fast to change. Once you know H, you know the whole vector field that carries any starting point forward in time.

For LeHami's test world the equations become concrete. A conservative pendulum (no push) obeys:

$$ \ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta $$

where g = 9.81 m/s², L is rod length, and θ¨ is angular acceleration. Push it with a torque u and you get the actuated version:

$$ \ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta + \frac{u}{m L^{2}} $$

Here m is mass, so u / (mL²) is how much the push matters — a heavy or long pendulum resists more. In the dataset the push is held constant for 3 frames (0.3 s), about 15% of one natural swing period T = 2π√(L/g). Change L from 0.7 m to 1.4 m and the natural frequency ω₀ = √(g/L) changes by 1.4× — a single learned model must handle all three.

The key physics law LeHami borrows: both of these flows — pushed or not — are incompressible. A small blob of many nearby starts can stretch into a banana, but its area never changes. Formally the flow preserves the symplectic form $\Omega = \sum dq_i \wedge dp_i$ (oriented area in each (q,p) plane), so it preserves the volume form $\Omega^n/n!$ and density rides along unchanged: $d\rho/dt = 0$. That's Liouville's theorem.

That incompressibility is exactly what an unconstrained AI predictor violates when it shrinks clouds. In the lab below you'll see the difference with your own eyes: pick a rod length, add torque, and drag time. Watch the blue dot slide along or across grey energy contours, and watch the green cloud stretch without ever losing area.

LeHami's Trick: Splitting the Mind in Two

Position alone can't tell you where you'll go. A photo of a pendulum tells you its angle, but not whether it's swinging left or right. For that you need velocity — the change of position over time.

LeHami refuses to learn velocity. It computes it. Watch the trick:

$$\mathbf{q}_t = \text{Enc}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t)\qquad \mathbf{p}_t = \text{SG}(\mathbf{q}_{t-3},\dots,\mathbf{q}_t)$$

q is the learned idea of position — a neural network turns pixels into a short vector. p is the momentum-like half. It is not learned at all. It's a fixed linear recipe, a Savitzky–Golay (SG) filter: fit a little parabola through the recent qs and take its slope at now. No weights to train, so the filter itself can never collapse to a constant.

There are two versions of that recipe. For training targets the paper uses a centred 5-frame stencil on $q_{t-2}\dots q_{t+2}$ with antisymmetric weights

$$(-2,-1,0,1,2)/10$$

For the anchor that starts a rollout — the moment you start imagining — it uses a causal 4-frame stencil on $q_{t-3}\dots q_t$, because the future frames haven't happened yet. The maths says the centred version is much better: its bias is $\frac{17}{30}q^{(3)}\Delta t^2$ versus $\frac{47}{60}q^{(3)}\Delta t^2$ for causal, and its noise amplification $\sum w_m^2$ is $0.10$ versus $2.45$ — about 24.5× less variance. In plain words: look both ways and noise cancels.

Why antisymmetry is everything. Centred weights are odd: $w_{-m} = -w_m$. Play the movie backwards ($m \to -m$) and $p_t$ automatically flips sign, $p \to -p$, just like real momentum under time reversal. A tied temporal ConvNet with symmetric kernel $[a,b,a]$ is even: $w_{-m}=w_m$, like $\cos(\omega\Delta t)$. Then $+\omega$ and $-\omega$ give the identical output — the model sees speed but is blind to direction. LeHami is odd by construction, so direction survives without any extra tuning.

Declaring $p \equiv \dot q$ makes the split canonical. The paper shows that for a constant-mass system this forces the learned kinetic energy to look like $K(\mathbf{S}^{-1}p)=\tfrac12\|p\|^2 + C$ on the data — the mass is absorbed by re-scaling coordinates, so LeHami learns no separate mass. The tiny remaining scale freedom $\mathbf{S}=\text{diag}(e^{\sigma})$ is a diagonal gauge the model learns; in practice it stays $0.95$ to $1.09$, near identity. Actions enter only through the potential, $H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{u})=K(\mathbf{p})+V(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{u})$, so the force $\mathbf{F}=-\partial V/\partial\mathbf{q}$ is a gradient for every $\mathbf{u}$ and the step stays symplectic for any action sequence.

The lab lets you feel why the filter shape matters. Try each stencil, add noise, and hit time-reverse — only the odd ones flip $p$ correctly.

Figure note (F8): This result confirms LeHami's split works as intended: angle θ is recovered mainly from the position half q (solid) while angular velocity ω lives almost entirely in the momentum half p (hatched). The baseline JEPA has only a single block z and cannot make this separation.

Going further: For systems where mass depends on configuration (e.g., double pendulum), p = q̇ is no longer canonical and a separable K(p) is misspecified — the paper notes this needs a jointly learned p. For dissipative friction they'd add a port-Hamiltonian D term.

The Magic Step That Never Squashes

Imagine you must predict the future one blink at a time. Your rule is: look at now, guess next, then use your guess as the new now. If your rule squashes even a tiny bit each time, after 30 blinks your whole cloud of possible futures collapses to a dot. Training can look fine — but rollout, when you actually imagine, shrinks.

LeHami fixes this with a three-line dance borrowed from physics, called leapfrog — kick, drift, kick. It keeps two halves: position-like q and momentum-like p. A learned scalar H(q,p,u) = K(p) + V(q,u) decides how they push each other. Think of K as how momentum wants to move, and V as how position and the action u pull on momentum.

$$\begin{aligned} p_{\text{half}} &= p_t - \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\,\nabla_q V(q_t, u_t) \;\text{(half-kick)}\\ q_{t+1} &= q_t + \Delta t\, S^{-1}\nabla K\!\left(S^{-1}p_{\text{half}}\right) \;\text{(drift)}\\ p_{t+1} &= p_{\text{half}} - \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\,\nabla_q V(q_{t+1}, u_t) \;\text{(half-kick)} \end{aligned}$$

The S = \mathrm{diag}(e^{\sigma}) is just a learned rescaling between the two halves — the paper finds it stays near 1 (0.95 to 1.09) — and \Delta t is the step size, here 0.1 seconds.

Here is the magic: each half-step is a shear. A shear slides one axis along the other, like pushing the top of a deck of cards sideways. Its Jacobian (the little matrix that says how tiny changes stretch) looks like [[I, 0], [-h/2·HessV, I]], which always has determinant 1. In plain words: area = 1, no squash. The full step is shear–shear–shear combined, \Psi_h = A_{h/2} \circ B_h \circ A_{h/2}, so it is also exactly area- and volume-preserving, a symplectic diffeomorphism, for every step size h, every parameter, and every action sequence.

Three things this forbids — at every step, no exceptions:
1. Uniform contraction: you cannot shrink in all directions at once. If \det =1, the stretch factors \sigma_i multiply to 1, so \sigma_{\text{max}} \ge 1 \ge \sigma_{\text{min}}.
2. Settling to a fixed point: a ball around a stable attractor would have to shrink to a point, but volume stays constant — contradiction.
3. Merging: two different starts z_1 \ne z_2 can never become equal later, because the map is invertible.

What it does not forbid is just as important. A cloud that cannot be squeezed can still be stretched into a long banana — error can grow. The paper is explicit about this (Remark 3): symplecticity removes degenerate collapse, it does not guarantee accuracy. LeHami can still be wrong, just never collapsed.

Try it below. Flip between LeHami's shear and a generic predictor that shrinks by a factor s each side. Drag k to make the potential curvier and dt to change the step. Watch the square's area and the singular values — and watch how log-volume stays flat at 0 for LeHami but slides negative linearly for the squasher, exactly as in the paper's Fig. 4 and Fig. 13.

For the curious — why the proof works: each shear has symmetric Hessians (because K,V are C^2), so DA^\top J DA = J with J = [[0,I],[-I,0]]. Composition keeps D\Psi^\top J D\Psi = J and \det =1 by chain rule. Iterating K times keeps it, so cumulative (1/n)\sum \log|\det J| =0 exactly.

Try this: set k=1.5 and K=15. In LeHami mode the square shears hard — one singular value >1, the other <1, product =1. Switch to MLP mode with s=0.98 and see both <1 and the right plot dive.

The three-line recipe, in words

Kick: nudge momentum using the slope of V at current position. Drift: move position using the slope of K at the nudged momentum (scaled by S). Kick again: nudge momentum again using V at the new position. Because your nudge depends only on the other half, you get shears, not squashes — and shears compose to a volume-preserving whole.

That one idea is why LeHami's rollout can run 30, 50, even more steps without its imagination shrinking to a point, whether there are actions or not.

A Vector Field, Not Just a Next-Frame Guesser

Most AI predictors are like a student who memorized next frame = ?. The time gap between frames — say 0.1 seconds — is baked into its weights. Ask it to predict 0.05 seconds ahead and it has nowhere to put that number. It just guesses the next frame again.

LeHami is different. Its predictor is a vector field. Think of a weather map where every point has a tiny arrow saying go this way, this fast. The learned scalar $H_{\phi}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p};\mathbf{u})$ defines those arrows through Hamilton's rules

$$ \dot{\mathbf{q}} = \frac{\partial H_{\phi}}{\partial \mathbf{p}}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf{p}} = -\frac{\partial H_{\phi}}{\partial \mathbf{q}} $$

$\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ means speed of q and $\dot{\mathbf{p}}$ means speed of p. In words: the slope of $H$ in the $p$ direction tells $q$ how to move, and minus the slope in the $q$ direction tells $p$ how to move. Once you know the arrows, you can walk along them for any amount of time you choose.

That is why the paper writes the step size $h$ as an argument to the flow, not a property of the weights. In training every clip has a recorded spacing $\Delta t_{ep}$ and the model integrates with the ratio $h = \Delta t_{ep}/\Delta t_{ref}$ — here $\Delta t_{ref}=0.1$ s. The same $H_{\phi}$ works at any $h$. The authors trained only at $h=1$ ($0.10$ s) and then, with weights frozen, integrated at $h=0.5$ and $h=0.25$ — two and four tiny leapfrog sub-steps per video frame. The rollout stayed on the true trajectory. Doubling to $h=2$ ($0.20$ s) broke it, because any explicit leapfrog leaves its stability band if the step is too large — just like walking too far along an arrow before looking again.

Same brain, different clock: an unstructured transformer has no slot for $h$, so it cannot change rate without retraining. A Hamiltonian has $h$ as a knob on the integrator.

The same picture gives you time-reversal for free. Flip the sign $h \to -h$ (and play the action sequence backwards) and the arrows point the other way. Roll $K$ steps forward and then $K$ steps backward and you return to where you started. In the paper the mismatch after a round trip stays below $10^{-4}$\% — not exact only because the momentum at the starting frame comes from a causal filter that is not perfectly symmetric, but nearly so.

That detail matters outside pendulums. If you can run time forward you can plan on non-uniform grids — small steps where precision matters, large steps elsewhere. If you can run it backward you can ask from which states could I still reach safety? — the backward reachable tube used for safety guarantees.

What the Latent World Looks Like Inside

Inside LeHami's mind there is a tiny physics world. The 64-number latent vector is split in half: q (position-like, 32 numbers) and p (momentum-like, 32 numbers). The paper looks at that 64-D cloud with PCA — a way to find the two directions where the cloud spreads most — and plots each test episode as a trail in those coordinates.

In the conservative pendulum (no pushes, Fig. 11), each episode becomes a closed loop. The loop lies flat at a single height. Height here is the learned energy $H_{\phi}(q,p) = K_{\phi}(p) + V_{\phi}(q)$ — one scalar number the network invents, playing the role of “total energy.” All points with the same $H_{\phi}$ form a level set: a flat shelf in this landscape. LeHami keeps each episode on its own shelf, just as the real pendulum $H = \text{kinetic} + \text{potential}$ keeps a frictionless swing at constant energy. Different starting pushes give different, non-touching rings. That is stratification.

LeWM, the unstructured baseline with a transformer predictor, does not. Its trails wander up and down in $H$, drifting from one plane to another (paper's Fig. 10). The geometry is the clue: LeHami's structure forces the rollout to respect energy-like shells; LeWM can shear and diffuse across them.

Actuation changes the rule, not the guarantee. With torque $u$, the paper sets $H_{\phi}(q,p,u)=K_{\phi}(p)+V_{\phi}(q,u)$. Now work is done, so $H_{\phi}$ should change — trails should cross level sets, tilting through the landscape (Fig. 14). What stays protected is the map itself: each leapfrog step still has $\det J = 1$, so volume is preserved even as $H_{\phi}$ is not. Constraint on the map, not on the value.

Who carries what? Frozen $k$-NN probes on the anchor frame ($K=0$) show it cleanly: the $q$-half decodes angle $\theta$ well, the $p$-half decodes angular velocity $\omega$ well (Fig. 8, solid vs hatched bars). That is what “canonical” means here — position and momentum live in separate slots by construction.

That split is also why training must regularize carefully. The paper uses SIGReg, which wants encoder outputs to look like a standard bell curve ($\text{mean }0, \text{variance }1$). Applied to the joint 64-D vector $\,[q\,||\,p]\,$, SIGReg can cheat: it inflates $q$ to $\text{std}(q)\approx 1.36 \approx \sqrt{2}$ and crushes $p$ to almost nothing — $\text{std}(p)/\text{std}(q)=0.0043$ and only $0.01\%$ of variance is within an episode (Table 8, $W{=}1$ joint) — because $p$ is not free, it is just $\,p = SG(q_{t-3}\dots q_t)$, a fixed derivative of $q$. The momentum block is killed. Applied to $q$ only — the paper's choice — $\text{std}(q)\approx 0.98$, $\text{std}(p)\approx 0.10$, ratio $0.10$, with $\sim15\%$ within-episode variation. Split-regularizing both helps more ($\sim49\%$ within) but $p$ still stays small because it is conditioned on $q$. In short: regularize where you learned, let the derivative be.

Does It Actually Work? Pendulums, Predictions and Plans

Say you have a video imagination of a pendulum that has to replan its pushes. Looking good for one frame isn't enough — you need to know: can it keep the speed and direction right for thirty frames, and can it do it without burning your compute budget?

LeHami was tested on 64 by 64 color pendulum videos in two worlds. The conservative world has no pushing and 300 episodes (210 train / 45 val / 45 test). The actuated world adds a recorded torque u held steady for 3 frames (0.3 seconds, about 15% of a swing) and has 900 episodes (630 / 135 / 135). Rod length L is 0.7, 1.0 or 1.4 meters drawn to scale, and in the actuated world mass m is 0.5, 1.0 or 2.0 kg shown as a bigger bob. A planner uses CEM with 300 tries, 30 rounds, 30 winners to pick the push sequence.

To check what the imagination knows, the paper freezes the model and trains a tiny reader — a 25-nearest-neighbor probe fitted at step K=1 — to decode angle θ and angular velocity ω from the frozen latent. At the very start, before any rollout, LeHami reads ω at R² = +0.975 while the per-frame LeWM baseline is at -0.667 (worse than just guessing the average) on the conservative pendulum. On the actuated pendulum the same story: ridge probe +0.491 vs -0.014, and with a stronger MLP reader +0.987 vs -0.011. A single picture has no motion — LeWM's z can't see it, but LeHami's split (q,p) already can because p is the motion.

Unrolled into the future, LeHami's velocity error is 2.8 times smaller for the first 10 steps and still 1.3 times smaller at the end of the horizon, and its angle prediction pulls ahead after about K = 11. This is not brute force. LeHami's predictor has 2.23 million parameters versus LeWM's transformer at 3.31 million, total 3.03M vs 4.44M (32% fewer). At only 0.75 PFLOP of training LeHami already beats LeWM's best at 6 PFLOP — more than 8 times less training compute for better accuracy. Per planning step it costs 13.6 MFLOP versus 21.7.

The tight-budget test makes the difference concrete. With at most 15 TFLOP per episode the planner must pick how often to replan, every R steps. Getting the sign of ω right is the hard part: 92% of the 50 held-out goals need the correct spin direction (tolerance τ_ω = 0.5 rad/s), so guessing speed but backwards still fails. LeHami gets the sign right 0.80 of the time, clearly above just coasting with u=0 at 0.68, while the tied LeWM with its symmetric kernel [a,b,a] scores 0.649 — actually worse than doing nothing. An untied LeWM can reach 0.962 on sign but only by learning separate time weights and paying more. Because LeHami's step is cheaper it can afford R = 6 (5 replans) where LeWM needs R = 9 (4 replans) under the same budget. Joint position-and-velocity goals show the tradeoff: LeHami 0.304 vs untied 0.636 — LeHami wins on direction, but position reaching is still the bottleneck.

LeHami is not for everything. If the real world сильно dissipates volume — heavy friction that shrinks phase-space — a purely volume-preserving map cannot match it. You would add a learned damping term D (port-Hamiltonian). And if the task is only static pose with no velocity need, this structure buys you little.

Now try it yourself. In the lab pick any of the 50 shared goals (green ghost), move the budget slider, and watch the feasible replanning interval R change. Run LeHami versus tied and untied LeWM and see three meters update: position, velocity size, and velocity sign. Notice how tied LeWM often wobbles to the wrong spin, and how lowering the budget forces LeWM to replan less often — exactly where LeHami's cheaper step keeps up.

What to try: Set budget to 8 TFLOP and run LeHami, then tied LeWM on the same goal — who gets the sign? Crank budget to 14 TFLOP and see both afford more replans but LeHami still costs less. Then pick a goal with large |ω*| and compare untied LeWM's 0.962 sign rate to LeHami's 0.80 — that's the price of the symmetric shortcut.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

latent space

AI's internal map where similar images get nearby points. Like compressing a big photo collection into a small idea-coordinate system.

encoder / latent state

Encoder (Enc): a neural network that turns pixels x into a short vector q. Latent state z = (q,p) is where the model thinks.

JEPA (world model)

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture: learns by predicting the next latent vector, not the next pixels. No decoder needed.

rollout

Rollout: feeding a prediction back as the next input, step after step, to imagine the future without new camera frames.

collapse

Collapse/contraction: distinct futures getting squeezed together or onto one attractor during rollout. Volume preservation is the opposite.

regularization (SIGReg)

Training trick (SIGReg) that forces encoder outputs to spread out like a bell curve. Stops encoder collapse in training batches, but does not control the predictor at test time.

Hamiltonian (H)

Physics recipe: position q plus momentum p evolve together from one scalar function H = total energy. H generates the flow.

phase space

Space of all (position, momentum) pairs = full state needed to predict the future. For pendulum: (angle θ, angular velocity ω).

symplectic

Special geometry that says oriented area in every (q_i , p_i) plane is conserved. If area is conserved, volume is conserved.

volume preservation

Exactly preserving volume at every step: a cloud can stretch and shear but never shrink to a point. LeHami guarantees det J = 1.

leapfrog (Störmer-Verlet) step

The kick-drift-kick integrator LeHami uses for H = K(p)+V(q,u). Second-order accurate and exactly symplectic by construction.

Savitzky–Golay (SG) stencil

Simple fixed filter that estimates momentum as time-derivative of position: p_t = SG(q_{t-3}..q_t). No learned weights; centred weights are (-2,-1,0,1,2)/10, antisymmetric so reversing time sends p→-p.

probe (k-NN probe)

Small frozen reader (ridge, 25-NN, 2-layer MLP) trained to decode θ or ω from frozen latents. Fitted once at K=0 or K=1 and kept frozen.
