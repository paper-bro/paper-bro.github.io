001~8 min

Has One Model Really Learned to Be a Robot?

Imagine one brain learns to drive many bodies — a single arm that stacks blocks, two arms that fold a towel together, hands with many fingers that pinch a toy, and even a humanoid that walks to a table and stops. The paper tested exactly that: GPT-6-Astra, a general-purpose frontier model from SenseNova, with the same fixed weights controlling all of them. No special training for each robot. No fine-tuning between tasks.

And on lab tests it looks amazing. That one model gets close to 100% on several standard benchmarks. But the authors don't stop there. Their headline claim is careful: GPT-6-Astra has largely crossed the threshold of embodied competence, but not yet that of practical embodied intelligence.

Competence vs practical intelligence: competence = can you get a high score when given study notes and unlimited retries in a simulator that pauses while you think? Practical = can you do it reliably, quickly, cheaply and safely in a real home where time keeps ticking and a mistake can break something?

Why the split? A lab gives you two advantages you don't get in a kitchen. First, you often get demonstrations on the exact task and robot — videos of success to study. Second, the simulator is patient: extra steps cost nothing, collisions don't hurt, and thinking time is free. In a home, demos of your exact chore often don't exist, the joint can overheat, a spilled drink can't be un-spilled, and the egg keeps cooking while the AI is still deciding.

The paper's own real-world videos make this concrete. One model does drive all eight platform types in Figure 8. But success depends hugely on the task: rigid pick-and-place like lifting a basket is 17 out of 18 trials. Deformable cloth folding is only 2 out of 4 on the Franka 3. Precise plug insertion is 0 out of 11 across three different single-arm setups. Long searches across rooms are 9 out of 23. Same brain, wildly different results.

That's the puzzle this course will unpack: how the lab scores got so high, what the harness around the model does, why real homes break it, and what a fairer test would look like. Use the lab below to feel the gap — move what counts as "good enough" for competence and for practicality, and watch which tasks stay green.

002Background needed first~10 min

The Exam Robots Used to Take

Imagine a driving test where everyone gets the same car, the same streets, and extra time if they stall. That's what this paper did for robots — to finally compare fairly.

Until now, most robot exams worked like this: researchers collected demonstrations of one task on one robot — say, "pick up the mug with this arm" — then trained a small, specialized brain just for that task. At test time they ran a few new versions of the same task, counted how many times the robot made a checker turn true — like mug_on_tray == true at a single moment — and stopped. The simulator also politely paused the world while the AI thought.

The paper kept the check the same for everyone, but changed how the test is run so a general model like GPT-6-Astra could show what it can really do. One big change: GPT-6-Astra's weights stayed completely fixed — no retraining per benchmark. If a benchmark had training demonstrations, the model was allowed to read them beforehand to build study notes. If it had no training set, it had to go in with no notes.

🧩

5 families, 12 exams

Tabletop arms: LIBERO-Long++, LIBERO-PRO, LIBERO-Ultra-Long, RoboDojo, RoboLab.
Humanoid: SIMPLE (walk + pick).
Dexterous hands: Bench2Dex (26 tasks), DexJoCo (11 tasks).
Household: BEHAVIOR (100 daily chores).
Navigation: VLN-CE R2R / RxR and LHPR-VLN (follow language to find places in order).

⏱️

1× vs 10× — same episode, more time

1× is the official step limit for that benchmark: the number of robot moves allowed before we stop scoring. 10× means: don't reset, keep the same kitchen, the same mess, and the same memory, and let the same episode continue up to ten times longer. Stop early if the robot succeeds or clearly fails. This measures correction, not a fresh retry.

Why two limits? With only 1×, a robot that could have fixed a wobbly grasp with one more try looks like it failed. With up to 10×, we see how much comes from the ability to notice "that didn't work" and try again — with history kept. The paper found the standard limit was hiding a lot of ability, especially on tasks that need correction. One exception: BEHAVIOR already allows very long episodes, so it was not extended to 10×.

Key insight: the next two lessons will unpack the two levers that raised scores — study notes from demos when they exist, and extra steps in the same episode to recover. Neither changes the robot or the task.

Try it yourself below: pick a task type, turn demos on or off, and drag the budget from 1× to 10×. Watch how the success curve and the extra time tick up differently for an easy pick versus a precise insert or a long navigation.

003~12 min

Study Notes Without Changing Your Brain

Think of GPT-6-Astra as a student who is not allowed to change her brain on exam day. She can't grow new connections. Her weights — the numbers inside the model — stay frozen. But she is allowed to bring study notes. The paper asks: how far does good study notes get you, without any retraining?

Before testing, for each task she is given the training demonstrations for that task — the same videos and action logs that a small specialist robot would normally be trained on. She studies them and writes one frozen knowledge package, called K. That package is then locked. Every test episode for that task gets the same copy, and she cannot edit it between episodes. No test episodes are ever used to build it.

The trick here is in-context learning without weight updates: the model learns by reading notes in its prompt, not by changing its brain.

What is inside K? The paper did not tell the model what to write — yet across 12 benchmarks the same six parts kept appearing:

  1. Task guidance: plain instructions — what the goal is, what order to do subtasks in, what to avoid.
  2. Motion references: example trajectories — how a demo arm moved, where it grasped, how phases flowed.
  3. Visual references: chosen demo images — what a good grasp looks like, where objects should be.
  4. Executable helpers: small reusable programs for things like forward and inverse kinematics (math that turns a desired hand position into joint angles and back), plus code to fetch the right reference.
  5. Checks & recovery: how to tell "did that work?" and what to try next if it didn't.
  6. Metadata & provenance: which robot, which cameras, and where each note came from, so numbers are not misread.

When those notes closely match the test, the lift is huge. On the humanoid benchmark SIMPLE, adding task-level notes lifted success from $70.8\%$ to $90.8\%$ at the normal step limit and made successful episodes shorter. On LIBERO the model neared $100\%$. On the dexterous-hand benchmark DexJoCo, which needs precise finger control, it nearly doubled the best published score. In a concrete case, Bench2Dex Ball Box Loading, she remapped demo joint angles to compensate for a $4$–$5$ cm difference in table height while keeping the demo's two-hand support shape — proposing, adapting and revising under camera feedback, not just replaying.

But the benefit depends on coverage — how much the training demos look like the test. Where the test drifts away, the suitcase doesn't help much. The RoboDojo "Open" group had no demos for its own 8 tasks, only demos from 4 other groups to study — it ended up the lowest of all RoboDojo groups. RoboLab has no training set at all, so no K could be built from task demos. There GPT-6-Astra still beat the best published baseline ($42.9\%$) with $57.1\%$, but that is only modest — far from saturation. Packages, the paper notes, are still mostly task-specific.

In other words, study notes without changing your brain can make a good student excellent — if the exam looks like the homework. Move the exam away from the homework, and the same notes travel poorly.

Next, open the lab: build your own package. Turn each of the six parts on or off, then slide how familiar the test is. Watch whether your little 2D arm can remap its demo to a new table height, and watch the predicted success curve rise and fall as you match — or break — what the paper found.

004~12 min

Ten Times More Steps

Imagine you are building a shelf and the first screw misses. If you are allowed to look, adjust, and try again without starting over, you will probably get it. The paper asks: how much better is GPT-6-Astra if we let it stay in the same episode and keep trying?

That is the extended budget. It is not a restart. The robot keeps its memory of what it just did, and the world keeps what happened — a moved bowl stays moved, a spilled cup stays spilled. The episode stops only when the checker says success, when it hits a terminal failure, or when it runs out of steps. The official limit is called 1×. The paper tests up to 10×, ten times as many steps from the same starting scene. BEHAVIOR was not extended because its official limit is already very long.

🔧

Where it helps a lot

RoboDojo roughly tripled: about 27.6% at 1× to 81.9% at 10×. About three quarters of episodes unfinished at 1× later finished, with the hardest precise work gaining most. Bench2Dex more than doubled: 25.6% to 61.5%.

🏠

Where it helps a little

LHPR-VLN long navigation went from 21.1% to only 28.9% at 10×, still the lowest overall. More wandering did not fix losing track of multiple goals in order. Extra tries cannot replace progress tracking.

Other benchmarks show the ceiling. With both study notes and extra tries, the LIBERO family and SIMPLE reach about 95% to 100%, DexJoCo reaches 87.3% and VLN-CE reaches about 88% to 90%. Those are close to solved in simulation — which is why the paper says future work must look beyond these scores.

Extra steps are not free. About 98% of tokens are cached input the model re-reads each turn. A useful rule of thumb from the paper: 1 million tokens ≈ 1 dollar. Even when success is near 97%, LIBERO-Long++ costs about 1 M tokens and about 1 hour per episode at 1×; LIBERO-Ultra-Long costs about 9 M tokens and nearly 2 hours. An average RoboDojo episode at 10× took over 3 hours of wall-clock time.

In the simulator, thinking time does not count against the step budget — the world pauses while the model deliberates. That makes extra tries look cheaper than they are on a real robot, where every second of thinking costs money, heats motors, and keeps a human waiting. The next lab lets you feel that trade-off.

Try it: pick a task type and drag the budget from 1× to 10×. Watch the pink-like curve climb and flatten, and watch the tokens, dollars and minutes tick up. Hit Play to auto-run retries and see why the same extra time buys a big jump for RoboDojo and only a small bump for long-horizon navigation.

005~12 min

System 2 Steering System 1

Think of driving a car. You have fast reflexes that keep the wheel steady every millisecond — that's System 1. And you have a slow, chatty navigator who reads signs, spots mistakes and says “turn around” — that's System 2. In the paper, System 1 is a small, fast robot policy called π0.5 that already knows how to move joints. System 2 is GPT-6-Astra itself, thinking in words and images.

The question is: how should the two talk to each other? The authors tried the options on the same 42 RoboDojo tasks and counted both success and tokens (the price of thinking).

Open-loop vs closed-loop. In open-loop, System 2 writes subgoals like “grab the mug,” then System 1 runs a whole chain of moves alone. Result on RoboDojo: 5.83% success, using only 0.387 million tokens. Standalone System 1 with no supervisor did about the same: 6.91%. Planning without checking barely helps.

In closed-loop, System 1 proposes a move, converts its joint angles into a hand pose with forward kinematics (math that turns “motor positions → where is the hand in 3-D space”), and System 2 reviews it before it runs. It can accept, nudge, or reject the move. With check-every-step (review horizon L=1) success jumped to 23.61% — but cost exploded to 7.664 million tokens.

The trick: check L=4 moves at once. Success rose a little more to 25.83%, while tokens fell 63.5% to 2.796 million. Fewer interruptions means less chatter and smoother execution. Add the study-notes package K from the last lesson on top of closed-loop, and success hit 54.17% — by far the best — but at 19.335 million tokens, about 6.9 times the best non-ICL closed-loop cost.

Key trade-off: frequent System 2 supervision is powerful but pricey. Batching reviews and making them more selective is how you buy performance without going broke.

What language should the brain speak? The harness also chooses the action representation — how a command is written.

An end-effector (EEF) command says “put the fingertip at this [x, y, z] position, with this orientation” in room coordinates. A joint-angle command says “set shoulder joint 3 to 32 degrees, elbow to 14 degrees …” for every motor.

GPT-6-Astra reasons in space, like you do when you imagine reaching for a cup. So EEF fits its thinking. Directional commands like “move 2cm left” gained +2.50 points over plain EEF. Direct joint angles lost -5.84 points. That’s the representation gap: don’t make a vision-language model do low-level motor math — let a controller handle it.

The same idea applies to chunking — how many moves System 2 commits to per thought. Fixed chunks of L=4 saved tokens to 0.435 million but lost -4.17 points: always doing four moves is clumsy on fiddly steps. Letting the model adaptively pick L in {1,2,3,4} gained +4.44 points at 1.146 million tokens: short bursts for precision, longer runs when it’s easy.

Below, tune the harness yourself. Switch between open and closed-loop, try EEF vs joint angles, and slide L from 1 to 4. Watch where your setting lands on the success-vs-tokens plot — can you find the sweet spot the paper found?

006~10 min

What Changes on a Real Robot

In simulation, the world politely waits while the AI thinks. On a real robot, it doesn't. That one difference changes everything.

To test it, the authors took the same GPT-6-Astra — same weights, no retraining — off the simulator and put it on eight real robots ordered from simple to complex: a single Franka arm with a gripper, a Piper arm with a Pika gripper, two Piper arms working together, Tianji M6S hands with many finger joints, a wheeled Galaxea R1 Pro, a Tracer base with an xArm, and a Unitree G1 humanoid.

This time the rules were harsher: zero-shot. No frozen knowledge package, no demos from the test task. The only help was what a deployment team would normally hand you — tool names, joint limits, where the cameras sit, safe open/close presets for a dexterous hand. No ready-made "pick" or "insert" skill. Trials were limited by wall-clock time, about 60 minutes, with a person holding the emergency stop and judging success by looking at the real outcome, not what the model claimed.

The same model drove every body, and what broke it was not the body but the task. Rigid pick-and-place — putting blocks in a basket or stacking — was reliable, 17 successes in 18 trials. But tasks that need soft-material handling or exact contact struggled: cloth folding on the Franka 3 worked in only 2 of 4 trials, precise plug insertion failed in 0 of 11 trials across three single-arm platforms, and room-scale search on the Tracer found its target in only 9 of 23.

The "see to do" trap: the hand holds the flower stem upright, so its own wrist camera stares straight down the stem and never sees the vase mouth below. A single Franka arm drops the flower beside the vase. Give the robot a second arm, and GPT-6-Astra discovers active vision on its own — it drives the free wrist camera around until the green ring of the vase appears, then inserts. Human teleop demos rarely show this (the operator watches from outside), so an AI that only copies demos would never learn to move its eyes.

Why can't the robot just try ten times longer, like in simulation? In simulation a bad move costs nothing. On hardware it costs safety, heat and time:

  • Safety stops. Brushing the table while folding thin cloth or a joint hitting its limit during plug insertion triggers a protective stop that ends the trial. Two of three PiPER plug insertions ended this way. A dropped object can be irreversible.
  • Mechanics. The model has no feel for load. Wuji finger joints stalled under load and hit 62°C, and Galaxea's torso overheated and ended both flower-collection runs.
  • Real time. In the lab an hour of thinking was invisible because the simulator paused. On the G1 humanoid about 90% of a run was deliberation, and successful manipulation took over an hour. While you think, the world keeps changing.

That's why the paper says demos for in-context learning are expensive to collect in real life, and trading more steps for higher success is "implausible" on physical hardware. The paper shows many more platforms and failure videos than we can cover here.

Try it yourself: put yourself in the robot's two cameras and feel why a second viewpoint turns an impossible insertion into an easy one.

007~10 min

The Failures That Remain

Imagine you ace practice driving tests because the instructor tells you the route, lets you retry, and pauses traffic while you think. That's competence on a closed course. Real driving — when rain erases lane lines, a scrape can't be undone, and you must remember where you parked while avoiding a new detour — is practical intelligence. In lessons 3–4 GPT-6-Astra got study notes (its frozen knowledge package) and up to 10× extra steps, and reached near-perfect scores on many lab benchmarks. This lesson asks what still breaks even then.

The paper's reviewers watched videos and logs and found the same three patterns over and over. They are not about needing more time — extra budget was already allowed and often ran out unused. They are about how the model checks and recovers.

👀

1. Verifying physical success

Camera looks right ≠ task is truly done. Real checks need contact, support and alignment, not appearance.

💧

2. Recovering from irreversible actions

Some moves change the world permanently. A spill or a dropped part has no undo, even with time left.

🧱

3. Preserving progress

Long jobs need earlier wins to stay won. A later adjustment can knock them down, and small errors add up.

Verifying is the most common. In Hang mugs the head camera sees handle overlapping peg and the model treats that as "hung" — but the peg is not actually through the handle, so the mug ends on the table. Closing fingers is taken as "grasped" even when no contact happened. In Jigsaw Puzzle Assembly it says all four pieces make the rectangle while noting the episode hasn't ended, then its corrections pull pieces apart instead of closing gaps. In Fill egg holder it reports all four eggs seated and still gets only partial credit. Visual plausibility diverged from the benchmark's true physical condition.

Irreversible is different from just failing. In Pour liquid into cup the cup tips early and liquid spills on the table; righting the cup and pouring again can't bring spilled liquid back, and the episode ends with budget unused. In DexJoCo Assembly a peg slips to the floor, is judged unreachable, and the arms park with most steps left. When a failed step is also a prerequisite — like opening a container before you can use what's inside — nothing downstream can advance until you fix it, and repeating the same motion wastes budget.

The paper's positive example matters: in Fold Glasses recovery worked only by changing strategy — switching to a side approach and doing a test lift to verify the grasp — before finishing after the normal limit. Success came from a new plan plus a real physical check, not another identical try.

Preserving progress shows up when horizons get long. In Store laptop and headphones both items are already placed, then a tweak to one earcup pulls the headband off its stand and it is never re-hung. In BEHAVIOR's can_meat a held jar tilts and falls while doing the next motion. Small slips in grasping, placement or navigation alter the setup for later actions. Reliable long tasks need to keep re-checking earlier conditions, not assume a subgoal stays done.

These are why more study notes alone don't close the gap. In the RoboDojo Open group — which had no task-specific demonstrations, only notes from other tasks — GPT-6-Astra with ICL got its lowest success rate, even with extra steps. In the real-world and household tests ahead, demos are missing entirely and the world doesn't pause, making verification, irreversible failures and progress loss even more visible.

008The future~12 min

A Home That Tests Like Real Life

Lab tests were kind to GPT-6-Astra: same puzzles, same start, and the simulator paused while it thought. A real home is not kind. To see what that means, the authors built a different kind of exam — a one-bedroom apartment in NVIDIA Isaac Sim that works like a real home.

They started with public furniture and a floor plan, then had coding agents place every object at real-world size on named supports — reviewed by people — and then tied every part to physics: hinges swing, drawers slide, position-based fluids pour, cloth folds, contacts collide, and heat flows from burner to pan to food. The point is causal fidelity: if the robot’s decision depends on it, the simulator should get the cause and effect right, grounded in real references.

Why change the exam? Old benchmarks were built for imitation: collect demos, fit a policy, test on held-out episodes of the same task. Success meant a fixed goal formula (BDDL) was true at one moment — e.g., "bacon cooked." A real request is "make breakfast." There are many good answers, from toast to a full meal, and the value depends on when it arrives.

That motivates the paper’s three principles for next-generation benchmarks:

1) Tasks from human needs. No fixed goal state. The judge gives utility over the whole outcome — graded, not pass/fail. Toast in 10 minutes can beat a full breakfast in 60 if the resident leaves in 15. Predicates like "burned" are defined on private physical quantities the robot cannot see directly.

2) No in-domain demos. The agent gets no training episodes for this home. It must explore at test time — map the layout, test the weird air fryer dial, learn where its wide body fits — and exploration costs world time and can cause side effects.

3) A world that keeps running. Thinking time is simulated: between observation and next command, the previous command keeps executing. The judge watches the whole trajectory on the world clock, even after the agent says "done." Obligations become temporal rules: :never leave the stove on unattended, :whenever you open the fridge, close it within a deadline. Those combine Intent (what was asked) with Duty (what you should do anyway).

A final-state check would miss most home failures. In Fig. 12 the fridge is closed at the end, and the book is on the shelf — but the door was open from 300s to 765s, and desk items were knocked to the floor. Only a trajectory judge sees it. This is why the paper says PDDL-style benchmarks need metric and signal temporal logic to state deadlines.

In this apartment GPT-6-Astra ran zero-shot on a G2 robot, seeing only onboard cameras, no demos. It failed in revealing ways: it focused on the book in its gripper while its elbow pushed the monitor and knocked things down; it stored milk, turned to carry fruit, and left the fridge open for 465 seconds; it held the fridge door with one arm but from where it stood the door blocked the other arm from the bacon — letting go made the door swing shut. With an unfamiliar tap it kept pushing the handle instead of turning it while the sink — drain closed — filled and spilled onto the floor. And watching an egg, it correctly decided "turn off when set," but deciding took 8 seconds with the burner still on, and by the time the burner went off, about 90 seconds after setting, the egg had charred.

These are not just quirks. Building a fair judge is still hard: vision-language success detectors reach only ~60% balanced accuracy on contact-rich assembly, simulators can be wrong in either direction, and an hour-long deployment is expensive to repeat. But the direction is clear — the next exam will score what you did and when, not just where things ended up.

Try it yourself below: the kitchen will not pause for you. Set how long GPT-6-Astra takes to think, then try to catch the egg exactly when it sets — while keeping an eye on the fridge and sink the judge never forgets.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Embodied intelligence

Intelligence that lets an AI understand and act in the physical world through a body - seeing, moving, touching - not just chatting or drawing.

VLA

Vision-Language-Action model: an AI trained to turn what it sees and a language instruction into robot actions. Usually needs training on many robot demonstrations.

WAM

World Action Model: an AI that starts from video prediction - guessing how the scene will look next - and also outputs actions. Another family of robot policies.

Benchmark

A test environment for robots, often in simulation, with a fixed task, success rule, and step limit. Ex: LIBERO, Bench2Dex, BEHAVIOR.

In-Context Learning (ICL)

Learning without changing the model's weights: you give examples or notes in the prompt, and the model uses them right away.

Knowledge package (K)

The frozen set of notes, images, motion examples and helper code GPT-6-Astra builds from training demonstrations and re-uses at test time. Weights stay fixed.

Step budget / interaction budget

How many actions the robot is allowed before the episode is stopped. 1× is the official limit; 10× is ten times that, in the same episode without resetting.

Action chunking (L)

How many low-level moves the big brain reviews at once. L=1 means check every single step; L=4 means check four moves per call, which saves tokens.

System 1 / System 2 / harness

Fast, low-level controller (System 1, e.g. π0.5) that executes motions vs slow deliberative reasoner (System 2, GPT-6-Astra) that plans, checks and recovers. A harness connects them.

Zero-shot

Trying a new task with no examples of that exact task and robot. Only general knowledge plus sometimes a human-written robot description.

Active vision

Moving your own camera to see better - e.g., moving a free arm so its wrist camera can see a vase mouth - instead of just moving the hand.

Intent vs Duty

The main request ('make breakfast') vs unstated obligations a helper should also satisfy ('don't leave the fridge open, turn off the stove'). The paper judges both.

World clock

In real life and the proposed benchmark, time keeps running while the AI thinks. Thinking 8 extra seconds means the egg keeps cooking.

Success rate (SR)

How many episodes out of 100 the robot fully satisfies the benchmark's check.
