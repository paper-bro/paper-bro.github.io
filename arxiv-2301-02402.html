001Lesson 1~6 min

Why finding everything precisely is so hard

Imagine you run a warehouse, a hospital, or a VR arena. You want to stick a cheap, battery-free sticker on every box, tool, or controller — hundreds or thousands of them — spread over a soccer-field-sized area, and know exactly where each one is, to better than a fingertip width, instantly. And you want to do it with a reader that costs about as much as a game console.

That is the wishlist Hawkeye starts from. In numbers it is: hectometer range (100 m+), subcentimeter 3D accuracy (under 1 cm), and large-scale (hundreds of tags at once). For asset tracking, smart factories, or keeping virtual objects glued to real ones, you need all three together. If you lose any one — if you can only cover a room, or you are off by 15 cm, or you can only read a few tags per second — the experience breaks.

Before Hawkeye, you had to choose which to give up. That is not because engineers were lazy, but because of physics and rules. Classic backscatter like UHF RFID lives below 6 GHz, where regulations only give you tens of kHz of bandwidth to work with. Think of bandwidth like the sharpness of your ruler: a narrow-band signal makes a fat, blurry peak when you try to measure distance, so you get errors of tens of centimeters. Workarounds needed you to roll tags past on fixed tracks or litter the area with reference tags you already knew the position of.

Newer systems cheated by faking wider bandwidth. RFind and TurboTrack stitch together 220 MHz and reach 3–5 mm accuracy, but they step outside the ISM band rules and only work at 6–10 m before the signal must be turned down to stay legal. Millimetro moved to the 24 GHz mmWave band where you are legally allowed a full 250 MHz (here, 24 to 24.25 GHz). That got range — 180 m — but its accuracy was still stuck at the raw radar step of that band, about 15 cm in the median, 78 mm at 5 m in their table. Far, but not precise.

Hawkeye is the first to check all three boxes at once in real tests: 6.7 mm median error at 160 m (1D), 8.7 mm 2D at 100 m, 8.9 mm 3D indoors, 100 tags read in 33.2 ms (3.2 ms to ask + 30 ms to compute, with room for 1,024 different IDs), using a ~200-dollar commodity 24 GHz radar and a 8.4 microwatt tag. That is over 60× finer than the radar alone.

How? That is what the next lessons unpack: a tag that acts like a boomerang mirror so almost no energy is wasted, and a software trick that reads the hidden shape of the radio spectrum to turn a blurry 15 cm bin into a sharp mm ruler — all while staying inside the rules.

Try it yourself below: set how far you need to see, how precise you need to be, and how many tags you need, then switch between Classic RFID, Millimetro, and Hawkeye. Watch which dots stay green as you push to warehouse scale.

002Lesson 2~7 min

Radar rulers made of whistles

Imagine a ruler made of sound. You whistle a note that slides steadily upward — weeeEEE — and listen for its echo. If the wall is far, the echo comes back late. When you compare what you're whistling now to what comes back, you get a steady hum. The faster that hum wobbles, the farther the wall.

That slide-whistle is an FMCW chirp c(t). The radar sends it for a short time T while its frequency rises linearly across a bandwidth B. For Hawkeye, B = 250 MHz from 24 to 24.25 GHz, and each chirp lasts about T = 32 microseconds. The tag reflects a copy delayed by the round-trip time Δt = 2×distance / c, where c is the speed of radio waves (3×108 m/s). Mixing the sent and received whistles — multiplying them — leaves a single pure tone called the IF signal at range frequency fr:

$$ f_r = \frac{B}{T} \cdot \Delta t = \frac{B}{T}\cdot\frac{2R}{c} $$

fr is the distance. Double R, double fr. To read it, the radar runs an FFT — a fast math trick that sorts a wiggly signal into "how much of each frequency?" A sharp spike appears at fr, and where that spike sits tells you R.

Why bandwidth is the ruler's tick marks: the FFT can only tell frequencies apart in steps of 1/T Hz. Convert that step to distance via the formula above and you get c/(2B) — about 60 cm for 250 MHz. In practice the paper's plain FMCW gets ~15 cm median error (Section 5). That's why even with a huge 250 MHz band, just reading the tallest bin isn't enough for millimeters at 180 m.

Hawkeye keeps this exact same chirp — same cheap hardware. By default it uses one transmit and one receive antenna, 8 dBm transmit power, samples the IF at 1 MHz with 8192 samples per chirp, and repeats 2048 chirps per interrogation. Nothing fancy in the radio itself.

How do we know the truth to measure error? For ground truth the authors first place a Leica laser meter (1 mm resolution) on a tripod to measure radar-to-tag distance, then swap in the radar on the same tripod. On the tag side a linear stage with 0.01 mm resolution moves the tag by known tiny steps.

Try it below. Start in "chirps" view: drag distance from 1 to 180 m and watch the received whistle slide right and the gap Δt grow. Switch to "FFT" view: the peak marches right with distance. Now drag bandwidth from 50 to 250 MHz — see the peak get narrower. That's the whole limit Hawkeye has to beat in the next lessons: same narrow chirp, but reading between the tick marks.

Deeper: if you want numbers, at 100 m Δt = 0.67 µs and with B/T ≈ 7.8×1012 Hz/s, fr ≈ 5.2 MHz before sampling. The paper's evaluation front-end samples this IF at 1 MHz after mixing/down-conversion; the proportional relationship fr ∝ R is what matters for the lab.

003Lesson 3~7 min

Teaching radar to ignore the room

Your radar is shouting into a messy room. Walls, chairs, a metal filing cabinet — they all shout back. How do you hear just the tiny sticker you care about?

Hawkeye's trick is to make the room line up neatly, so anything that doesn't line up must be a tag.

Start with what the radar sends. Normal FMCW sends one whistle that rises in pitch — a chirp called $c(t)$ lasting $T = 32$ microseconds. Hawkeye sends that same chirp again and again, like a drum loop:

$$s(t)=c(t)\ast \sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(t-nT)$$

In plain words: take one chirp $c(t)$ and stamp a copy every $T$. The little $\ast$ just means "glue copies together" and $N$ is how many copies — Hawkeye uses $N=1025$ chirps (enough for up to 1024 different tag IDs). So $s(t)$ is just a perfectly periodic train of identical chirps.

Now listen. A reflection from clutter — walls and furniture — is just a delayed copy of what you sent:

$$s(t-\Delta t)$$

where $\Delta t$ is the round-trip travel time. Same shape, same period $T$. A signal that repeats every $T$ can only live on very special frequencies: multiples of $1/T$. With $T=32$ microseconds that's $1/T = 31.25$ kHz, so $0$, $31.25$ kHz, $62.5$ kHz and so on.

Think of it like stadium seats. Clutter is an obedient crowd that only sits in the numbered seats $n/T$. Every wall, every chair reflection piles into those same seats. All the seats in between stay empty. In a real office or hallway the room's echoes are smeared over $230$ microseconds of delay — normally a disaster — but here they all collapse onto that same neat comb.

A Hawkeye tag breaks the rule on purpose. It blinks its reflection on and off at its own private speed $f_m$, say $150$ kHz, different for each tag. That's Frequency-Shift Keying. Its signal is:

$$s(t-\Delta t)\cdot e^{j2\pi f_m t}$$

The $e^{j2\pi f_m t}$ part just means "wiggle at $f_m$" — it's multiplying by the tag's blink. That wiggle changes the period. Instead of $T$, the tag's period becomes the least common multiple of $T$ and $1/f_m$. In frequency, everything is shifted by $f_m$ — off the numbered seats.

Clean gap = findable tag. Because clutter only fills bins $n/T$, a tag at $n/T + f_m$ sits in the zeros. Try $f_m=0$ and it collides — which is exactly what ordinary FMCW does.

That difference is what you see in the paper's Figure 9. Ordinary FMCW (left) is a messy overlap. HD-FMCW (right) is two combs that don't touch: blue clutter on $n/T$, orange tag floating between them. Later lessons will teach how Hawkeye then measures that offset $f_m$ and reads the hidden distance inside it, but this isolation is the foundation.

Try it yourself: turn clutter on, add the tag, slide $f_m$, and watch the orange peaks land in clean gaps. Then switch to $N=1$ to break the drum loop and see why one chirp alone can never separate room from tag.

004Lesson 4~9 min

Boomerang antennas and lossless flips

A normal mirror is a bad tag. Shine a flashlight at it from the side and the beam bounces off sideways — back to your eyes only when you stand straight in front of it. Hawkeye needed the opposite: a postage-stamp that always throws the signal back to whoever shone it, from almost any angle, up, down, left or right.

The trick is a planar Van Atta Array (VAA). Think of 16 tiny patch antennas in a 4×4 grid on a circuit board (Rogers RO4003C). Each patch by itself is tuned to be perfectly matched — Hawkeye tuned the feed point so less than 10% bounces back at the antenna itself (S11 < -10 dB across the whole 250 MHz band at 24 GHz). That leaves the signal free to travel through wires.

The wiring is the boomerang: opposite antennas are wired together in centrosymmetric pairs — top-left to bottom-right, top-right to bottom-left, and so on. The wires (transmission lines) differ in length by exact multiples of the guided wavelength $\lambda_g = 7.56$ mm (the wavelength inside the board at 24.125 GHz). If a wave arrives at $30^\circ$, the left patches see phase $-2\varphi$, the right $-\varphi$. After traveling through the paired wires, the order flips to $-\varphi, -2\varphi$ — a mirror image that re-radiates back toward the source. Paper measurement: 17.2 dB stronger than a same-size metal plate in azimuth over a $90^\circ$ field of view, and 23.9 dB in elevation over $140^\circ$.

Real boards aren't perfect. Hawkeye caps the longest wire to only $9\lambda_g$ longer than the shortest. That keeps the worst phase error under $16.9^\circ$ across the 250 MHz band — just $0.05$ dB loss. It's why the boomerang still works anywhere in the band.

Now add the ID. To send a 0 or 1 without losing half the signal, Hawkeye doesn't absorb — it flips. Each of the 8 antenna pairs has a $90^\circ$ hybrid coupler and two tiny PIN diodes. With the right impedances — $35.3\,\Omega$ and $50\,\Omega$ quarter-wave segments — the incoming wave splits into four paths inside the coupler. Two paths cancel at the input, two add at the paired antenna, so energy slides through with <$1.17$ dB loss (measured across 24 GHz). Toggling the diodes between low and high bias changes the reflection path length, adding exactly $180^\circ$. Error stays under $3^\circ$ (only $0.003$ dB). An open-ended $\lambda_g/4$ stub acts as an RF choke, blocking >$30$ dB so bias current doesn't leak and spoil the retro beam.

005Lesson 5~6 min

Ghost echoes and how to mute them

Inside a building, a radar ping doesn't take one path — it takes many. It hits the tag, but also the walls, desks, and ceiling, and each bounce comes back a little late. An ordinary tag is like a shiny ball: it scatters those late bounces in all directions, so the radar hears a forest of ghost copies spread over time.

The paper measured just how bad that forest is. In an office and a hallway, the ghost delays stretched past 230 microseconds — that's the time for radio waves to zig-zag the room many times over. On a delay profile (power vs. delay) the room alone shows tall bumps long after the first arrival.

Hawkeye's tag works like a boomerang, not a ball. Its planar Van Atta Array wires opposite antennas together with transmission lines that flip the phase, so it only sends energy back exactly where it came from. A wall bounce that left at a wrong angle simply doesn't get returned. Late echoes have nowhere to go except back to the radar only if they were aimed at the radar in the first place.

The result is in Figure 8 below — compare the top (plain environment) and bottom (with Hawkeye). The first peak is the direct line-of-sight (LOS) path. With Hawkeye, every later peak drops to at least 20 decibels below it. A decibel (dB) is just a ratio: −20 dB means 100× weaker in power, 10× weaker in amplitude. The time-spread collapses.

Key insight: retro-reflectivity turns multipath from "echoes everywhere" into "echoes only back to the source," so indoor clutter looks almost like open field.

How much does that matter for accuracy? A lot. In a hallway test the radar-to-tag line was moved out to 80 meters, and Hawkeye kept a median error under 4 millimeters the whole way, with 100% detection. The ghost error was only about 8.8 mm in the office and 2 mm in the hallway — tiny for a room full of reflections.

Even blocking the tag barely hurts, because the direct retro path is so dominant. The paper tried a 3.25 mm cardboard sheet in front of the tag at 2.2 meters: median error went from 2.2 mm to 3.4 mm (+1.2 mm). A 5 mm plywood board pushed it to 5.7 mm (+3.5 mm) — the 90th percentile was still 11.9 mm, near a centimeter. With a normal scatterer those blocks would smear the signal much more.

This cleaning step is what lets Hawkeye do real 3-D indoors with three radars stuck on a wall (at e.g., (1.8, 1.25, -1.2) meters and two nearby spots). Each radar sees only its own retro return, so they don't jam each other. By contrast, a single radar trying to guess angle (AoA) had a median angle error of 4.7° — at 100 meters that becomes meters of position error (100 m × 0.08 rad ≈ 8 m). Multilateration avoids that amplification.

Beyond this course: the full multipath math uses ray tracing and channel impulse responses, and the paper's gain numbers (17.2 dB azimuth, 23.9 dB elevation over a same-size flat plate) come from calibrated monostatic RCS measurements. We use a simplified delay-power picture here.

Try it yourself in the lab: start with retro gain at 0 dB (like a flat plate) and watch the hallway's late peaks tower over the LOS and the error jump to many centimeters. Slide it up toward 20 dB — Hawkeye's operating point — and see the ghosts shrink below the −20 dB line, the top-down bounces fade, and the error drop to millimeters. Then flip the blocker to cardboard or plywood and see why even NLOS stays near a centimeter.

006Lesson 6~10 min

Turning blur into a sharp ruler

Plain radar is blurry. Think of trying to read a ruler where you can only see marks every 15 centimeters — anything in between is a guess. That is Hawkeye’s starting point: with 250 megahertz of bandwidth at 24 gigahertz, the normal FFT can only place a tag to about 15 centimeters. Yet Hawkeye reports 2.5 millimeters. The trick is not new hardware, but reading the blur itself.

Remember two numbers from the last lessons. The room’s clutter — walls, desks, floors — all share one period T (one chirp length, 32 microseconds here). In the IF spectrum they pile up exactly on bins n/T. A tag is different: it flips its phase at its own rate fm, about 150 kilohertz and unique per tag, so its signal is:

$$ s(t-\Delta t)\,\cdot\,e^{j2\pi f_m t} $$

meaning the comb is shifted by fm away from the clutter bins. The other shift, fr, comes from the round-trip delay — and fr is the distance we want.

The problem: fm wanders. A cheap crystal can drift 500 parts per million with temperature. At 50 kilohertz that is 25 hertz, and Hawkeye shows that would look like 24.6 centimeters of distance error if you ignore it.

Step 1 — erase fm using the clutter. Because Hawkeye knows clutter must sit on n/T, it can measure how far the tag’s comb is offset from those bins. That offset is fm — plus a tiny Doppler fd if moving — found live, with no prior calibration. It then zeroes the n/T clutter bins and slides the tag comb back onto n/T. What is left is a clean tone at only fr. In the paper this survives a swing from 9.5°C to 38.4°C where fm wandered from 150.901 to 150.949 kilohertz: without the fix the error was 58.9 mm cold and 176.8 mm hot; with it, under 4 mm.

Step 2 — use the leak to get super-resolution. Even after cleaning, fr rarely lands exactly on a bin. A tone of length T at frequency fr does not give one sharp line; it leaks with a characteristic shape:

$$ \text{FFT magnitude} \propto T\,\mathrm{sinc}\big(\pi T (f - f_r)\big) $$

peaks every 1/T with an envelope centered at the true fr. Normally you only see the coarse peaks and miss the center. Hawkeye takes just one T-long slice of that clean tone, adds zeros until it is Tpad=128T long, and FFTs again. Zero-padding does not add information, but it asks the FFT for 128 times more frequency points — bins every 1/128T — so the sinc envelope becomes clearly visible and its maximum can be picked as fr. All of this is just FFTs and IFFTs, O(N \log N), running in 30 milliseconds on a desktop.

Why this works: a longer window would normally give finer resolution, but we do not have a longer tone — we have only T of clean signal. Zero-padding interpolates the existing sinc without inventing new data, letting you locate its peak far more precisely than the original 1/T spacing.

The payoff at the radar is dramatic: median range error 2.5 mm — more than 60 times better than the 15 cm raw FMCW — holding 6.7 mm at 160 meters outdoors, and 100% detection to 140 meters. Try it below.

Play with the leak: set a true distance between bins, add drift, and watch the coarse peak lie. Turn on Hawkeye’s fm correction to pull the sinc back, then drag Tpad from 1× to 128× to densify the bins and watch the error fall from ~15 cm to a few millimeters — the same 60× jump the paper measures.

007Lesson 7~8 min

Many moving mirrors at once

So far the tag sat still. Real warehouses have robots, arms, shopping carts — everything moving. And there isn't one tag, there are hundreds, all shouting at the same radar at the same time. How does Hawkeye not get confused?

The key is that each tag has its own blink rate, $f_m$. Think of a choir where each singer holds a different note. One radar burst — in the paper $N=1025$ chirps, each $T=32$ microseconds long, so $32.8$ ms total — can hold a whole choir. Hawkeye spaces those notes about $30.5$ Hz apart. That spacing is tiny, but it's enough for $1024$ different IDs in one burst, and it tolerates cheap crystals that wander by $500$ ppm (about $75$ Hz at $150$ kHz) when the room gets hot or cold.

Movement adds two fingerprints on top of that note. First, Doppler — the motion itself shifts frequency by $f_d$. For a 24 GHz radar, $f_d = 2v/\lambda$, where $v$ is speed and $\lambda \approx 1.25$ cm is the wavelength. A robot ambling at $17$ cm/s, like in the paper, gets $f_d \approx 27$ Hz — about one whole ID slot. Hawkeye handles it the same way it already handles $f_m$ drift from temperature (Lesson 6): the peak you see is offset by $f_m + f_d$ from the clutter bins at $n/T$. Measure that offset, zero the clutter bins, shift the peak back, and both $f_m$ and $f_d$ disappear together. That's why the same $f_m$-removal trick works for moving tags (Fig. 12a).

The second fingerprint is smear. If the distance is changing during the chirps, the range frequency isn't one value $f_r$ anymore, it's a time-varying $f_r(t)$. On the FFT that looks like a broadened, shorter peak (Fig. 12b), not a sharp spike. Hawkeye uses that as a motion detector: if a tag moved more than $1$ cm within a symbol, the peak widens by $\ge 1.4$ Hz, and Hawkeye flags it as mobile.

To actually follow the motion, Hawkeye does the IFFT back to time, gets $f_r(t)$ as a wiggly tone, then zero-pads each little $T$-long snippet to $128\,T$ — the same sinc trick from Lesson 6 — to snap a location for that instant. Do that every $520\,T$ and you get $60$ updates per second. No new hardware, still $O(N\log N)$ FFTs.

Put it together and one burst locates everyone. Hawkeye processes tags iteratively, one $f_m$ slot at a time, pulling out its sinc and reading its center. In the paper 100 tags were packed $5$ mm apart on a $10\times10$ acrylic board, each blinking between $100$ and $250$ kHz. Three radars on a wall (retro-reflectivity means each radar only hears its own echo, so no access-control is needed, and the radars just share time over NTP to within a millisecond — only $3.6$ mm error even for a $13$ km/h runner) triangulated them in $33.2$ ms total. Median error was $19.9$ mm for that brutal dense pack; for 5 tags glued to a dancing humanoid robot the median 3D error was $7.66$ mm ($90^{th}$ percentile $15.96$ mm), and for a single robot moving $17$ cm/s it was $2.6$ mm.

Try it below: build your own choir of tags, make one run, change the spacing, and watch when the choir stays in tune — and when it turns to mush.

008Lesson 8~7 min

From lab bench to soccer field

Hawkeye sounds almost too good on paper: a $200 radar and a sticker-sized tag that sips 8.4 microwatts, yet finds things to a few millimeters at a soccer-field distance. Does it hold up outside the lab? The team carried the same hardware from a hallway to a concert hall to a real soccer field to find out.

The headline test is a straight 180-meter line outdoors. Every 20 m they parked a tag on the line — 100 trials at 20 tiny offsets per stop, checked against a Leica laser that is good to 1 mm. Out to 100 m, 9 out of 10 shots land within 8.9 mm. The middle shot — the median — stays under a centimeter all the way to 160 m, where it is 6.7 mm. And the radar still hears every tag to 140 m (100% detection), 96% at 160 m, dropping to 54% only at 180 m. That is the same 250 MHz at 24 GHz that Millimetro used to get ~15 cm, but Hawkeye sharpens it by more than 60× in software.

Indoors the air is full of bounces. In a multipath-rich hallway they repeated the line to 80 m and the median never rose above 4 mm, with 100% detection — the retro-reflective tag keeps non-line-of-sight copies at least 20 dB below the direct copy. For real 3D they put three radars on a wall in a concert hall and triangulated one tag moved on a stage that is accurate to 0.01 mm. Median 3D error: 8.9 mm, with 5.9 mm along x, 2.7 mm along y, 3.4 mm along z; 90% of shots under 13.8 mm. Outdoors they did 2D with just two radars forming a 100-m equilateral triangle with the tag: median 8.7 mm (6.2 mm x, 4 mm y), 90th 15.9 mm. With only one radar the angle error alone (median 4.7°) would spray the 2D guess by 2.08 m at 20–25 m — distance is still 2.7 mm, but you need multilateration to keep hectares precise.

Hawkeye at a glance versus the best before it: at 5 m Hawkeye's median is 2.5 mm and it still works to 180 m for 100 tags (1024 IDs in theory). Millimetro is 78 mm at 5 m over 180 m for ~6 tags; RFind is 3.4 mm but only to 6 m; TurboTrack 5.1 mm to 10 m for 2 tags. Only Hawkeye hits range, accuracy, and scale together, on an unmodified commodity radar.

Scale and everyday mess were tested too. One hundred tags — built as a dense 10×10 board with only 5 mm gaps, the worst case for coupling — were localized together in 33.2 ms (3.2 ms of chirps + 30 ms processing). Median 3D error 19.9 mm even that cramped, 90th 48.4 mm, each tag on its own fm tone from 100 to 250 kHz spaced 30.5 Hz apart, so even a cheap 500-ppm crystal can't cause ID collisions. Put cardboard (3.25 mm) or plywood (5 mm) in front and the median rises only 1.2 mm (to 3.4 mm) and 3.5 mm (to 5.7 mm). Chill the tag oscillator to 9.5°C or heat it to 38.4°C so fm wanders from 150.901 kHz to 150.949 kHz, and Hawkeye — because it measures fm fresh each shot from the offset to the clutter comb — stays under 4 mm. Without that correction the same wander would be 58.9 mm and 176.8 mm.

The paper is clear that these are measured numbers, not simulations, and it will release code and antenna files. ABCWord bank

Word bank.

FMCW chirp

A radar signal whose frequency rises linearly with time during each chirp. The delay of the reflection tells distance.

IF signal & range frequency fr

Signal made by mixing transmitted and received chirps. Its frequency fr is proportional to round-trip delay and thus distance.

Bandwidth & resolution

Range the signal can tell apart, set by bandwidth. 250 MHz ≈ 60 cm raw resolution; phase tricks improve it.

RCS (Radar Cross Section)

How strongly a tag reflects back. Measured in dB; higher means detectable farther.

Retro-reflectivity

Tag that sends waves back to exactly where they came from, instead of scattering everywhere. Key for 3D and indoor robustness.

Planar Van Atta Array (VAA)

Flat antenna array where opposite antennas are wired together with transmission lines differing by guided wavelength λg. Flips phase sequence to steer reflection back to source.

Guided wavelength λg

Wavelength inside the circuit board (7.56 mm at 24.125 GHz center on Rogers RO4003C). Sets TL length differences to multiples of λg, max spread 9λg for <16.9° error.

FSK & modulation frequency fm

Frequency-Shift Keying: tag flips phase 180° periodically at fm (e.g., 150 kHz). Gives each tag an ID and moves its signal away from clutter.

HD-FMCW

In HD-FMCW all wall/furniture reflections land on bins n/T. Tag's fm shifts it to bins in-between, so tag is isolated from clutter.

Spectral leakage & sinc envelope

When a frequency isn't exactly on an FFT bin, energy leaks to neighbors with envelope T·sinc(πT(f-fr)) centered at true fr. Hawkeye uses the envelope shape for super-resolution.

Zero-padding

Appending zeros to a time snippet of length T to make it Tpad=128T long. Makes FFT bins denser, revealing the sinc center with mm accuracy without new hardware.

Doppler fd

Extra frequency from motion. Hawkeye removes fm+fd together; time-varying fr(t) causes dispersion ≥1.4 Hz if moved >1 cm per symbol.

Multilateration / AoA

Using distances from 2-3 radars to triangulate x,y,z. Works without coordination because VAA only returns to its interrogator.
