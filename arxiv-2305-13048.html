001~8 min

Why your chatbot gets slower the longer you chat

Imagine you ask a chatbot a follow-up question. To answer sensibly, it needs the whole chat so far. A Transformer — the engine behind most chatbots today — literally re-reads every previous word and compares every word to every other word. With 100 words that's 10,000 comparisons. With 1,000 words that's 1,000,000. It gets slow fast, and it eats battery and memory.

That pattern has a name. Computer scientists write it as a cost in Big-O notation. Say $T$ is the number of tokens (roughly words) in the history, and $d$ is the model's width (how big each word-vector is, often a few thousand numbers). Then:

$$\text{Transformer inference: } O(T^2 d)\text{ time, } O(T^2 + T d)\text{ memory}$$

$O(T^2 d)$ means work grows like $T$ times $T$ times $d$. Double $T$, you do about four times the work. Memory is similar: the Transformer keeps a $T \times T$ table of who-attended-to-whom, plus $T \times d$ word vectors. The paper lists these numbers in Table 1.

Recurrent networks (RNNs) do the opposite. An RNN reads one word at a time, updates a tiny memory called a state $h_t$ (memory after token $t$), and throws the old words away. That is cheap: time grows like $T$, not $T^2$, and memory stays tiny. But it has a catch for training: to learn, an RNN needs $h_t$, which depends on $h_{t-1}$, which depends on $h_{t-2}$... you can't compute all steps at once. So RNNs can't use thousands of GPUs in parallel the way Transformers can.

The paper's goal in one sentence: build a single model that trains in parallel like a Transformer but runs step-by-step like an RNN. From Table 1, their model RWKV costs only
$$\text{RWKV inference: } O(T d)\text{ time, } O(d)\text{ memory}$$
That is linear in $T$ for time and constant in $T$ for memory — roughly $T$ times cheaper than a Transformer for long chats.

How? RWKV replaces the expensive every-to-every comparison with a simple fading memory rule (you'll meet the exact formula in the next lessons). For now, focus on the cost difference: if $d$ is fixed, RWKV's work climbs like a gentle ramp when $T$ grows, while a Transformer's work climbs like a steep parabola. Same for memory: RWKV carries just a few vectors ($O(d)$) from step to step, no giant $T \times T$ table.

That matters for phones and laptops, where memory and battery are limited, and for very long documents where $T$ can be thousands or tens of thousands. In the lab below, drag the chat length $T$ from 100 to 8,000 tokens and toggle between Time and Memory. Watch the two curves race — and check the live numbers for how many times cheaper RWKV becomes as the chat gets longer.

002Background~10 min

How Transformers pay attention to everything — and why RWKV fades the past

Imagine you are reading a mystery. A Transformer is like a detective who, for every new clue, re-reads all previous pages and compares the new clue to each one, one pair at a time. That is powerful, but a lot of work — if the book has $T$ words, it does about $T \times T$ comparisons.

That pairwise work is written in the paper as:

$$\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}(QK^{\top})V$$

In plain words: each word makes three little vectors. $Q$ is its question: "what am I looking for?" $K$ is its label: "what do I offer?" $V$ is its content: "what do I actually mean?" For the current word $t$, the paper (Eq. 8) computes a vote from every past word $i$:

$$\text{vote from } i \text{ to } t \propto e^{q_t^{\top} k_i}$$

You take the dot-product $q_t^{\top} k_i$ — a number that is big if question and label match — put it inside $e^{(\cdot)}$ to make it positive, then divide by the sum of all votes so they add to 1. That weighted average of all $v_i$ is the output. Every word can vote for every other word. The whole $T \times T$ table of votes is learned.

The paper then asks: what if we don't need a full table? The Attention-Free Transformer (AFT) in Eq. 9 tried this:

$$\text{vote} \propto e^{w_{t,i}+k_i}$$

Here $w_{t,i}$ is a learned bias that depends only on positions $t$ and $i$, not on the words themselves, plus a per-word importance $k_i$. It still needs a huge $T \times T$ matrix $w_{t,i}$ — one number for every pair of positions.

RWKV's squeeze: Don't learn a whole matrix. Learn one fading number per channel.

RWKV (Eq. 10) replaces the whole matrix with a single rule:

$$w_{t,i}=-(t-i)\,w$$

$w$ is a tiny vector (one value per channel) that the model learns, and it must be $\ge 0$. The distance $(t-i)$ is how many steps ago word $i$ was. So the vote becomes $e^{-(t-i)w + k_i} \odot v_i$.

Because $-(t-i)w \le 0$, we always have $e^{w_{t,i}} \le 1$. And it shrinks exponentially as you go back: one step ago weight $\approx e^{-w}$, two steps ago $e^{-2w}$, three steps $e^{-3w}$, and so on. If $w$ is large, the model forgets fast and only the last words matter. If $w$ is near 0, $e^{-(t-i)w} \approx 1$ and it remembers far back.

That means no $T \times T$ table. For each channel the model just learns how fast to fade. The current word's own importance $k_i$ can still boost an old word if it was important, but the built-in bias is always to fade.

In the lab below you can feel the difference. Click a query word and watch who gets to vote. On the left, a Transformer can give strong votes to words anywhere depending on the $q_t^{\top}k_i$ match. On the right, RWKV's votes follow the fading bar — slide $w$ up and far-away words disappear, slide it to 0 and they all stay level. 003~10 min

The fading-memory formula that replaces attention

Imagine you ask a friend what happened at a party last week. They don't replay every second equally. A vivid moment from days ago can stick, yesterday's details matter a bit, and what just happened matters a lot. RWKV's memory works like that — with a simple fading rule.

Transformers remember by comparing every word to every other word. RWKV asks a cheaper question: for the current word t, how much should each past word i count? It answers with three ingredients you can read off:

  • v_i — the content of word i (the vector of numbers stored for that token)
  • k_i — the importance of word i (did this token deserve a louder vote?)
  • w — the time decay (a learned number ≥0 per channel) that controls how fast the past fades)
The heart of RWKV — Eq. 16 in the paper — is just a weighted average:
$$wkv_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{t-1} e^{-(t-1-i)w + k_i} \odot v_i + e^{u+k_t}\odot v_t}{\sum_{i=1}^{t-1} e^{-(t-1-i)w + k_i} + e^{u+k_t}}$$
Read it in plain English: numerator = add up all past contents v_i, each multiplied by its weight. Denominator = add up those same weights to normalize. So wkv_t is "the average of what I've seen, weighted by how recent and how important it was". ⊙ just means multiply channel-by-channel.

Let's unpack each weight. For any old token i < t:

$$\text{weight}_{i} = e^{-(t-1-i)\,w + k_i}$$

(t-1-i) is how many steps ago it was — 0 for yesterday, 10 for ten steps back. Multiply by w and put a minus sign: -(distance)·w. Because w ≥ 0, e^{-(distance)·w} ≤ 1 and it shrinks exponentially as distance grows. That's the fading memory. Small w (near 0) = slow fade, long half-life, remember far back. Large w = fast fade, only recent tokens matter. k_i then nudges it up or down — a memorable word gets a bigger k_i and survives longer.

What about the word right now, t itself? History could drown it out, so RWKV gives it a separate bonus. The paper calls this u:

$$\text{weight}_{t} = e^{u + k_t}$$

u is also learned per channel. Think of it as "extra loudness just for now" — it isn't multiplied by any distance, so you can crank up the current token without affecting the decay chain for the past. That prevents the model from forgetting what it just read.

This one formula replaces the QKᵀ attention matrix. No table of size T × T — just one w and one u vector per channel, plus per-token k_i, v_i. Different channels learn different w: some carry long context, some focus locally. The paper shows exactly that later.

004~8 min

A cheap trick: blend the last two words

RWKV has a sneaky-simple trick for seeing word pairs like "New York" or "hot dog" without doing expensive attention. It just mixes today's word with yesterday's word before it does anything else.

For each channel, it learns a number μ (called mu, between 0 and 1) that says how to blend the two:

$$r_t = W_r \cdot (\mu_r \odot x_t + (1-\mu_r)\odot x_{t-1})$$

This is equation 11 from the paper. Read it left to right: x_t is today's input, x_{t-1} is yesterday's, μ_r picks the mix, and W_r is a learned matrix that projects it to the right size. The same idea makes k_t and v_t (equations 12–13), and the channel-mixing block does it again for r'_t and k'_t (equations 14–15).

Why bother? If μ=1 you see only today — you can't tell "New York" from just "York". If μ=0 you see only yesterday — equally useless. A learned middle like μ=0.5 sees both at once, so the model gets free local context, like a cheap bigram detector, with no extra layers. The paper implements this as a tiny time-shift (in code: nn.ZeroPad2d((0,0,1,-1))) — just slide the sequence by one).

Each channel learns its own μ. One channel might learn μ≈0.9 (care about the new word), another μ≈0.2 (care about the old word). Together they cover many pair-patterns.

Mixing is only half the story. After the model computes its fading memory wkv_t (from the last lesson), it has to decide how much to let out. That's the receptance gate:

$$o_t = W_o \cdot (\sigma(r_t) \odot wkv_t)$$

This is equation 17. σ is the sigmoid — it squashes r_t to between 0 and 1. Think of σ(r_t) as a bouncer: near 1 = door open, let the memory through; near 0 = door closed, block it. ⊙ means multiply element-wise, so each channel has its own bouncer.

The channel-mixing block does the same gating but with a different inner activation:

$$o'_t = \sigma(r'_t) \odot (W'_v \cdot \max(k'_t,0)^2)$$

This is equation 18. max(k',0)^2 is squared ReLU — if k' is negative, output 0; if positive, square it. Squaring makes strong signals stand out even more, and the σ(r') gate again decides what passes.

In the lab below, blend yesterday and today yourself. Move the μ sliders between 0 and 1 for the two word pairs and watch the blended vectors, the gate opening, and the final output change. See if you can find why the middle blend is the only one that "knows" it's a pair.

005~10 min

One equation, two superpowers

The trick that makes RWKV special is embarrassingly simple: one formula can be run two different ways.

In lesson 3 you met the centerpiece, the WKV operator. In plain words it is a weighted average of past values:

$$wkv_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}e^{-(t-1-i)w + k_i}\odot v_i + e^{u + k_t}\odot v_t}{\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}e^{-(t-1-i)w + k_i} + e^{u + k_t}}$$

The top adds up what was said ($v_i$) times how much it should count. The bottom is the normalizer that keeps it an average. $k_i$ is importance of token $i$, $v_i$ its content, $w\ge 0$ is a per-channel decay that fades old tokens as $e^{-(t-i)w}$, and $u$ is a learned bonus just for the current token so today is not drowned by history.

Training superpower: do everything at once. A layer spends most work on big matrix multiplies $W_{r,k,v,o}$ for all tokens — cost $O(BTd^2)$ for batch $B$, length $T$, width $d$ — just like a Transformer. The WKV weighted sum itself is only $O(BTd)$ and is parallel over batch and channels. No serial bottleneck on the heavy part.

Inference superpower: remember almost nothing. Rewrite that sum as a rolling update that carries just two tiny vectors forward:

$$a_t = e^{-w}\odot a_{t-1} + e^{k_t}\odot v_t,\quad b_t = e^{-w}\odot b_{t-1} + e^{k_t},\quad wkv_t = \frac{a_{t-1} + e^{u+k_t}\odot v_t}{b_{t-1} + e^{u+k_t}}$$

Start with $a_0=0, b_0=0$ (Eq. 19–22). $a_t$ is the running numerator, $b_t$ the running denominator. To generate the next word you need only the previous $a_{t-1},b_{t-1}$ and the new $k_t,v_t$ — never the whole history. That is why inference memory is $O(d)$, a constant per layer, not $O(T^2+Td)$ that grows with the conversation.

Real code adds one safety net: $e^{k_t}$ can overflow. So RWKV stores a shared exponent $p_t$ and always subtracts a max (Eq. 23–28), e.g. $q=\max(p_{t-1},u+k_t)$ and computes $e^{p_{t-1}-q}$ instead of huge numbers. With that trick the state per layer is five vectors — $x_t, y_t, a'_t, b'_t, p_t$ — totaling $5DL$ numbers ($4DL$ in perfect math). A constant, however long you chat.

What should the model remember? Figure 11 shows the answer the 169M model learned on its own. Early layers use large $w$, so $e^{-w}\approx 0$ — they look only locally. Late layers use $w\approx 0$, so $e^{-w}\approx 1$ — they carry information forever. The right panel traces a fact (“Eiffel Tower is in Paris”) retrieved in layer 4 just after “Eiffel” and then handed forward through time until the final “of” needs it.

Try the lab: flip between the two faces of the same equation. In parallel mode see all tokens light up at once; in sequential mode step token-by-token and watch the tiny $a,b$ state roll forward. Change $w$ to make memory long or short and see why inference stays $O(d)$.

006~8 min

The hidden recipes that make it trainable

RWKV's fading-memory trick is elegant on paper, but elegant doesn't mean easy to train. Early on the authors hit a very practical problem: the first steps of training were slow and wobbly. Fixing that took two hidden recipes — how you start the numbers, and proving the gradients won't blow up.

Recipe 1: start tiny, then stretch. Most language models start their word embeddings — the vectors that represent each word — with fairly large random numbers, like a normal spread with standard deviation 0.02. RWKV did the opposite: start every embedding with a tiny uniform number between ±1×10⁻⁴, then put a LayerNorm right after.

Why? Think of an arrow from the origin. If the arrow is long, pushing its tip sideways a little barely changes its direction. If the arrow is tiny, the same push swings it to a completely new direction — and LayerNorm then stretches that tiny arrow to normal length, magnifying the new direction. Appendix F and Figure 9 show the result: with a batch of 400 and the same tiny training run, the “small-init” loss curve dives noticeably faster than the normal 0.02 init. The model escapes its noisy starting randomness more quickly.

Small init = small arrows = cheap to turn. LayerNorm is the magnifier that makes that cheap turn count.

Recipe 2: a custom starting pattern for the rest of the model. Most weights Wr, Wk, Wv start at zero so the model begins silent, not noisy. The per-channel blends μ that mix xt and xt-1 and the decay w and bonus u start in a careful zigzag across depth l/L and channel i/s:

$$w_i = -5 + 8\cdot\left(\frac{i}{d-1}\right)^{0.7+1.3l/(L-1)}$$

Early layers get larger w (fast forgetting, local work), later layers get smaller w (slow forgetting, carry information). Bonus u starts as 0.5·(((i+1) mod 3)-1)+log 0.3 — just a little alternating wiggle so channels start distinct. The output projections start as 𝒩(0,2). Appendix E lists the full table; the paper notes this choice noticeably changes speed of convergence.

For the curious — the guarantee behind it:
In Appendix H the authors simplify wkv_T to a weighted average and show:
$$\left|\frac{\partial wkv_T}{\partial W_v}\right| = |E[x_t]| \le \max_t|x_t|$$
and
$$\frac{\partial wkv_T}{\partial W_k}=\text{cov}(x_t, v_t)$$
Translation: gradients for the key and value weights are bounded by the biggest input and by a covariance — they don't grow with sequence length T. So they can't explode as you read more tokens. Vanishing is controlled on purpose by the decay w itself, not by accident. The WKV scan still runs sequentially, so the team wrote a custom CUDA kernel to make that one step fast while all the big matrix multiplies stay parallel.

The paper is honest that this doesn't solve everything — a single vector state still funnels memory — but these starting tricks and the bounded-gradient design are why a 14-billion-parameter RNN could actually be trained stably like a Transformer.

Watch which loss dives first, how far a tiny arrow turns versus a long one after the same nudge, and notice the orange gradient line staying flat as the sequence gets longer — that's the no-explosion guarantee in action.

007~10 min

Scaling to 14 billion and keeping up with Transformers

Can a simple fading memory really keep up with a Transformer that remembers everything? The paper answers by building big and measuring.

The authors trained six RWKV models from 169 million to 14 billion parameters — by far the largest dense RNN ever at the time. Each was trained for one epoch on the Pile: 330 billion tokens, with a first context of 1,024 tokens. That is the same pile of text a comparable Transformer would see; the comparison is made FLOP-matched, meaning for the same amount of computer work, who does better?

Table 2 translates size into cost. For a forward pass on one token, the paper counts FLOP ≈ 2×(2VD + 13D²L) and about three times that for training. In numbers: 169M needs about 2.6×10⁸ FLOP per token, 14B needs about 2.8×10¹⁰. Six dots on a compute budget, from small to huge.

The most surprising result is not one big model, but 45 models of different sizes trained on different amounts of data. Plot test loss vs compute on log–log scales and RWKV falls on a straight line with r² = 0.994 on its best frontier — and even when the authors extrapolate another 10× more compute, the line still holds with r² = 0.875 (Fig 4). Earlier papers claimed LSTMs did not follow Transformer-style scaling laws. RWKV does.

Scaling is empty if scores are bad. On 12 NLP tasks averaged together (Fig 1: ARC Easy & Challenge, BoolQ, COPA, HellaSwag, LAMBADA, PIQA, Winogrande and more), RWKV at each size sits right next to Pythia, OPT and BLOOM trained for similar tokens and matched FLOPs. No single task dominates; the point is competitive at the same compute, but with linear-time inference.

What about really long context? The 1,024-token models were then finetuned longer: 2,048 for 10B extra tokens, then 4,096 for 100B, then 8,192 for 100B — all from the same Pile. Test loss on the Pile keeps decreasing as context grows (Fig 6). That matters because most open-source Transformers cannot efficiently train that long. RWKV shows it actually uses the extra history.

On the Long-Range Arena benchmark (sequences 1,000 to 16,000 tokens, Table 4) RWKV averages 72.07%, second only to the specialized S4 model at 86.09%. It is close on language-like tasks: ListOps 55.88 vs S4 59.60, Text 86.04 vs 86.82, Retrieval 88.34 vs 90.90, but lags on Image and Pathfinder. On Enwik8 text compression (Table 5) RWKV gets about 1.17–1.18 bits per character, essentially tied with a small Transformer at 1.13 while using O(Td) time and O(d) memory instead of O(T²d).

In short: same learning curve, same scores per FLOP, plus the RNN bonus of long, cheap context. Try it below — pick a model size to move along the scaling line, drag context to see loss fall, and switch to task bars to see where RWKV ties and where it still trails.

008~8 min

Lightning fast, but you have to ask nicely

RWKV is the car that gets great mileage but only has a small glovebox. It flies through long chats — yet it can only carry what fits in that box. If you pack the box in the wrong order, you won't find what you need.

Why it's lightning fast

Remember the paper's Table 1: for a chat of length T tokens and hidden size d, a Transformer needs about O(T²·d) time and O(T² + T·d) memory at inference. It has to re-look at every past token for every new token.

RWKV needs only O(T·d) time and O(d) memory — the same tiny state no matter how long the chat gets. The whole state is just 5·D·L numbers (and 4·D·L in perfect math): the inputs to time-mix and channel-mix, plus a numerator, denominator and exponent for the running average. Figure 7 in the paper shows it: Transformer's curve bends up like a parabola, RWKV's is a straight gentle line. On a phone or laptop, that is the difference between "still instant" and "out of memory."

Key trade: constant memory is fast, but you funnel all history through one vector. The past never stays in full detail — it fades by design.

Limitation 1 — funneling, not photocopying

The paper is honest about this in Section 9. RWKV's w lets it choose to remember (small w, decays slowly) or forget fast (large w), and the bonus u protects the current token. But because step t only sees the compressed summary at-1, bt-1 from before, tiny details from thousands of tokens ago can wash out. Learned decay helps, but it is mechanically more limited than full QK⊤ self-attention that can jump back to any exact token.

That is why long-context benchmarks are mixed: on Long-Range Arena RWKV averaged 72.07 vs S4's 86.09 — very strong on text-like tasks (ListOps 55.88, Text 86.04, Retrieval 88.34) but weaker on Image and Pathfinder where exact distant pixels matter.

Limitation 2 — you have to ask nicely

RNNs read left-to-right and can't look back to re-weight the instruction. Transformers can: if the instruction came early, they can still attend back to it with full strength later.

RWKV can't. Appendix L proves how much this matters. On RTE (does premise imply hypothesis?) the same 14B Raven model scored 44.2% F1 with a ChatGPT-style prompt:

"Having premise ... judge if hypothesis ... is logically connected"

Reorder to put the question first — "Is this entailment or not? premise: ... hypothesis: ..." — and the score jumped to 74.8% F1, almost double, with no extra training. Other tasks show the pattern: sarcasm detection RWKV even beat ChatGPT (50.96 vs 49.88), but MathQA collapsed (5.43% vs 71.4%) where intermediate results were in the "wrong" order for a forward-only reader. The authors' rule of thumb: put what you want answered after the data it needs.

Future work the paper suggests: a parallel-scan for training to reach O(B log T · d), a bigger state to remember more, reusing RWKV for encoder-decoder cross-attention, and tools to interpret and steer the state.

Try it below: flip the prompt order and watch RWKV's score swing while Transformer's barely moves. Then stretch the chat length — see linear time stay flat while Transformer explodes, and see that old tiny detail fade in RWKV's glovebox.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

FLOP

Number of operations a computer does. RWKV paper uses FLOP per token = 2*(2VD +13D²L) forward, 6× that for training.

Parameters

All the learnable numbers in the model. RWKV trained sizes from 169M to 14 billion parameters.

RNN (Recurrent Neural Network)

Reading a sequence step-by-step, keeping a small memory (state) from the past. Fast for generating text, but hard to train in parallel.

Transformer

Model that looks at all tokens at once with attention. Easy to train in parallel, but time and memory grow like T² with sequence length T.

Attention

A matrix trick behind Transformers. RWKV's WKV replaces the usual QKᵀ dot-product with a channel-wise weighted sum that can be done recurrently.

WKV operator

RWKV's core formula that mixes past values with decaying weights: wkv_t = ( sum e^{-(t-1-i)w+k_i}⊙v_i + e^{u+k_t}⊙v_t ) / ( sum e^{-(t-1-i)w+k_i}+ e^{u+k_t} )

Time decay (w)

RWKV's time-decay vector w ≥0. Controls how fast old information fades. Large w = forget fast, small w ≈0 = remember long. Trained per channel.

Bonus (u)

Extra learnable vector u that gives special weight to the current token itself, separate from the decay chain.

Token Shift (μ)

RWKV trick: each input is a mix of current and previous token: μ⊙x_t + (1-μ)⊙x_{t-1}. Lets model compare new vs old without extra cost.

Receptance (R)

The receiver gate: σ(r_t)⊙wkv_t . Decides how much of the past information to let through, like a bouncer.

Recurrent state

The small memory carried from step to step during generation: 5 vectors per layer (x_t, y_t, numerator a', denominator b', exponent p). Total 5DL numbers.

Scaling law

Rule of thumb like "loss falls in a straight line on log-log plot when you increase compute." RWKV follows it with r²=0.994 like Transformers.

Parallel training

Doing many tokens at once using matrix math. Transformers do this; classic RNNs cannot because step t needs step t-1.

Inference

Using the trained model to generate. RWKV inference cost is O(Td) time and O(d) memory vs Transformer O(T²d) and O(T²+Td).
