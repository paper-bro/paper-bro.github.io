001Lesson 1 · The problem~8 min

Why teaching a robot to dance costs a supercomputer

A humanoid that can skip, roll and do a full backflip by copying you sounds like magic — until you see the electricity bill. To teach that, researchers collected thousands of human dances and asked the robot to track them, frame by frame, in a physics simulator.

That tracking is what "whole-body control" means here: the robot watches a reference motion clip and tries to put every body part in the same place, at the same speed, without falling. The big public library used in the SuperMimic paper is called BONES-SEED. After filtering out impossible clips like walking up stairs, it still holds 129,514 clips = 264.56 hours of motion. More clips = a more capable robot, but also more homework.

One number to remember: GPU-hours = GPUs × hours. 1 GPU for 3 hours = 3 GPU-hours. 128 GPUs for 168 hours = 21,504 GPU-hours, reported as ~21,000 GPU-hours. That's the price tag.

Why the old way needed a supercomputer

There were two old routes. Either you trained a tiny expert for each clip and then squished all experts into one student — a lossy copy — or you brute-forced one giant generalist with model-free reinforcement learning (the algorithm called PPO). The best brute-force system, SONIC, took that second route: about 168 hours on 128 L40 GPUs, roughly 21,000 GPU-hours (128 × 168 = 21,504), to cover its 611 hours of motion.

Why so much? The paper's key insight is simple: whether the robot is skipping or doing the worm, it has the same skeleton, the same joints, and the same feet hitting the same floor. All dances share one set of rigid-body + contact physics.

PPO doesn't know that. It treats physics as a black box. It tries a noisy action, rolls the simulator forward, gets a score, and nudges the policy a little in the direction that scored higher. Then it does it again. Millions of times. It re-learns "what happens if I push here" from scratch for every new dance, instead of reusing a shared model of the world. So when you double the library, you roughly double the compute.

SuperMimic's bet is: learn that shared physics once, in a tiny differentiable copy, and reuse it for every dance. The claim is dramatic: 3 hours on 1 H100 GPU beat SONIC's checkpoint on the same 129,514-motion benchmark — more than 1,000× fewer GPU-hours (about 7,200× by raw numbers: 21,504 / 3) and more than 50× less wall-clock time (168 hours → 3 hours), with only 25 failures versus SONIC's 179. The paper notes 2.3× less data and at most 5.5× faster chips can't explain that gap.

In the lab, feel that gap yourself. Drag the dataset from 1 hour to the full 264.6 hours and flip between the two ways of learning. Watch GPU-hours, wall-clock time and how many dances still fail — and notice which curve stays almost flat.

002Lesson 2 · How learning normally works~10 min

How robots usually learn: try, fail, and noisy grades

Last lesson we saw the price tag: 128 GPUs for a week to copy 129,514 dances. Why is the old way so hungry? To understand the fix, we first need to see how robots usually learn without knowing any physics.

Think of training a robot like grading homework, but you don't have the answer key. You can only watch a student try something, give a score, and say "do more of what seemed good." That's model-free RL, and in this paper the version is called PPO.

Here are the pieces the paper uses to describe it. At each instant t the robot has a true state st — everything about its pose in the simulator. What the real robot actually feels is an observation ot, which is a slightly noisy view:

$$o_t = \bar{o}_t + \eta_t$$

\bar{o}_t is the clean sensor reading (joint angles q-q⁰, joint speeds, tilt from gravity, last action) and \eta_t is sensor noise. The paper actually lists it: about ±0.01 rad on joint positions, ±0.5 rad/s on joint velocities, ±0.2 rad/s on base angular velocity, and ±0.05 on projected gravity. The robot also gets a goal gt — for now, the next 10 frames of the human reference dance it should copy.

The policy is the brain: a deterministic rule at = \pi_{\theta}(ot,gt). The symbol \pi_{\theta} just means "the network with knobs \theta turns observations + goals into actions." An action at is not a torque directly; it is a normalized joint setpoint that gets mapped to q_{sp} = \alpha \cdot a_{t} + q^0 — a gentle spring target the motors pull toward. With low gains this acts like a torque proxy.

Why add extra noise when collecting data? During data collection the simulator actually executes a_{t} + \epsilon_{t} where \epsilon_{t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1^2) is small random wiggle per joint. That wiggle and the executed noisy action are saved in a replay buffer. The wiggle is not a mistake — it widens the examples the world model will later learn from.

After each try the robot gets a reward r_{t+1}. In the paper this is a dense score every 0.02 seconds: how close are the body positions, orientations and velocities to the reference, plus the root body, minus small penalties for jerky actions or hitting joint limits. A perfect track is near 1 per step, falling early pushes the total near 0.

But rewards in the future should count a little less — the robot cares more about not falling now than about a perfect pose 10 seconds from now. And if the episode already ended (the robot fell past 0.25 m height or 78° tilt), later rewards shouldn't count at all. The paper combines both ideas in one formula for the total score J:

$$J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\ge 0} \gamma^{t} c_t r_{t+1}\right] \quad\text{with } c_{0}=1,\; c_{t+1}=c_t(1-d_{t+1})$$

\gamma = 0.99 is the discount: each step forward multiplies that step's reward by another 0.99, so a reward 100 steps later counts only 0.99^{99} \approx 0.37 as much. d_{t+1} is 1 if the episode terminated, else 0. So c_{t}, the survival weight, stays 1 while the robot is up and snaps to 0 after a fall, zeroing every future term.

Now the key difference. PPO treats physics as a black box. It cannot ask "how would the reward change if I nudged this joint a tiny bit?" It can only do: sample a slightly different random action, run the simulator, see the advantage ("was that better than average?"), and nudge the policy in the direction \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} weighted by that advantage. That's the likelihood-ratio trick. Because each grade comes from one noisy rollout, the direction wobbles a lot — high variance. To average out the wobble you need millions of steps. The baseline in the paper uses 8192 parallel environments per GPU just to get enough samples.

That dense, every-step reward is why training is stable even with PPO, but stability is not speed. You are still grading by random tries instead of doing the math.

003Lesson 3 · The pocket simulator~10 min

Build a pocket world that predicts what happens next

Imagine trying to teach a friend to ride a bike, but you can't explain how the bike works — you can only shout "good!" or "bad!" after they wobble. That's how the old method learns. SuperMimic does something smarter: it builds a tiny copy of physics inside its head and uses it to practice.

That copy is called the world model. Its job is simple to state: look at what is true now, what you do, and what kind of world you're in, and predict what happens next.

$$\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t+1} = f_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t, \mathbf{a}_t, \boldsymbol{\xi})$$

Here $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t$ is the guess of the robot's state (joint angles, velocities, body poses), $\mathbf{a}_t$ is the action the policy chose (joint set-points), $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is the sim context — the hidden settings of this episode like friction $\mu$ between 0.3 and 1.2, tiny joint calibration offsets $\pm0.01$ radians, and torso center-of-mass shifts — and $f_{\omega}$ is a neural network with weights $\omega$ that we learn.

We give the same network extra heads so it also guesses the other things the simulator would produce: $\hat{r}_{t+1}=r_{\omega}(...)$ the tracking reward, $\hat{\bar{\mathbf{o}}}_t=g_{\omega}(...)$ the clean sensor reading before noise, and $\hat{d}_t=d_{\omega}(...)$ the chance the robot has fallen. Some heads are learned, some are computed directly — learned ones have a training cost, analytic ones don't.

How do we train it? We don't ask for one perfect step. We cut a short window of 8 real steps from a replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$ (up to 1000 steps per environment, 4096 environments per GPU, old data stays valid because physics doesn't care who collected it). Start at the logged real state $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_0 = \mathbf{s}_0$, roll the model forward 8 times on the logged real actions, and compare at every step:
$$\mathcal{L}_{WM}(\omega)= \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}} \sum_{j=1}^{8} \Big[ \|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_j-\mathbf{s}_j\|^2 + (\hat{r}_j-r_j)^2 + \|\hat{\bar{\mathbf{o}}}_j-\bar{\mathbf{o}}_j\|^2 + \text{BCE}(\hat{d}_j,d_j) \Big]$$

Each term is plain: squared error for state, reward and clean observation, and binary cross-entropy for fell-or-not. We use $k=8$ steps on purpose. If you only ever train on 1-step predictions, tiny errors snowball — like copying a drawing one line at a time without ever checking the whole shape. Training on 8 steps forces the model to correct its own drift, and in practice the paper finds it then stays stable for hundreds of steps, far longer than the 8 it was trained on.

The world model is deterministic — it doesn't invent randomness. Known noise like the action wobble $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_t$ and sensor noise $\boldsymbol{\eta}_t$ is handed in from outside, and $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is given as conditioning. That keeps predictions sharp.

004Lesson 4 · Learning through imagination~10 min

Teach by showing the math path, not by guessing

Imagine teaching a kid to shoot a basketball by only saying “good job” or “missed” after each throw. They would have to try hundreds of noisy variations to figure out what helped. That is how the old method learns: it nudges the policy in the direction of ∇_θ log π_θ — the direction that makes a sampled action more likely — weighted by how good the sampled return was. It works, but the grade is a noisy sample, so you need millions of tries.

SuperMimic teaches differently: it shows the exact math path from a future reward back to the action that caused it. The trick is a tiny learned copy of physics that you can do calculus through.

For each training step, SuperMimic freezes its learned world model f_ω and imagines. It pulls a starting state s₀ and its world settings ξ (like friction) from a replay buffer of past simulator trips. Then for H = 32 imagined steps it loops:

  • the model predicts the clean observation bar_o_t_hat = g_ω(s_t_hat, ξ),
  • it re-adds real sensor noise η_t, so the observation the policy sees is o_t_hat = bar_o_t_hat + η_t,
  • the policy acts a_t = π_θ(o_t_hat, g_t), where g_t is the goal (10 future reference frames),
  • the model steps forward s_{t+1}_hat = f_ω(s_t_hat, a_t, ξ) and predicts r_{t+1}_hat and d_{t+1}_hat.

The score it optimizes is the imagined return, with a helper to look beyond the imagination:

$$\hat{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s_0\sim\rho_0,\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1} \gamma^{t}\,\hat{c}_{t}\,\hat{r}_{t+1} + \gamma^{H}\,\hat{c}_{H}\,V_{\psi}(\hat{s}_{H},g_{H},\xi)\right]$$

In plain words: γ = 0.99 is the discount — rewards far in the future count a little less. c_t_hat is the soft survival weight — it is close to 1 if you have not fallen and fades toward 0 after a predicted fall, so imagined falls gently reduce future credit without a hard stop. r_{t+1}_hat is the predicted tracking reward. And V_ψ(s_H_hat, g_H, ξ) is the critic — a small network that guesses “how much more reward will come after step H.” Adding it means the world model only has to be accurate for 32 steps, not forever.

Why the chain rule helps: Because f_ω is differentiable, SuperMimic can apply backpropagation-through-time (BPTT). Think of the chain rule as dominoes: the reward at step H tugs on s_H_hat, which tugs on a_{H-1} via ∂f_ω/∂a, which tugs on s_{H-1}_hat via ∂f_ω/∂s, and so on all the way to a_0. The paper calls these first-order analytic gradients — one clean calculus arrow per step, instead of averaging many noisy guesses. In a head-to-head on the same world-model rollouts, the old sampled method (PPO-WM) needed about 64× more imagined steps to reach SuperMimic’s best return. The gain is not just that the world model runs fast; the gradients themselves are better.

That critic is why H can stay short. A paper test varied H on 40 LAFAN1 clips: without a critic, H ≤ 16 failed completely, and without a critic reaching a return of 0.82 was slowest. With a critic, H = 16 already matched the critic-free H = 32, and H = 32 was fastest to 0.82. Pushing to H = 64 then 128 nearly tripled the time and lowered the final return — more steps means more tiny model errors and more math per update, with no extra benefit.

Then drag H to 8, 64, or 128 and notice how the counter for “imagined steps to target” shoots up — that is exactly why SuperMimic sticks with H = 32 plus the critic’s guess beyond it.

005Lesson 5 · The loop that learns itself~10 min

The three jobs that loop together

Imagine a tiny factory with three workstations sharing one conveyor belt. No station works alone — the belt carries parts around in a loop, and each station gets better because the others did.

That is SuperMimic's training loop. Every iteration it cycles through (1) Collect, (2) Fit world model, (3) Improve policy. The shared belt is the replay buffer B — a memory of recent simulator trips (1000 steps for each of 4096 parallel environments on one GPU). It keeps getting overwritten with the newest trips.

1 — Collect: the current policy πθ runs in MuJoCo Warp — the GPU physics simulator where 4 tiny 5 ms physics substeps equal one 50 Hz control step — with a little action wobble ε ~ N(0,0.1²) added. The wobbly action actually executed, at+εt, plus the resulting state, are pushed into B, replacing the oldest entries. Old trips stay valid: physics does not care which policy collected them.

2 — Fit world model: sample a short k=8-step window from B, start the model at the logged state ŝ0=s0, roll it forward on the logged actions, and take one Adam step on its prediction loss LWM — squared errors on state, reward and observation plus a classification loss on fell-or-not. This 8-step autoregressive training is what makes very long imaginary rollouts stay stable later.

3 — Improve policy: freeze the world model, draw a starting state s0 and its simulator settings ξ from B, and imagine H=32 steps forward: predict observation, re-add sensor noise η, let πθ choose at, predict next state and reward. The score it climbs is roughly

$$\hat{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1} \gamma^{t}\hat{c}_{t}\hat{r}_{t+1} + \gamma^{H}\hat{c}_{H} V_{\psi}(\hat{s}_{H})\right]$$

— the imagined rewards for 32 steps plus a critic Vψ that guesses all reward beyond step H. Because the model is differentiable, calculus can flow gradients back through all 32 model Jacobians to tell each early action how it helped the final score. The critic itself is fit to its own bootstrapped targets:

$$\hat{R}_{t}=\hat{r}_{t+1}+\gamma(1-\hat{d}_{t+1})[(1-\lambda)V_{t+1}+\lambda\hat{R}_{t+1}],\quad \mathcal{L}_{V}=\mathbb{E}\sum (V_{t}-\hat{R}_{t})^{2}$$

with λ=0.95 and γ=0.99. Targets are frozen while the critic trains for 5 epochs over shuffled mini-batches. There is no extra target network, and soft survival weight ĉt replaces hard falling.

The critic is the shortcut. Without it the policy must earn all its reward inside the imagined window. On the paper's horizon sweep, without critic H≤16 fails completely, and H=32 is needed to learn at all. With critic, H=16 already matches critic-free H=32, and H=32 hits return 0.82 fastest (0.88 h). Make H too long and it backfires: H=64→128 nearly triples time to 0.82 and final return drops — too many Jacobians to chain and the H100 runs out of room.

The critic does not just save time — it changes behavior. When shoved while holding a T-pose at 0.5 to 4 m/s in simulation, the critic-trained policy settles in 0.5 to 2.4 seconds. The critic-free one keeps stumbling even at 0.5 m/s and falls at 4 m/s. Short horizon plus a good guess about the future beats a long, myopic stare.

006Lesson 6 · Why it scales~10 min

One brain for 129,000 dances: why it scales

Imagine you have to learn 129,000 dances. The brute-force way is to hire a new teacher for every dance. SuperMimic does the opposite: it builds one tiny brain that understands the one thing every dance shares — the same skeleton hitting the same floor.

That shared physics is why scaling works. Every clip, whether it's a skip, a side roll, or a 360-degree flip, is just the same humanoid body obeying the same contact rules, just in a different pose. A world model is a small neural network that learns those rules: given a state s, an action a, and world settings ξ like friction, it predicts what happens next.

The paper tests this directly. As the training library grows from 1 hour to 7 to 40 to 264 hours (from ~500 clips to all 129,514), the world model's mistake on 8 steps ahead stays flat: 0.00679, 0.00738, 0.01096, 0.00993 — roughly 0.007 to 0.011 the whole way. It is not memorizing dances; it is getting more evidence about the same body. More clips don't need a bigger model, just like reading more books in the same language doesn't require a bigger dictionary.

Because that one model captures the common physics, the computer needed to reach a good tracking score grows slowly — not in proportion to data. In the paper's sweep, GPU-hours to a target return go from about 0.539 to about 1.892 while data grows 264×. If cost grew with data, that last point would be 0.539 × 264 ≈ 142 GPU-hours — off the chart.

Then there are two separate speedups that multiply. First, raw speed: on one H100 at TF32 precision, the pocket world crunches far more steps per second than the full simulator MuJoCo Warp. At the 2,000–16,000 parallel copies researchers actually use (the shaded zone in the paper), it's more than 10× faster — 11× at 2k, 26× at 16k, and 62× at 64k where the simulator flattens. One step here is one 50 Hz control tick (four 5ms substeps for MuJoCo).

Second, fewer steps needed: even when both methods learn inside the same pocket world, the analytic-math method (backprop through the differentiable model) needs about 64× fewer imagined steps than the guessing method (PPO on the world model, PPO-WM). The paper is careful to separate this: the win is not just “a faster simulator,” it's better gradients.

Together, that is why 1 H100 for 3 hours can beat 128 L40s for 168 hours (~21,000 GPU-hours). The checkpoint hits a normalized return above ~0.90 on the same 129,514-motion benchmark and fails on only 25 motions versus 179 for SONIC (239 for SONIC v1.1). After 9.8 hours on 2 GPUs it fails on zero. The authors note the accounting is conservative: SuperMimic used 2.3× less motion data (264 vs 611 hours) and the H100 is at most 5.5× faster in peak tensor speed than an L40 — nowhere near enough to explain a >1000× GPU-hour gap (about 7200× actual).

007Lesson 7 · Sim to real~8 min

From make-believe to metal: making it walk for real

So far your pocket world has lived only on a computer. To make a real robot move, you have to close a sneaky loophole: if the brain sees exactly how slippery the floor or how heavy the torso is, it can cheat.

In SuperMimic the simulator randomly changes three hidden settings every episode and logs them as ξ: floor friction μ between 0.3 and 1.2, a tiny joint calibration offset between -0.01 and +0.01 rad, and a torso center-of-mass shift up to ±0.05 m. The world model does get to see ξ during imagination:

$$\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t+1}=f_{\omega}(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,\mathbf{\xi})$$

That lets it predict the next state cleanly, because it knows why this floor is slipperier. The policy the robot actually runs never sees ξ. On the real Unitree G1 it only gets its own noisy feelings: joint angles (±0.01 rad noise), joint speeds (±0.5 rad/s), base spin from the IMU (±0.2 rad/s), which way is down (±0.05), and what it last did — the last 10 frames of ot plus its goal gt.

Key idea: train with a cheat sheet, deploy without it. Because the world model explains away the variation with ξ, gradients teach one policy to work for all ξ values. Blind at run time, it stays robust.

Actions are also kept gentle on purpose: the policy outputs a normalized setpoint at turned into a low-gain PD target qsp= α·at+q0. With low gains that behaves more like a soft torque than a stiff position, so it survives bumps.

The paper tested one single tracker — no per-trick tuning — on hardware. It did four very different things: a skipping jump (0.2 s snapshots), a side roll with torso and limbs on the floor (0.3 s), a 90° flip (0.2 s), and an in-place 360° flip. Those cover walking, full-body contact, and acrobatics.

SuperMimic still has limits it is honest about: its world model has only ever seen simulator transitions (no real-robot data yet), and it has only trained on flat ground — no stairs, terrain, or objects. That is future work.

008Lesson 8 · What changed~8 min

The payoff: 1000× cheaper and a robot that flips

So what did SuperMimic actually save — and when should you stop copying dances and start rewarding the job itself?

First, the bill. The previous best general tracker, SONIC, needed about 128 GPUs running for 168 hours. GPUs times hours is GPU-hours, so that's roughly 21,000 GPU-hours and a week of wall-clock time. SuperMimic beat SONIC's checkpoint score of about 0.90 normalized return — that's total tracking reward divided by its maximum, so 1.0 is perfect and falling early is near 0 — in just 3 hours on 1 H100. That's 3 GPU-hours. Divide 21,000 by 3 and you get about 7,200 times less computer, reported conservatively as more than 1,000× fewer GPU-hours and more than 50× faster wall-clock (168 hours down to 3).

The same benchmark tells the failure story. On the filtered BONES-SEED set of 129,514 motions (264.56 hours of movement), SONIC's released checkpoint failed on 179 motions — it even failed every flip, every side roll, and one whole dance — and its v1.1 checkpoint failed on 239. After 3 hours SuperMimic failed on only 25 out of 129,514, and after 9.8 hours on 2 GPUs it failed on none. In a clean side-by-side on the small LAFAN1 library (40 clips, 2.45 hours), SuperMimic on 1 GPU hit a return of 0.82 in 1.2 hours, while PPO trained in the real simulator for the full 18-hour budget only reached 0.60 on 1 GPU, 0.70 on 4 GPUs, and 0.72 on 8 GPUs — barely better from 4 to 8 GPUs.

Second, what about a new way to steer the robot, like a joystick for speed? SuperMimic can do that without retraining physics. It keeps one shared decoder — the part that turns an idea into joint commands — and gives each goal its own encoder. An encoder turns its goal into a tiny 32-number code $z$ (squashed with $\tanh$), and the shared decoder turns $z$ plus the last 10 proprioceptive frames into an action $a_t$. Training picks a goal type at random and maximizes $\hat{J}_i(\theta)$ for that type through the same world model.

For velocity, the command is $g_t=(v_x, v_y, \omega_z)$ — desired forward/side speed in the robot's heading frame and turn rate. There are two ways to teach its encoder. Distillation keeps using the motion-tracking reward and simply reads the command from a reference dance — "do what the dancer did when they happened to move at this speed." RL instead rewards the job directly:

$$r_{\text{vel}} \approx 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|v_{xy}-v^{\text{cmd}}_{xy}\|^2}{0.5^2}\right) + 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\omega_z-\omega_z^{\text{cmd}})^2}{0.5}\right) + \text{small regularizers}$$

So $r_{\text{vel}}$ is near 4 when velocity matches the command and drops quickly as error grows, plus tiny penalties for jerky actions or leaning.

The paper's early test ran 672 step commands — a 15 by 15 grid on each of three planes ($v_x$ vs $v_y$, $v_x$ vs $\omega_z$, $v_y$ vs $\omega_z$, each for 5 seconds, 4 trials), skipping standing still. Measuring relative steady-state error as the average speed error in the last 2 seconds divided by command size, the RL encoder won 650 out of 672 times with a median error of 0.16 vs 0.49 for distillation. Distillation only won 15 times, and it completely fell over in all 4 trials on 7 fast turns of at least 2.8 rad/s — exactly the corners where the dance library has almost no examples. Copying dances works where dances exist; rewarding the actual task generalizes where they don't.

Takeaway: compute is no longer the bottleneck. One model that knows shared physics can be trained on one GPU, reach zero failures, and then teach new skills from their own rewards. The lab maps this.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

motion-capture (mocap) dataset

A file of human movement (like walking, dancing, flipping) recorded with cameras or suits. Each clip is a target for the robot to copy.

proprioceptive observation

The live numbers a robot feels about itself: joint angles, joint speeds, which way is down, and what it just did.

simulator

The computer copy of physics that decides what happens if the robot pushes with a certain force. MuJoCo Warp is the GPU version used here.

world model

A small neural network that learns to predict the simulator: given current state + action + world settings, it guesses next state, reward, and if the robot fell.

policy

A rule the robot follows: look at what you feel (observation) plus what you want (goal like a reference pose) → choose joint setpoints (action).

reward / normalized return

A number that tells the robot how well it tracked: close bodies + right orientation + right velocity = near 1.0, falling early = near 0.

model-free RL (PPO)

PPO is the usual trial-and-error learner: try random noisy actions, keep what got higher reward. Its grade is a noisy sample.

backpropagation-through-time (BPTT)

A calculus chain rule trick: roll the world model forward H steps, then walk gradients backwards through every step to see exactly how each action helped.

critic bootstrap (V_ψ)

A guess of future reward from a state beyond the imagined H steps. Lets the model be accurate for only 32 steps instead of forever.
