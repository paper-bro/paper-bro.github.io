001The bottleneck~5 min

Why streaming AI video gets stuck in traffic.

Streaming AI video live is like laying train tracks right in front of a moving locomotive. If a screen plays video at 16 frames per second (FPS), the generator must invent at least 16 fresh frames every single second or playback freezes. Instead of creating a whole movie at once—or working one tiny frame at a time—streaming models generate video in short bundles called chunks. In this paper's setup on Wan2.1-T2V, each chunk holds 3 compressed latent frames, which a partner decoder network called a VAE (Variational Autoencoder) expands into 12 full-color RGB frames at $832 \times 480$ resolution—0.75 seconds of 16 FPS video.

Every chunk $i$ begins as pure random static, written $Z_{i}^{(0)}$, and goes through $S$ cleanup updates called denoising steps across decreasing noise levels $\nu_{0} > \cdots > \nu_{S} = 0$ until it becomes a clean latent chunk $B_{i} = Z_{i}^{(S)}$. At step $s$, the cleanup rule is:

$$Z_{i}^{(s+1)} = \Psi_{\nu_{s+1}}\!\left(G_{\theta}(Z_{i}^{(s)}, \nu_{s} \mid \mathcal{C}_{i,s}), \epsilon_{i,s}\right), \qquad 0 \leq s < S$$

Inside this formula, $G_{\theta}$ is the Diffusion Transformer (DiT), the main neural network with learned weights $\theta$ that inspects the noisy chunk $Z_{i}^{(s)}$ and current noise level $\nu_{s}$ to predict a clean version. So characters, clothes, and lighting match earlier scenes, $G_{\theta}$ also reads $\mathcal{C}_{i,s}$, a compact key/value (K/V) memory saved from previous chunks. Next, the stepping function $\Psi_{\nu_{s+1}}$ mixes in fresh random noise $\epsilon_{i,s}$ at the quieter noise level $\nu_{s+1}$ so the next step can sharpen finer details—until the final step, where $\Psi_{0}(x, \epsilon) = x$ leaves $B_{i}$ completely clean.

Here is where the traffic jam appears. Any K/V vectors computed during denoising come from noisy inputs. Under the paper's clean-history convention, once $B_{i}$ is clean, the model runs one extra full DiT pass—called store—on clean $B_{i}$ solely to save fresh, clean K/V memories for future chunks while discarding its output prediction. On one GPU, running these passes back-to-back requires $N = S + 1$ forward rounds for every single chunk, a bottleneck called NF1C ($N$ Forward Rounds, 1 Chunk Output). Even one-step denoising ($S = 1$) still needs $1 + 1 = 2$ full forward passes per chunk!

From traffic jam to assembly line (1F1C): On a single H200 GPU, a 4-step baseline ($S = 4$ plus store and VAE decoding) reaches just 19.06 end-to-end FPS at 1.3B parameters—barely clearing 16 FPS—and crawls at 5.51 FPS on a 14B model. Wave Forcing targets 1F1C: by staggering the $S$ denoising stages and 1 store stage across $S + 1$ full-model GPU replicas, it emits one finished chunk every concurrent round after pipeline fill—completing $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ chunks in $N_{\mathrm{chunk}} + S$ rounds instead of $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}(S + 1)$ without skipping a single step.

002Wavefront scheduling~6 min

Denoising video chunks along a moving diagonal.

To hit 1F1C—one finished chunk every concurrent round—Wave Forcing gives each step of the assembly line its own GPU holding a full copy of the Diffusion Transformer. With $S$ denoising steps, $S$ GPUs run denoising stages $s = 0, 1, \dots, S - 1$ while one more GPU runs the clean-history Store pass at $s = S$. Instead of waiting for chunk $0$ to finish before starting chunk $1$, a fresh noisy chunk enters stage $0$ on every forward round $k$.

Picture chunks as columns ($i = 0, 1, \dots$) and stages as rows ($s = 0, \dots, S$). At round $k$, stage $s$ updates chunk $i$ according to one simple coordinate rule:

$$k = i + s, \qquad i = k - s$$

Once the pipeline is full, round $k$ advances a moving anti-diagonal—a wavefront $\mathcal{W}_k = \{Z_k^{(0)}, Z_{k-1}^{(1)}, \dots, Z_{k-S+1}^{(S-1)}\} \cup \{B_{k-S}\}$ of width $S + 1$. While GPU $0$ starts cleaning brand-new noise $Z_k^{(0)}$, GPU $1$ refines older chunk $Z_{k-1}^{(1)}$, and the Store GPU refreshes clean K/V memory from finished chunk $B_{k-S}$.

Across $N_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ requested chunks, the stream takes $S$ rounds to fill the GPUs, runs at full width during steady state, and takes $S$ final rounds to drain—totaling $N_{\mathrm{chunk}} + S$ rounds. The share of active GPU slots over the whole run is the structural slot occupancy:

$$\eta_{\mathrm{slot}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{chunk}}}{N_{\mathrm{chunk}} + S}$$

Short clips spend a noticeable slice of rounds filling and draining, whereas long streams keep almost every GPU busy: on the paper's 100-second videos ($399$ latent frames, or $N_{\mathrm{chunk}} = 133$ chunks) with $S = 4$, slot occupancy reaches $133 / 137 = 97.1\%$.

Cutting backward links with causal attention

How can a newer chunk denoise before the chunk ahead of it is completely clean? An earlier joint-denoising method called Rolling Forcing (RF) used bidirectional attention across active chunks ("reverse refinement"), letting every active chunk read every other active chunk. That creates a two-way tangle: earlier, cleaner chunks also depend on later, noisier chunks.

Wave Forcing removes those reverse links with chunk-wise causal attention. Tokens still attend freely inside their own chunk, while across active denoising stages $\mathcal{A}_k$, query stage $s$ sees K/V from stage $j$ only when:

$$M_{\mathrm{WF}}(s, j) = \mathbf{1}[j \geq s]$$

Here $\mathbf{1}[j \geq s]$ equals $1$ (visible) when $j \geq s$ and $0$ (masked) when $j < s$. Because $i = k - s$, larger stage numbers $j > s$ belong to earlier, cleaner chunks ($k - j < k - s$). Newer, noisier chunks read guidance from cleaner predecessors, never the reverse.

Four ordered pieces of bounded K/V memory

At each Transformer layer $\ell$, stage $s$ builds its K/V context $\mathcal{C}_{k,s}^{\mathrm{WF},\ell}$ by concatenating ($\oplus$) four segments in exact order:

$$\mathcal{C}_{k,s}^{\mathrm{WF},\ell} = A_k^\ell \oplus H_k^\ell \oplus U_k^\ell \oplus \Bigl[\bigoplus_{\substack{j \in \mathcal{A}_k \\ j \geq s}} (K_{k,j}^\ell, V_{k,j}^\ell)\Bigr]$$
⚓

1. Anchor & Clean History ($A_k^\ell \oplus H_k^\ell$)

Chunk $0$'s clean K/V stays pinned forever as anchor $A_k^\ell$ to preserve the opening scene and subject, followed by a fixed-size sliding window $H_k^\ell$ of recent clean chunks.

⚡

2. Live Store & Cleaner Stages ($U_k^\ell \oplus j \geq s$)

Next comes $U_k^\ell$ from the Store GPU (which moves into history after the round), then live denoising K/V starting with self ($j = s$) and cleaner stages ($j > s$).

In a filled round, if each chunk has $N_C$ tokens and the clean prefix ($A_k^\ell \oplus H_k^\ell \oplus U_k^\ell$) holds $N_{\mathrm{prefix}}$ tokens, stage $s$ compares its $N_C$ queries against:

$$N_s = N_{\mathrm{prefix}} + (S - s) N_C$$

Cleaner GPUs (larger $s$) therefore read fewer keys, and because $H_k^\ell$ has a fixed cap, total K/V buffer memory stays bounded no matter how long the video runs.

003Staged model training~6 min

Three training stages tame motion and camera drift.

In Lesson 2, Wave Forcing deleted the backward attention arrows from Rolling Forcing (RF) so cleaner GPUs never wait on noisier ones. What happens if you simply apply that one-way causal mask to a pretrained RF model and press play? Motion fizzles out: on short clips, VBench Dynamic Degree (a 0 to 100 score of how much movement occurs) falls from 51.39 to 37.50, and Quality dips from 83.77 to 83.35. Worse, streaming a 100-second video creates a snowball effect. Every finished chunk enters the Store K/V cache to guide later chunks. If a generator only ever practiced on spotless training clips, its own tiny errors pollute that memory, gradually blurring textures or nudging the camera off course.

To solve both problems, the authors adapt the 1.3-billion-parameter Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B student using a frozen, high-quality 50-step Wan2.1-T2V-14B model as its teacher. Their main tool is Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD), a training method that pushes a fast 4- or 5-step student to generate video chunks whose realism matches the slow 50-step teacher's. Training unfolds in three stages (the 4-step student starts from Self Forcing's initialization and runs all three; the 5-step student reuses released RF weights and skips Stage 1):

  1. Stage 1 — Mixed-noise initialization: Keeping bidirectional attention across active chunks at different noise levels, each step flips a fair coin (50% / 50%) between an RF rollout and a sequential Self Forcing (SF) rollout under DMD. Mixing in SF stabilizes early motion.
  2. Stage 2 — Off-policy teacher guidance: Next, the student practices on clean video chunks $Y_{\mathrm{win}}$ pre-generated by the 14B teacher (“off-policy” means learning from fixed teacher recordings rather than the student's own live guesses), re-noised at staggered timesteps after clean teacher K/V history.
  3. Stage 3 — On-policy causal adaptation: Finally, the student generates its own multi-chunk rollouts and refreshes its own Store K/V history (“on-policy” learning), adapting directly to self-generated history.

In Stage 2, given text prompt $\mathbf{c}$, the student predicts clean chunks $\widehat{Y}_{\theta}$ and minimizes two losses together:

$$\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{DMD}}(\widehat{Y}_{\theta};\mathbf{c})+\lambda_{\mathrm{p}}\operatorname{LPIPS}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{perc}}(\widehat{Y}_{\theta}),\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{perc}}(Y_{\mathrm{win}})\right)\right],\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25$$

Here $\mathbb{E}$ averages over training examples, $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{DMD}}$ scores realism against the teacher, $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{perc}}$ decodes downsampled latents into sampled RGB frames, and $\operatorname{LPIPS}$ compares visual features frame-by-frame (weighted by $\lambda_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25$) so subjects and motion match $Y_{\mathrm{win}}$. Stage 3 then averages equally across three self-generated rollout branches $b$—with individual losses $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RF}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WF}}$, and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SF}}$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{3}=\mathbb{E}_{b\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{\mathrm{RF},\mathrm{WF},\mathrm{SF}\}}[\mathcal{L}_{b}]=\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RF}}+\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WF}}+\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SF}}$$

Only WF runs at deployment (using Lesson 2's causal wavefront context), while RF and SF act as training stabilizers. The paper goes further in Appendix A, detailing an auxiliary fake-score network and exact update schedules for DMD.

Why a higher motion score can mean failure: On 100-second 4-step tests, the full recipe balances VBench-Long Quality (80.89), Dynamic Degree (74.97), Subject Preservation (91.88 out of 100), and Camera Drift (0.36, where 0 is rock-steady). Skipping Stage 2 freezes action (Dynamic plunges to 15.73; Subject falls to 76.53). Removing SF hurts both Quality (75.35) and Dynamic (23.66), while removing WF leaves stiff movement (Dynamic 48.12). Surprisingly, removing RF pushes Dynamic up to 88.82—because framing breaks down! Subject Preservation drops to 77.48 and Camera Drift jumps 17-fold to 6.24, proving that a sliding camera inflates raw motion scores while losing the main character.

004Wave Parallelism~7 min

Cleaner GPUs run ahead and hide network waits.

Lining up GPUs along a causal wavefront lets every stage run concurrently, yet a subtle traffic jam hides inside each forward round. At every Transformer layer $\ell$, a noisier GPU cannot run attention until it receives fresh layer-$\ell$ Key/Value (K/V) memories from Store and every cleaner GPU ahead of it. A naive Sync baseline inserts an AllGather barrier before attention at every single layer—locking all GPUs to the slowest arrival (91.96 ms median steady-state tick at 1.3B BF16, and 464.27 ms at 14B).

Wave Runtime (WaveRT) breaks that lockstep by splitting layer $\ell$ on rank $s$ into two halves: $P_{s,\ell}$ computes local Q/K/V projections and rotary position embeddings (RoPE), while $C_{s,\ell}$ runs attention and the remaining block. Step $P_{s,\ell}$ depends only on rank $s$'s local state, whereas $C_{s,\ell}$ must wait for $P_{j,\ell}$ from Store and every cleaner active rank $j > s$:

$$P_{s,\ell} \rightarrow C_{s,\ell} \rightarrow P_{s,\ell+1}, \qquad P_{j,\ell} \rightarrow C_{s,\ell} \quad (j > s)$$

Why wouldn't $C_{s,\ell}$ still stall at every layer? Recall from Lesson 2 how many key tokens $N_s$ rank $s$ reads in a filled $S$-step round, given $N_C$ tokens per chunk and $N_{\mathrm{prefix}}$ anchor, history, and store tokens:

$$N_s = N_{\mathrm{prefix}} + (S - s)N_C$$

Cleaner ranks have a larger stage index $s$, so they attend to fewer keys. Their attention layers finish faster, letting them advance to layer $\ell + 1$ earlier and build a natural producer lead—on the 14B model, producer ranks $r_1, r_2, r_3$ and Store complete a forward round in 370.50–379.37 ms versus critical consumer $r_0$'s 414.23 ms.

WaveRT turns that head start into hidden communication. For any required K/V edge $e$, let $t_e^{\mathrm{ready}}$ be when the producer finishes $P_{j,\ell}$, $t_e^{\mathrm{need}}$ be when the consumer locally reaches attention, and $\tau_e$ be the delivery cost (queueing, transfer, and signaling). The producer's head-start slack $\Delta_e$ and exposed wait $w_e$ are:

$$\Delta_e = t_e^{\mathrm{need}} - t_e^{\mathrm{ready}}, \qquad w_e = \max(0, \tau_e - \Delta_e)$$

A consumer stalls for the worst wait $\max_e w_e$ across all required producers. Layer 1 starts cold with $\Delta_e = 0$, leaving an unavoidable initial wait, but in later layers cleaner GPUs pull ahead. Once $\tau_e \le \Delta_e$ for every required producer, $w_e = 0$ and chip-to-chip transfer disappears behind local math.

WaveRT refines delivery step by step: Overlap publishes K/V right after $P_{j,\ell}$; One-sided uses CUDA IPC peer-to-peer copies with device readiness flags; Staggered prefetches flag checks; and Paged writes incoming K/V straight into pre-ordered attention slots (KPage/VPage). Avoiding receiver-side tensor concatenation cuts context assembly from 19.02 ms to 5.91 ms, lowering 4-step BF16 tick p50 from 91.96 to 78.88 ms (-14.2%) at 1.3B and from 464.27 to 413.80 ms (-10.9%) at 14B.

Same math, zero compromise (Proposition 1): Holding model weights, random noise draws, positions, and local operators fixed, readiness flags guarantee that Paged asynchronous attention consumes the exact same K/V values in the exact same order as Sync—verified bit-identical on the 1.3B model.

Watch how Layer 1 always pays an initial red wait, while cleaner GPUs pull left in later layers until $\Delta_e$ swallows $\tau_e$ and the wait vanishes.

005Streaming pixel decode~6 min

Balancing the decoder so pixels keep up with latents.

Finishing the Store pass still leaves video in compressed latent form—not pixels a screen can display. To unpack each 3-frame latent chunk into 12 crisp $832 \times 480$ RGB frames, WaveRT feeds clean latents into a streaming VAE decoder. Running that decoder on a single chip after every round would stall playback, so WaveRT divides its 8-GPU H200 node across denoising + Store + VAE: 4 + 1 + 3 GPUs for 4-step Wave Forcing, and 5 + 1 + 2 GPUs for 5-step Wave Forcing.

Dedicated VAE GPUs run asynchronously alongside the DiT wavefront, overlapping pixel decoding with future denoising rounds. Still, an assembly line only emits finished video as fast as its slowest worker. In steady state, the time between decoded chunks $T_{\mathrm{emit}}^{\mathrm{E2E}}$ follows:

$$T_{\mathrm{emit}}^{\mathrm{E2E}} \approx \max\!\left(T_{\mathrm{round}}^{\mathrm{WP}},\ \max_{p} T_{\mathrm{VAE},p}^{\mathrm{chunk}}\right) + T_{\mathrm{delivery}}^{\mathrm{exposed}}$$

Here $T_{\mathrm{round}}^{\mathrm{WP}}$ is one Wave Parallelism DiT round, $T_{\mathrm{VAE},p}^{\mathrm{chunk}}$ is the time VAE GPU stage $p$ spends decoding one chunk, and $T_{\mathrm{delivery}}^{\mathrm{exposed}}$ is any unhidden transfer overhead. Whichever stage inside $\max(\cdot)$ takes longer caps your end-to-end (E2E) frames per second.

How should we slice the VAE's $M_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ sequential decoder blocks across $P_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ GPUs while keeping each stream's causal-convolution caches intact? Splitting naively by math operation counts (FLOPs) fails badly: late upsampling layers push huge high-resolution pixel tensors through memory without many multiplications, leaving an overloaded stage at 115.76 ms per chunk. Instead, WaveRT profiles each block's real streaming time $\tau_{m}^{\mathrm{VAE}}$ and uses dynamic programming to balance 3 VAE stages to 73.15 ms, 60.91 ms, and 80.89 ms—cutting the worst stage from 115.76 ms down to 80.89 ms.

How dynamic programming picks the cuts: WaveRT chooses contiguous boundaries $0 = b_{0} < \cdots < b_{P_{\mathrm{VAE}}} = M_{\mathrm{VAE}}$ solving $$\min_{0=b_{0}<\cdots<b_{P_{\mathrm{VAE}}}=M_{\mathrm{VAE}}} \max_{1\leq p\leq P_{\mathrm{VAE}}} \sum_{m=b_{p-1}+1}^{b_{p}} \tau_{m}^{\mathrm{VAE}}$$ In plain words: test every valid way to group consecutive decoder blocks across the VAE GPUs, sum each GPU's real block latencies, and choose the split whose slowest GPU finishes earliest.

That ~81 ms decoder ceiling explains a puzzle in the paper's 4-step runtime tests (measured with dummy DiT weights and a real VAE across standard BF16, faster SageAttention, and 8-bit Sage+W8A8 linear layers). On the compact 1.3B model, turning on Sage+W8A8 trims the steady DiT tick from 78.88 ms to 76.05 ms (157.79 steady FPS). Yet complete-run E2E speed does not rise: BF16 hits 134.81 FPS (versus 147.58 DiT FPS, a $7.07\times$ speedup over the 19.06-FPS single-GPU baseline), Sage hits 134.79 FPS, and Sage+W8A8 reaches 132.89 FPS. Because the 1.3B DiT already finishes under 79 ms, the VAE and output path—plus pipeline fill and drain—cap E2E throughput (just as 5-step 1.3B with 2 VAE GPUs hits 104.82 DiT FPS versus 96.46 E2E FPS).

On the giant 14B model, the bottleneck flips. A 4-step BF16 DiT tick takes 413.80 ms—over five times slower than the 80.89 ms VAE—giving 28.64 E2E FPS. Because the DiT is now the clear limiter, Sage+W8A8 slashes the tick to 354.45 ms and jumps E2E speed by 17.0% to 33.50 FPS ($6.08\times$ the 5.51-FPS single-GPU baseline).

006Parallelism showdown~6 min

Splitting stages beats splitting single layers.

Up to now, we have put each GPU in charge of a different denoising stage along the moving wavefront. That is not how multi-GPU video generators usually divide their work. Traditional Context Parallelism (CP) puts every GPU to work on a single forward pass together—slicing up the video tokens or attention heads inside each Transformer layer, trading pieces across the network, and moving to the next denoising step only after every GPU finishes.

Imagine six chefs making a five-step recipe. Here is how the three main strategies split the job:

🔄

Ulysses CP

Splits tokens across GPUs, then runs two All-to-All group swaps in every layer to switch into split-attention-heads and back. Attention heads must divide evenly by the GPU count.

💍

Ring Attention

Gives each GPU a slice of tokens and passes K/V memory blocks around a multi-round GPU ring at every layer, hoping local tile math takes long enough to hide each hop.

🌊

Wave Parallelism

Gives each GPU a full model replica owning one staggered chunk-stage (or Store) on the causal wavefront, replacing two-way layer barriers with overlapped one-way K/V writes.

To test these strategies head-to-head without decoder bottlenecks, the authors turn off the VAE and measure DiT throughput across six model scales from 1.3B to 14B parameters using dummy weights and matched budgets: 5 steps on 6 GPUs for 1.3B–9B, and 4 steps on 5 GPUs for 14B (whose 40 attention heads divide evenly by 5, not 6). Over the active window, CP runs Rolling Forcing's bidirectional attention while Wave Parallelism (WP) uses Wave Forcing's causal mask, measuring the combined effect of stage scheduling and one-way visibility.

Under standard BF16 math, WP beats the faster CP baseline at five of six scales ($0.985\times$ to $1.862\times$). At 1.3B, WP reaches 150.09 FPS versus 80.60 FPS for Ulysses and 41.19 FPS for Ring; at 9B it nearly ties Ulysses (45.70 vs. 46.41 FPS, trailing by 1.53%); and at 14B it leads 28.71 to 26.72 FPS.

Now speed up local math with Sage+W8A8 (SageAttention plus 8-bit linear layers). WP wins at all six scales, delivering $1.025\times$ to $2.530\times$ the throughput of the faster CP baseline. Look at why the gap explodes at 1.3B: faster 8-bit kernels shrink local compute time so much that Ulysses gets choked by its two per-layer All-to-All swaps—actually dropping from 80.60 FPS in BF16 down to 61.17 FPS in Sage+W8A8—while WP climbs to 154.75 FPS.

The trade-offs of Wave Parallelism: Every stage GPU must hold a full copy of the model weights, and a single wavefront pipeline is capped at $S + 1$ DiT GPUs for $S$ denoising steps. On giant 9B–14B models, huge matrix multiplications dominate each layer, giving Ulysses more compute time to amortize communication and narrowing WP's Sage+W8A8 lead to $1.025\times$–$1.085\times$ (33.60 vs. 30.98 FPS at 14B).

007Measuring long video~5 min

High motion scores can hide a wandering camera.

Generating 134.8 frames per second only matters if a 100-second video is actually worth watching. Surprisingly, standard video benchmarks like VBench and VBench-Long can hand top trophies to broken videos. When a metric measures "motion" by checking how much pixels shift from frame to frame, a camera that slowly wanders off into an empty wall looks wonderfully active!

That exact trap shows up in the paper's 100-second evaluations on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B. Causal Forcing achieves the highest VBench-Long Quality (82.15) and a near-perfect Dynamic Degree (96.61 out of 100). Yet when you watch the clip, the camera drifts steadily sideways: the main character vanishes off-screen at 30 seconds and 75 seconds, leaving only pillows and a headboard. At the opposite extreme, Rolling Forcing keeps the camera steady, but its character practically freezes into a statue, dropping VBench-Long Dynamic Degree to 36.63.

To catch both failure modes, the authors test a fixed subset of 100 subject-focused MovieGen prompts with two targeted 0-to-100 diagnostics built on SAM3 object segmentation and DINO visual identity features:

🎯

Subject Preservation (higher is better)

Tracks the prompt's main character across the video. Clear detections score weight $1$, frames where the character disappears score $0$, and boxes touching the outer $1\%$ border are weighted down as the visible mask shrinks below $18\%$ of its initial size.

🎥

Camera Drift (lower is better)

Flags persistent, unwanted sliding toward any frame edge $e$ by combining border proximity with both character and background velocities.

How do you penalize a wandering camera without punishing a cool action tracking shot—say, a camera panning alongside a sprinter? For each frame border $e$, the paper defines an instantaneous drift risk:

$$\text{Risk} = \max_{e} \bigl(E_{e} \cdot F_{e} \cdot B_{e}\bigr), \qquad E_{e} = \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{g_{e}}{0.1}\right)$$

Here $g_{e}$ is how close the subject's mask is to edge $e$ (as a fraction of the screen diagonal), so edge proximity $E_{e}$ stays zero until the subject enters the outer $10\%$ border zone. $F_{e}$ measures persistent outward motion of the foreground subject toward edge $e$ over the past 5 seconds, and $B_{e}$ measures persistent outward motion of background points in that same direction. Because all three terms multiply together, a healthy tracking shot that keeps a runner centered ($E_{e} = 0$, $F_{e} = 0$) gets zero drift penalty even while the background rushes past.

The sweet spot on Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B: 5-step Wave Forcing leads standard VBench Total (81.64 vs. Rolling Forcing's 81.15) and matches VBench-Long Quality (81.26 vs. 81.18), while lifting Dynamic Degree from 51.39 to 94.44 on short clips and from 36.63 to 91.45 on 100-second videos. At the same time, it reaches 97.10 Subject Preservation (vs. Rolling Forcing's 92.75 and Causal Forcing's 71.11) and keeps Camera Drift down at 0.37 (vs. Rolling Forcing's 0.17 and Causal Forcing's 11.14)—all at 96.46 end-to-end FPS!

008Results and horizon~5 min

Co-designing math, training, and GPUs unlocks live video.

None of Wave Forcing's breakthroughs could have delivered live, stable long-video streaming alone. Changing the attention mask without retraining drained the video's motion; retraining without hiding GPU network waits or balancing the pixel decoder would have left expensive chips stalled. Speed-of-light streaming comes from co-design—shaping the math, the training recipe, and the multi-GPU runtime around one another:

📐

Causal Wavefront

Chunk-wise causal attention lets newer, noisier chunks read earlier, cleaner neighbors—unlocking 1F1C across $S + 1$ staggered replicas.

🎯

3-Stage Adaptation

Mixed-noise initialization, teacher DMD + LPIPS, and a $\tfrac{1}{3}$ RF/WF/SF self-history mix keep motion lively without losing the subject.

⚡

Wave Runtime

Cleaner ranks run ahead to overlap paged IPC K/V writes, feeding a dynamic-programming-balanced streaming VAE.

Together on eight H200 GPUs at $832 \times 480$ resolution, 4-step Wave Forcing on the 1.3B model reaches 147.58 DiT FPS (147.6 in the abstract) and 134.81 end-to-end (E2E) FPS (134.8 in the abstract)—a $7.07\times$ E2E speedup over the 19.06-FPS single-GPU baseline. The 5-step 1.3B model hits 96.46 E2E FPS ($5.52\times$ over its 17.46-FPS baseline) while scoring 91.45 Dynamic Degree and 97.10 Subject Preservation on 100-second videos. Even at 14B parameters (benchmarked for execution speed with dummy DiT weights and a real VAE), WaveRT with Sage+W8A8 turns an unplayable 5.51-FPS single-GPU crawl into 33.50 E2E FPS—a $6.08\times$ speedup and more than double the 16-FPS live playback rate.

Honest limits at a fixed step budget

The authors are equally clear about what Wave Forcing does not solve yet. First, for a fixed budget of $S$ denoising steps, Wave Parallelism uses $S$ denoising replicas plus 1 Store replica—capping DiT pipeline width at $S + 1$ GPUs unless combined with finer-grained or context parallelism, while WP's speedup over context parallelism narrows on giant models. Second, at 1.3B, the gap between 147.58 DiT FPS and 134.81 E2E FPS shows residual VAE and output-path limits. Third, the three-stage training recipe remains complex, and training a full 14B student exceeded the team's compute budget.

High FPS does not equal instant reaction: An assembly line that finishes a frame chunk every 89 milliseconds (134.81 FPS) still makes each new chunk travel through all $S$ denoising stages, Store, and the partitioned VAE before its pixels appear.

That distinction defines the next frontier: interactive world models where a viewer switches prompts, steers a camera, or presses game controls mid-stream. Turning Wave Forcing into a live interactive engine requires more than steady throughput—it requires deciding which already-in-flight chunks receive a new command, refreshing cached K/V memories (KV recaching) so old history does not fight the new instruction, scheduling multiple concurrent user streams, and building richer evaluators that test instruction timing alongside visual stability.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Latent chunk

A short, compressed block of video frames (3 latent frames in this paper, which expand into 12 pixel frames) that the AI generates together.

Denoising

A step-by-step cleanup process where a neural network turns random static into a clear image or video chunk over S rounds.

DiT

Diffusion Transformer, the main neural network that predicts clean video chunks at each denoising step using attention layers.

VAE

Variational Autoencoder, a partner network whose decoder converts compressed latent chunks into full-resolution RGB video frames.

K/V cache

Compact memory vectors saved at every Transformer layer from earlier chunks so newer chunks can keep characters, lighting, and scenes consistent.

Store pass

An extra forward pass run on a freshly cleaned chunk solely to compute and save clean K/V memories for future chunks.

NF1C vs. 1F1C

NF1C needs N = S + 1 sequential forward rounds to finish one chunk, whereas 1F1C finishes one chunk every single concurrent round once the pipeline is full.

Causal wavefront

A staggered multi-GPU schedule where consecutive video chunks advance through progressively cleaner stages at the same time, and noisier chunks only read from earlier, cleaner chunks.

Anchor chunk

The very first clean chunk of a video, kept permanently in attention memory so long videos remember their starting look and subject.

DMD

Distribution Matching Distillation, a training method that teaches a fast few-step student model to match the realism of a slow 50-step teacher model.

Producer lead

The natural head start cleaner GPUs gain within each round because they read fewer K/V tokens per layer, letting them send K/V data before noisier GPUs reach attention.

Paged K/V delivery

Writing incoming K/V tensors from another GPU directly into pre-assigned slots in the receiver's attention buffer so no extra concatenation step is needed.

Subject Preservation and Camera Drift

Two long-video stability diagnostics that track whether the main character stays visible on screen and whether the camera persistently slides off to one side.
