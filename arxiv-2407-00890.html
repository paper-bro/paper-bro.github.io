001The problem~10 min

Can a ChatGPT Cousin Predict the Economy?

Imagine you have to tell the country what will happen next month to jobs, to prices at the store, to the interest rate on a loan, and to how many new homes get started. Get it right and businesses can plan, families can budget, and policymakers don’t overreact. Get it wrong and a lot of people feel it.

That’s what macroeconomic forecasting tries to do: use the past to guess the near future for the whole economy. Not one number, but dozens at once. The paper you’re about to follow asks a simple, sharp question — can a new kind of AI that was built to write text do this number job better than economists’ tried-and-true tools?

The AI in question isn’t ChatGPT itself. It’s a cousin called a Time Series Foundational Model, or TSFM for short. Like ChatGPT learns from billions of words, a TSFM learns from billions of time points — hourly electricity use, daily web clicks, weekly sales — and then tries to predict a new number series it has never been trained to specialize in.

To make the contest fair, economists have a shared scoreboard: a collection called FRED-MD. Think of it as a giant dashboard of the US economy. It holds 120 monthly series — from PAYEMS (total jobs), to PCEPI (prices people pay), to FEDFUNDS (the central bank’s interest rate), to HOUST (housing starts). The records run from January 1959 to December 2022, updated each month by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis.

They’re not all the same kind of wiggly line. FRED-MD groups them into 8 families: Output & Income, Labor Market, Housing, Consumption & Orders, Money & Credit, Interest & Exchange Rates, Prices, and Stock Market. A good forecasting method should handle all of them, not just the easy ones.

Here’s how the paper plays the game, and it’s clever about realism. You can’t peek at the future. The authors start by letting every model study only January 1960 to December 1984. Then they ask: forecast January 1985 (1 month ahead) up to December 1985 (12 months ahead). Next month, they add January 1985 to the training data and forecast February 1985 onward. They repeat, adding one month at a time, all the way to December 2022. It’s called a recursive pseudo out-of-sample exercise — pretend real time.

The main scorecard in the paper stops at December 2019, before Covid-19 scrambled everything. That gives about 35 years of honest, month-by-month tests, for every series and every horizon from h = 1 to h = 12. Later lessons will peek at the post-Covid years, but first we learn how to read the dashboard and why a 12-step forecast is much tougher than a 1-step one.

002How to score~8 min

How Do We Know Who's Winning?

What counts as a "good" forecast depends on what you're comparing it to. The paper uses a dead-simple baseline: an AR(1). That's short for "autoregressive of order 1" — just predict next month from this month alone, with a straight-line fit learned from past data. If your fancy model can't beat that, it's not useful.

For each model i, each variable j (like jobs, prices, or housing), and each horizon h (1 month ahead, 3 months ahead, up to 12), the paper computes the errors. An error is simply

$$e = \text{actual} - \text{forecast}$$

so ebcmk is the AR(1)'s error and ei is the other model's error, for the same variable and same dates. Square them, add them up over the whole test period, and divide. That's equation (11) from the paper:

$$RMSFE_{ijh} = \sqrt{ \frac{\sum e_{i,j,\tau+h}^2}{\sum e_{bcmk,j,\tau+h}^2} }$$

RMSFE means Root Mean Squared Forecast Error. The ratio on top is yours, the bottom is the AR's. Below 1 means you win — your errors were smaller. Above 1 means the simple guess beat you. Exactly 1 is a tie. Because some series (like stock prices) are always noisier than others, this relative score lets you compare across all 120 series fairly.

Reading a boxplot in one glance: the paper draws one box per model containing all 120 ratios. The line inside the box is the median (the middle value — half the series did better, half worse). If most of the box sits below the red line at 1, that model usually beats the AR. The whiskers show the smallest and largest non-outlier values. Extreme dots are hidden so the plot stays readable, but the paper always reports them in the table as Max and Std.

That last detail matters a lot. A median of 0.96 sounds good — half your forecasts beat the benchmark. But if your Max is 7.27 (as it is for one TSFM at h=1), it means on one variable you were more than seven times worse than the simple guess, because squaring blows up one big miss. The opposite is also true: econometric models like the BVAR have a median of 0.983 but a Max of only 1.16 — no disasters.

So the paper always shows four numbers together: median, standard deviation (how spread out the 120 ratios are), minimum (your best series), and maximum (your worst). Stability and the worst case tell you as much as the average.

Try it: in the lab below, drag your model's forecasts and watch the squared errors and the RMSFE ratio update live. See how hard it is to keep the ratio below 1 once you add one big miss — and why the paper watches the max as closely as the median.

003The old guard~12 min

How Economists Already Forecast

Economists don't just draw a line through yesterday's dots. Their two workhorses learn from many series at once, but always with guardrails so they don't hallucinate.

Start with the BVAR. BVAR just means Bayesian Vector Auto-Regression. Collect N variables together in a vector yt — say, jobs, prices, rates — at month t. A VAR with p lags says today is a weighted mix of the last p months plus noise:

$$ y_t = \Phi_c + \Phi_1 y_{t-1} + \Phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \Phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0,\Sigma) $$

We read it piece by piece. Φc is the intercept, like a starting level. Each Φl is an N × N table of weights telling how last month's l (one month ago, two months ago...) pushes today's variables. εt is the surprise that month, with spread Σ. In short matrix writing, with Y = XΦ + E, every row of Y is one month, every column is one variable.

With 120 variables and p=12, each equation has over 1,400 weights to estimate — far too many for ordinary least squares. So the BVAR adds a Minnesota prior, a polite assumption that says "don't be extreme unless data really insists."

Think of the prior as an elastic band pulling wild estimates back toward something sensible. A "Normal-Inverse-Wishart" prior just means we assume coefficients are bell-shaped given the noise level, and the noise itself has an inverse-Wishart shape — two compatible bells that keep the maths tidy.

Concretely, the prior expects the weight on a variable's own first lag to be near 1 if the series wanders like a random walk, or near 0 if it snaps back quickly, and it expects everything else to be near 0. Its tightness shrinks with the lag: variance is λ12 / l2 × σi2/̂σj2 — bigger l means more shrinkage by 1/l2. λ1 is the master knob: λ1 → 0 locks to the prior, λ1 → ∞ lets OLS run wild. Two extra "dummy" priors — sum-of-coefficients and single-unit-root — quietly discourage crazy deterministic trends. The paper doesn't guess λ1; it picks the value that makes the observed data most likely (maximizing the marginal likelihood in equation B.4).

The second workhorse, the factor model, does the opposite compression: it distills 120 noisy series into a few shared drivers. First it runs PCA plus an EM trick to fill gaps and extracts the first common factor f̂1t — think of it as the economy's shared pulse that month.

$$ y_{i,t} = \alpha_h + \beta_h(L)\hat f_{1,t-h} + \gamma_h(L) y_{i,t-h} + \varepsilon_{i,t} $$

Here yi,t is one series at horizon h, βh(L) and γh(L) are lag polynomials (just weighted sums of past f̂ and past yi), and lags are chosen by BIC, a rule that rewards fit but penalizes complexity. Forecasts just plug today's factor and today's own lags into the estimated line:

$$ \hat y_{i,t+h} = \hat\alpha_h + \hat\beta_h(L)\hat f_{1t} + \hat\gamma_h(L) y_{it} $$

Why are these old tools so strong? They bake in decades of economic sense, they're interpretable, and in the paper they make few outright crazy forecasts — their error distributions are left-skewed and tight. The lab lets you feel that: loosen the prior and a VAR with many lags quickly hallucinates; tighten it and the forecast steadies.

Try it below: slide λ1 from 0 to 5 and p from 1 to 12. Watch the red OLS dots jump and the green BVAR dots settle, and see the forecast path go from jagged to smooth. Notice how higher lags are shrunk hardest.

004The challengers~12 min

The New Challengers: Time Series AIs

Economists write equations. Text AIs write sentences. How do you turn a wiggly economic line into something a text AI can read?

The answer in this paper is: you teach a Time Series Foundational Model — TSFM — to treat numbers like words. Like ChatGPT learns by reading billions of words, a TSFM learns by looking at billions of time points from energy use, web traffic, weather, sales, and yes, economics. Then you freeze its brain and ask it to forecast a completely new series it never studied. The authors call that zero-shot forecasting.

$$\hat{y}_{T+1:T+H\,|\,T}=f_{\hat{\theta}}(y_{1:T})=f(y_{1:T}\,|\,X_{1:T},\hat{\theta})$$

In plain words: the forecast for the next $H$ months ($\hat{y}_{T+1:T+H}$) is a function $f$ of the past you show it ($y_{1:T}$). The function depends on huge past training data $X_{1:T}$ and billions of learned weights $\hat{\theta}$ — but you don't retrain it. You just run it.

Three stages, every time (Fig.1 in the paper):
1. Tokenization — turn numbers into tokens.
2. Pretraining — learn a Transformer on tokens.
3. Zero-shot — apply frozen model to your new series.

Tokenization is the trick. Text already has words. Time series doesn't, so the paper's TSFMs invent them:

  • Scaling: $\tilde{x}_t=(x_t-M)/S$ — subtract a centre $M$ and divide by a spread $S$ (like median and inter-quartile range) so a 5% interest rate and a 5000-point stock index can use the same vocabulary.
  • Patching: chop the line into short overlapping windows called patches, just like grouping letters into words. The paper gives an example with patch size 3 and overlap 2: $\{4.7,4.76,6.8\}$, $\{4.76,6.8,7.2\}$, $\{6.8,7.2,6.1\}$.
  • Quantization: split the value range into $B$ bins and replace each number by its bin number $q_t$ in $1\dots B$. With $B=4$ and bins $[4,5),[5,6),[6,7),[7,8)$, the series above becomes $\{1,1,3,4,3\}$. Bigger $B$ = finer detail, but a bigger dictionary to learn.

Those tokens become dense vectors — embeddings — and go into a Transformer. Its self-attention lets every patch look at every other patch to learn trends and seasonality, whether it uses an encoder, decoder, or both. To make up for scarce time data, developers also create fake data by mixing real series together.

Six TSFMs tested in Table A.1: tiny to huge. TTM ~1M params on ~1B points, LagLlama ~2.5M on 352M tokens, Chronos 84B tokens, TimesFM 200M on 100B points, Time-GPT 100B points, Moirai up to 311M on 27B observations across nine domains. The paper uses the first-generation versions released early 2024 as its main test.

Different TSFMs choose different patches, bins and architectures. That's why later they don't all forecast equally well. In the lab, try turning numbers into tokens yourself: change the patch size and the number of bins and watch what the AI actually sees.

005First race~10 min

Only Three AIs Actually Win

Imagine entering six new AIs in a marathon against a simple weekday jogger. The jogger is an AR(1) — it just says "tomorrow will look like today." To win you need a RMSFE ratio below 1: your average squared mistake is smaller than the jogger's. Above 1, you lost.

The paper runs that race on 120 US economic series, month by month from January 1985 to December 2019 (before Covid). Six Time Series AIs — LagLlama, Moirai, Chronos, TimesFM, TTM-Enhanced and Time-GPT — all try zero-shot, meaning they are pretrained on billions of points elsewhere and frozen, not retrained on this data.

Look at h=1 (forecasting next month). Only three are competitive:

  • Chronos — median 0.961 (often beats the jogger)
  • Moirai-large — median 0.999 (about tied, slightly ahead)
  • TimesFM — median 1.014 (about tied, slightly behind, but mass near 1)

The other three consistently lose at the median: LagLlama 1.015, TTM-Enhanced 1.044, Time-GPT 1.077. That pattern holds at h=3, 6 and 12 too. Time-GPT has most of its box above 1 at every horizon. LagLlama does a touch better than the worst two, but the paper says it is systematically beaten by the top three.

Median tells you the typical series. Max tells you the nightmare. At h=1 TimesFM's worst series is 1.482× the AR error, Moirai-large 1.436×, and LagLlama an extreme 7.271× — meaning some forecasts were wildly off. The figure hides those dots to keep the scale readable, but Table 1 reports them.

Why does that matter? TSFMs have a heavier right tail — more chance of an occasional hallucination. The boxes for the top TSFMs are wider than later econometric boxes (standard deviations around 0.10–0.13 vs ~0.06), so a good median can sit next to bad surprises.

One more detail inside the winner circle: size helps. Moirai-large beats Moirai-base at h=1, 0.999 vs 1.008, and similarly at longer horizons. Bigger pretraining and more parameters shave a bit off the error.

The takeaway is not "AI fails" — three models do hang with a decent baseline without any extra training. But zero-shot is not plug-and-play. You need to monitor it, because the same model that wins on many series can blow up on a few.

006Face-off~10 min

Who's More Reliable?

So far the AIs were racing each other. Now they face the economists' champions: a Bayesian VAR (BVAR) and a Factor model. Both are old, plain, and trusted. The question is not just who wins on average, but who you can trust not to embarrass you.

Remember the score. For each of the 120 series and each horizon $h$, the paper computes

$$RMSFE_{ijh}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum e_{i}^2}{\sum e_{bcmk}^2}}$$

where $e_i$ is model $i$'s error and $e_{bcmk}$ is the error of a simple AR(1) that just extrapolates last month. Below 1 means you beat that simple guess. Above 1 means you lost to it. The paper then looks at the whole distribution across 120 series: its middle (median), its spread (standard deviation), and its extremes (min and max).

At $h=1$ month ahead, the centres look surprisingly similar. The BVAR median is 0.983 with a spread of only 0.060. The Factor model is even a touch better at 0.965 (spread 0.065). The best AIs are right there too: Chronos at 0.961 (0.075), Moirai-large at 0.999 (0.102), TimesFM at 1.014 (0.129). If you only looked at medians, you might call it a tie.

The difference is in the shape. The economists' boxes are tight and left-skewed — most of their mass sits below 1, and their worst case is modest: about 1.16 for the BVAR and 1.10 for the Factor model. The AIs are more dispersed and right-tailed. TimesFM's max is 1.482, Moirai-large's is 1.436, and one AI even hits 7.27 on one series. In plain words: the AIs can be brilliant on a few series and completely unreasonable on others. The BVAR almost never is.

Think of it like archery: both hit near the bullseye on average, but the economists' arrows cluster tightly around it, while the AIs spray a few arrows far off target.

What about adding flexibility more simply? The paper also tries two non-linear econometric tools, NNAR (a small neural net) and BART (a sum of trees). Non-linearity alone does not save them. At $h=1$, NNAR's median is 0.988 but its max is 1.910 and its spread is 0.234 — the widest of all. BART is consistently worse than the simple AR, median 1.017 at $h=1$ and above 1 at every horizon up to $h=12$. The richer AIs do capture something these simpler non-linear models miss, but they pay for it with less stability.

The paper's takeaway is blunt: BVARs and Factor models give you more reliable gains. Zero-shot AIs are roughly comparable on average, but you cannot use them mechanically — you need to watch for the occasional wild forecast.

007Team-up~12 min

The Winning Combo: Fixing Mistakes with AI

Economists have a reliable workhorse. AIs have a flexible nose for weird patterns. What if we let each do what it does best?

Think of it like grading homework. The economist (the BVAR from Lesson 3) makes a first prediction:

$$ \hat{y}^{(E)}_{i,t+h}=\mathbb{E}[\,y_{i,t+h}\mid \mathcal{I}_t\,] $$

That's the expected value of the future series $y$ for variable $i$ given all information up to today, $\mathcal{I}_t$. Inevitably, it leaves a mistake — the residual:

$$ \hat{e}_{i,\tau}=y_{i,\tau}-\hat{y}^{(E)}_{i,\tau} $$

in plain words: truth minus economist's forecast. If the economist missed a nonlinear wiggle — say, housing slowing faster than a linear trend — that wiggle lives in $\hat{e}$.

The paper's trick is simple, in three steps. 1) Fit the BVAR and save its residuals. 2) Hand only those residuals, with history $\mathcal{H}_t$, to a Time Series AI (Moirai-large or TimesFM) and ask it to forecast the mistake:

$$ \hat{e}^{(F)}_{i,t+h}=\mathbb{E}[\,\hat{e}_{i,t+h}\mid \mathcal{H}_t\,] $$

3) Add the fix back:

$$ \hat{y}^{(H)}_{i,t+h}=\hat{y}^{(E)}_{i,t+h}+\hat{e}^{(F)}_{i,t+h} $$

a hybrid forecast = economist base + AI correction. You keep the stable structure, and let the AI paint only the leftover nonlinear part.

On the main pre-Covid test (Jan 1985–Dec 2019, 120 series, horizons $h=1$ to $12$) this team wins. At $h=1$ the green hybrid boxes sit most clearly below 1, beating the red line that means "beat the simple AR." Median RMSFE falls from 0.999 to 0.973 for Moirai-large and from 1.014 to 0.980 for TimesFM, both now better than the BVAR alone at 0.983. Their spread shrinks too (std down to about 0.075).

The rescue is biggest in the tails — those "hallucinations" where a raw AI blew up. At $h=1$ the worst-case ratio drops from 1.436 to 1.139 for Moirai and from 1.482 to 1.150 for TimesFM, huddling right next to the BVAR's max of 1.158. The best cases get even better too: the left tail improves from 0.827 to about 0.734 or 0.720. In short, the economist tames the AI's wildest guesses, and the AI sharpens the economist.

Why does it work? It works when they fail on different homework. At $h=1$ the paper measures across the 120 series how correlated two models' errors are. BVAR vs TimesFM is only 0.07 — basically unrelated. BVAR vs Moirai-large is 0.33, vs Chronos 0.52. Meanwhile the AIs agree with each other (Moirai-Chronos 0.80). Uncorrelated means complementary: plenty for the residual step to add.

Move to longer horizons and that complementarity fades. By $h=6$, BVAR-TimesFM correlation has climbed to 0.59 and BVAR-Chronos to 0.84 — almost every model now struggles on the same sticky series. The hybrid still helps, but less. That's exactly why Figure 4's gains are largest at $h=1$.

008When, why, what's next~14 min

Not Every Number Is Equally Predictable

So far we compared averages across all 120 series. The paper's last twist: that average hides huge differences. A forecast that looks okay overall can be brilliant for jobs and terrible for interest rates — or great at 1 month but broken at 12.

First lens: persistence. How sticky is a series? The paper measures it with a single number between -0.46 and 1.00. Near 1.00 means today's value sticks tomorrow — a near random walk, like some interest rates. Median in FRED-MD is 0.52. About 36% of series are above 0.70, and 28% are clustered above 0.90, very sticky.

At $h=1$ (one month ahead), BVAR, the Factor model and Chronos are flat: their red moving-average line stays near $RMSFE = 1$ no matter the persistence. NNAR is the opposite — as persistence goes to 1, its errors shoot above 1.5 for several dots. BART and TimesFM also worsen, Moirai only a little. At $h=12$, most models' averages improve up to about 0.8-0.9 then turn back up above 0.95, especially Moirai and TimesFM. NNAR and BART just get worse the stickier the series, at both horizons.

Key: sticky is not automatically easier. For some AIs, sticky = harder, not easier — they hallucinate bigger misses on the smoothest series.

Second lens: economic family. The 120 series belong to 8 FRED-MD groups — Labor, Housing, Prices, Money, Interest and Exchange Rates, Stocks, and two more. Labor Market (31 series) is the easy, democratic group: at $h=1$, every model's median is 0.905 to 0.988, all at or below 1. Everyone can substitute for each other there.

Interest and Exchange Rates is the tough room: most medians exceed 1 at every horizon. NNAR and BART medians 1.09 to 1.22. TimesFM and Moirai-large also lag 1.01-1.16. Chronos is the exception, staying 0.96-1.00 — its strength lives exactly where other TSFMs fail. Housing shows the biggest split at $h=12$: TimesFM median 0.555 (it almost perfectly predicted the 2007-09 collapse) while BART median is 1.052 in the same group. Stocks (only 3 series) are hard for everyone, like random walks — BART rises to 1.105 at 12 months. Pooling all groups averages away these opposite stories.

Third lens: did the AIs cheat? TSFMs have no timestamps on their training data. Three of six included FRED-MD itself, and all were trained through 2024, past the test period. To mimic that unfair peek, the authors re-trained BVAR and Factor on data all the way to December 2022. With that peek, BVAR and Factor boxes fall entirely below 1 and clearly beat TSFMs. The fair test actually understated how strong the economists' tools are.

Finally, are AIs getting better? Yes. Second-generation models improve at every horizon (Figure 11, Table 4). Chronos-2 median: 0.945 vs 0.961 old at $h=1$ (0.937 vs 0.966 at $h=3$, 0.967 vs 0.980 at $h=6$, 0.979 vs 0.993 at $h=12$). Moirai 2.0 small: 0.968 vs 0.999 old at $h=1$, and its worst case drops 1.436 to 1.068 at $h=1$ — more reliable, not just better centered. Chronos-2 is now the best single model in the table. Fine-tuning the old models on FRED-MD itself, by contrast, gave only a tiny bump but cost about 10 hours on a GPU vs seconds on a CPU for BVAR.

Go deeper: the paper's bottom line is not "AIs win" or "economists win." Because models fail on different series (near-orthogonal errors at $h=1$), the hybrid BVAR+TSFM was most robust, especially short-horizon, and newer TSFMs keep narrowing the gap. But reliability, leakage, and domain still matter as much as raw median.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

TSFM (Time Series Foundational Model)

A huge AI model pre-trained on billions of time points from many domains (energy, weather, web, sales) so it can forecast new series without being retrained for that series. In the paper: LagLlama, Moirai, Chronos, TimesFM, TTM, Time-GPT.

RMSFE ratio

How the paper scores a forecast. RMSFE is Root Mean Squared Forecast Error. The paper divides a model's RMSFE by the RMSFE of a simple AR(1) benchmark. Below 1 = you beat the simple guess. Above 1 = worse. Formula (11) in the paper.

AR(1) benchmark

The simple baseline every model is compared to: predict next month as a linear function of last month only, estimated by OLS. If you can't beat this, you're not useful.

Pseudo out-of-sample

Do a pretend real-time test: start with data up to Dec 1984, forecast Jan 1985 to Dec 1985, then add one month and repeat until Dec 2022. For each horizon h=1 to 12.

BVAR

An economist's workhorse. A Vector Autoregression y_t = Phi_c + Phi_1 y_{t-1}+...+Phi_p y_{t-p}+error that forecasts many variables together. Bayesian version uses priors (Minnesota, sum-of-coefficients) to keep it stable with many variables.

Factor Model

Compress 120 series into a few hidden drivers (factors) with PCA, then forecast each series with y_{i,t}=alpha+beta(L)f_{1,t-h}+gamma(L)y_{i,t-h}+error. The paper uses the first factor and picks lags with BIC.

Zero-shot forecasting

Using a huge pre-trained model directly on a new dataset without retraining it. The paper does this for all TSFMs first.

Fine-tuning

Taking a pre-trained TSFM and doing a little extra training on your dataset (FRED-MD) to adapt it. The paper finds this helps only marginally for TimesFM and Moirai-large.

Tokenization

Chopping a continuous number line into discrete bins or patches so a Transformer can treat numbers like words. Paper describes scaling, patching and quantization.

Data leakage

When a pre-trained model has already seen your test data during its training, so it looks better than it would in real time. Three of the six TSFMs in the paper include FRED-MD in training and all lack timestamps.

Persistence

How sticky a series is, measured in the paper by its 1-lag partial autocorrelation (-0.46 to 1.00, median 0.52). Near 1.00 = near random walk like some interest rates.

Hallucination

A wild forecast far off reality. Paper shows TSFMs sometimes produce RMSFE up to 7.27 at h=1 while BVAR max is 1.158.
