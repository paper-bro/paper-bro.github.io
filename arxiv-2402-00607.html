001Lesson 1 · The problem~5 min

Why time-series data keeps tripping up AI

Imagine you build one AI to read 100 points at a time — just a little window of a longer story. You show it heartbeats, then stock prices, then hourly electricity use. It should be similar, right? It's all lines over time. In practice it's not. The line for a brain wave looks nothing like a stock crash or a summer heat wave — different speeds, different heights, different kinds of messiness.

The paper that this course follows starts exactly here. The authors, Fu and colleagues, point out that time-series AI hits three walls when it learns from real data:

  1. Data quality problems: real recordings often include noisy or even adversarial examples — like a sensor glitch that looks like a spike. A model that copies those learns to be brittle.
  2. Bias and blind spots: if your hospital data mostly comes from one kind of patient, or your finance data mostly from quiet markets, the model inherits those narrow habits. It works where it was trained and stumbles elsewhere.
  3. Poor generalization: time-series love sudden shifts — a pandemic wave, a blackout, a flash crash. A model trained on yesterday's patterns can miss tomorrow's because it has never seen that shape before.

This is surprising because large language models do get better when you mix many domains of text together. Time-series doesn't work that way yet. A model that learns weather well often does worse on traffic or EEG brain signals, because the basic statistics — how fast things change, how noisy they are, what counts as a trend — are completely different across domains.

The natural fix sounds like: make fake data to fill the gaps. But almost every current generator needs real data to train the generator itself — you show it real EEG or real electricity, it learns to mimic that. That helps a little with data quality and bias, but it can't promise to cover unseen cases. If the generator never saw a certain kind of shift, it will never make it.

So the authors ask a bolder question: can we make fake time-series that need zero real data and zero learnable parameters to create, yet are versatile enough that a model trained only on the fakes beats one trained on real data? They call their answer InfoBoost, shown in the diagram below: build the fake from three separate, named parts — rhythms, noise (and how much noise), and long-term trend — and then teach a model to take them back apart.

We will unpack that recipe over the next lessons, but first get a feel for why "one-size-fits-all" is hard. In the lab, pick a domain and watch how the same 100-point window changes its speed, fuzziness, and overall look. Notice the stats: Range, Wiggle, and Trend slope. Try to find two domains that a single model would struggle to handle with the same rules — that's the problem InfoBoost tries to solve.

002Lesson 2 · Background you need~6 min

Everything that repeats can be built from waves

Think of a song you know. A guitar chord sounds like one rich sound, but it is really three or four simple notes played together. Time-series are similar: a heartbeat, a stock price wiggle, or the daily hum of electricity looks complicated, but a lot of the repeating part can be built by adding up very simple, smooth waves.

In this paper that simple building block is a sine wave — the smooth up-and-down you get if you trace a spinning wheel. You only need three numbers to define one:

  • Amplitude ( A ) — how tall the wave is. The paper keeps it between 0 and 1 before mixing. Bigger amplitude = bigger swing.
  • Frequency ( f ) — how fast it repeats. High frequency = many wiggles in your window, low frequency = slow sway.
  • Phase ( φ , phi) — where the wave starts in its cycle, between 0 and 2π (one full circle). Same shape, just slid left or right.
$$ y = A \, \sin(2\pi f t + \phi) $$

Here t is time as you move across your sampling window, and y is the value at that time. Change A, f, or φ and you change that one ingredient.

Superposition just means adding. Take wave A, wave B, wave C, and at each time point add their heights. The result can look jagged, spiky, or almost smooth — even though each part was perfectly regular. The paper's core intuition is: almost any repeating pattern (what it later calls Multi-source Rhythmic Data) can be made this way by stacking a handful of random sines.

Why bother? Fourier transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), Continuous Fourier Transform (CFT), and Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) do the reverse: they try to look at a complicated wiggle and say “which frequencies with which amplitudes and phases were added to make this?” If that worked perfectly, you could turn any time-series from “value over time” into “ingredients over frequency” — a powerful way to understand and compare data.

It works beautifully on clean signals. But the paper points out three places where classic frequency extraction gets fragile on real data:

  1. Multiple sources mixed together. Real data is often several independent rhythms piled on top of each other. Pulling them back apart into separate frequency sets is hard.
  2. Noise everywhere. Real data has random wiggles of many kinds. These break the neat assumption that “every point can be described by just amplitude + phase per frequency.”
  3. Trends longer than the window. If a slow drift has a period longer than your sampling window length N, you never see a full cycle. A normal transform can miss it completely.

The InfoBoost approach does not try to fix the transform. It will instead build data where those three pieces are known separately from the start — so a model can learn to pull them apart again. But first you need to feel how the building works.

Try it below: move amplitude, frequency and phase for three sines and watch the black sum change. Then check the little frequency view. When you set a sine to frequency 3 with amplitude 0.5, you should see a dot exactly there. Classic methods try to recover those dots from the black line alone — now you can see why noise and a long trend would make that guessing game much harder.

003Lesson 3 · InfoBoost ingredient 1~5 min

Recipe part 1: rhythms from a handful of sines

Real heartbeats, stock ticks, and weather readings all wiggle in a rhythm — but never a clean one. The trick InfoBoost uses is simple: instead of copying real wiggles, build brand-new ones by stacking a handful of perfect sine waves.

That stack has a name in the paper: MRD, short for Multi-source Rhythmic Data. "Multi-source" just means "several independent sine waves added together." A single sine wave needs three numbers: amplitude (how tall, 0 to 1), frequency (how fast it repeats), and phase (where it starts, 0 to 2π). MRD is what you get when you pick a random count of them — usually 3 to 10, the paper's Figure 2 shows 5 as one example — and add them up.

$$ f_{\max}=\frac{f_s}{2}=\frac{1}{2t} \qquad f_{\min}\approx\frac{1}{N} $$

Those two limits are the guardrails. $N$ is your sampling window length — how many points you look at at once. $t$ is the time between two points (for everyday discrete data the paper sets $t=1$). The first guardrail, $f_{\max}=1/(2t)$, comes from the Nyquist-Shannon rule: you must sample at least twice as fast as the fastest wave you want to capture, or it will look like a different, fake-slow wave — that's called aliasing. With $t=1$, that means nothing faster than $f_{\max}=0.5$ is allowed.

The second guardrail, $f_{\min}\approx 1/N$, says the slowest wave you can still see has to fit at least once inside your window. A 100-point window can't reliably show a wave that takes 500 points to complete. By sampling every frequency uniformly between $f_{\min}$ and $f_{\max}$, InfoBoost gives every allowed speed an equal chance — simple fast wiggles and slow rolling waves alike.

Each MRD wave is built in three quick steps: 1) pick how many sines (3–10), 2) for each sine roll a random frequency in $[1/N, 1/2]$, phase in $[0,2\pi]$, amplitude in $[0,1]$, 3) add them all and squash the total to the 0-to-1 range. The paper also scales the starting amplitudes by the count so a 10-wave stack isn't automatically 10× taller.

The result looks familiar: sometimes a clean pair of bumps, sometimes a messy tangled line that still feels natural. Because the choices are random but the limits are strict, you can generate endless different MRDs and they will still obey real-world sampling physics. That endless, rule-following variety is what lets later lessons train a model without ever showing it real data.

004Lesson 4 · Ingredients 2 & 3 + mixing~6 min

Recipe parts 2 & 3: noise, trends, and the perfect blend

You learned InfoBoost builds a rhythm from a handful of random sine waves. Real data is more than rhythm — it is rhythm plus fuzz plus slow drift, all tangled together. Lesson 4 adds the other two ingredients and the recipe for stirring them.

Noise: 15 flavors, then 3 cleaning tricks

Real noise is not just one kind of hiss. The paper builds a noise library of 15 different distributions in 5 families:

  • CCD — Common Continuous like normal (bell) curves
  • CDD — Common Discrete like Bernoulli (coin-flip), geometric, Poisson (count spikes)
  • HTD — Heavy-Tailed where rare huge jumps happen
  • DRND — Related to Normal like t-distribution and Pareto
  • SPD — Shape-parameter families like Beta and Gamma that can be skewed

A noise sample is never just drawn and done. It goes through 3 steps for variety:

  1. Random parameters — each distribution has its own knobs; the paper samples them randomly so the same family looks different each time.
  2. Partial y-axis flip — flip some of the result upside-down so values do not all pile up at one extreme.
  3. Small-kernel smoothing — blur with a small window to create little temporal wiggles. The kernel is kept small compared to the total window length $N$ so this noise does not accidentally look like a slow trend.

Trend: the slow drift beyond your window

Some trends are longer than the $N$ points you see — like a year-long swing seen through a week-long window. InfoBoost calls this Trend Information (TI) and picks randomly between two ways to make it:

🌊

(a) Multi-Sine Trend

A few sine waves like the rhythm, but with periods forced to be longer than $N$, with a random multiplier $>1$ for extra variety. Few spikes, slow shape — a long-wave drift.

🫧

(b) Random Noise Trend

Make noise as in 3.1.2, then blur it with a large kernel. The large smoothing washes out fast wiggles and leaves only a slow, irregular drift.

The paper deliberately uses a large kernel here and a small kernel for noise, so the two ingredients stay distinct.

The perfect blend: ratios that sum to 1

Each ingredient is first standardized to the same scale, $[-1,1]$, so none dominates by size alone. Then the paper samples three weights that must add to one:

$$r_{rhyth} + r_{noise} + r_{trend} = 1$$

Those are the shares of rhythm, noise, and trend in the final data. The final synthetic trace is just the weighted sum:

$$\text{Sync} = r_{rhyth}\times\text{Rhyth} + r_{noise}\times\text{Noise} + r_{trend}\times\text{Trend}$$
Think cooking: Rhyth, Noise, Trend are three bowls already seasoned to taste ($[-1,1]$). The $r$ values are how many spoons from each bowl you add. More $r_{noise}$ makes the rhythm harder to see; more $r_{trend}$ tilts everything slowly up or down.

005Lesson 5 · Learning without real data~6 min

How fake data teaches a model to take things apart

Imagine someone hands you a smoothie and asks: how much strawberry, how much yogurt, how much spinach went in? With a real smoothie you can only guess. But if you made thousands of smoothies and wrote down every recipe, you could train a taster to work backwards — taste → recipe.

That's the trick in InfoBoost. A real recording — hourly electricity use, a heartbeat trace — never comes with labels that say "40% rhythm, 30% noise, 30% trend" or "the main rhythm was at frequency 0.12". The paper calls those missing labels MRD (multi-source rhythmic data), TN & NR (noise type and noise ratio), and TI (trend information). By construction, every fake example does have them.

So how do those labels become something a neural net can learn? The paper's step is called "synthetic info normalization." Take all the knobs used to build one Sync — frequencies, phases, amplitudes (0 to 1), count of sines, noise type and class, trend class, smoothing kernel sizes, and the three mixing ratios — more than a dozen kinds in total.

$$r_{\text{rhyth}}+r_{\text{noise}}+r_{\text{trend}}=1 \quad\text{and}\quad \text{Sync}=r_{\text{rhyth}}\times\text{Rhyth}+r_{\text{noise}}\times\text{Noise}+r_{\text{trend}}\times\text{Trend}$$

Each knob is squashed to a common scale, $-1$ to $1$ (some to $0$ to $1$), just like converting inches and pounds to the same score. Then that single number is copied — technically broadcast or interpolated — across the whole sampling window of length $N$. The result is a multi-channel matrix with shape (number-of-knobs) × N. If $N=64$ and you track 15 knobs, the label is a 15-by-64 sheet, not just one answer.

Key idea: input = one wiggly line Sync. Label = the whole recipe sheet that made it. The model learns the mapping wave → recipe. The paper trains this with a simple straight-line loss schedule — no fancy tricks.

Why not overfit? And which model? The researchers tried three very different architects — Bi-LSTM (a memory-based recurrent net), DLinear (a simple linear net), and PatchTST (a transformer) — on only synthetic inputs and recipe sheets. Because the recipe can be re-rolled infinitely — pick a new random frequency, a new noise type, new ratios — there are effectively endless unique training examples. That endless variety makes memorizing impossible. Visually, DLinear drew the cleanest separations, so InfoBoost keeps it as its universal extractor. The paper notes the other two also work.

Once trained, you can freeze that extractor and feed it real data it never saw. It still tries to paint the recipe sheet — this much looks rhythmic, this much looks noisy, this much looks like a slow drift — giving you an explicit, peek-inside view of any time-series.

006Lesson 6 · The fair fight~6 min

Putting synthetic to the test against real data

How do you prove fake data is actually good? You make it compete fairly against real data — same amount, same test, same judges.

The InfoBoost team ran a kind of time-series Olympics. They gathered 35 public datasets from very different worlds: brain waves (EEG), disease counts, electricity use, crypto prices, traffic and weather. For simplicity they grouped them into 5 big families.

The task they chose was reconstruction: show a model a real trace and ask it to rebuild it as exactly as possible. If it rebuilds well, it has learned the right patterns. If it memorized only its training set, it fails on new data. That makes reconstruction a direct test of generalization — working on unseen data.

The fair-fight setup: randomly pick about 70% of the datasets — 24 of 35 — and take roughly 200,000 windows as the real-trained training set. Then make the same number — ~200,000 — of purely synthetic windows with no real data at all for the sync-trained model. Both are tested on the remaining real test set they never saw. Because the pick is random, sometimes the real-trained side even gets a head start — a training type that overlaps the test type.

To be extra fair they tried three very different model styles: BiLSTM (a recurrent model that reads time forward and backward), DLinear (a simple linear model), and PatchTST (a transformer, like those in language models). And they used four different rulers, all "lower is better":

  • MSE — mean squared error, the average squared gap between rebuilt and original.
  • SDL — 1 minus SSIM, how structurally different the shapes look.
  • DTW — dynamic time warping distance, which allows a wiggle to be slightly shifted in time and still count as similar.
  • DH — distance between histograms, whether the overall distribution of values matches.

Result: out of 60 match-ups (5 groups × 4 metrics × 3 models), the model trained only on fake data won 55. In the two clear losses — both DH and DTW for PatchTST on the Energy group — the fake-trained model was still very close. Everywhere else, fake was better, often by a lot.

007Lesson 7 · Unlimited fuel~5 min

Why never reusing fake data wins faster

Imagine studying for a test with only one practice test. You do it over and over. First you get better, then you start memorizing the exact questions and get worse on a new exam. That's overfitting — and the paper saw it happen.

In Lesson 6 the “limited” synthetic model got about 200,000 fake examples, the same amount as the real-data model. That was fair, but 200,000 is still a fixed pile. If you keep training for many epochs (an epoch = one full pass over the training pile) at the same learning rate, the model can memorize that pile.

The effect was strongest for PatchTST, the transformer model. Transformers are powerful pattern-matchers, so they memorize fastest. Their loss — the error on fresh real data they never saw — dipped, then started to climb again.

Key idea: limited data + many repeats = memorization. The model learns the quirks of its 200k examples, not the general rules.

InfoBoost has a simple fix that only fake data allows: never reuse. In the “unlimitSync” setup, the computer throws away the old fake batch and creates a brand-new synthetic set at every epoch. No data limit, no extra cost to collect real data.

Figure 6 and Table 1 in the paper tell the story. With fresh data each epoch the model learns faster and never climbs back up:

  • DLinear — Real: best 4.0×10-7 at epoch 21. Limited Sync: 1.5×10-7 at epoch 21. Unlimited: 8×10-8 at epoch 8 — lower and 13 epochs earlier.
  • BiLSTM — Real: 7.4×10-7 at 68. Limited Sync: 3×10-8 at 65. Unlimited: 1×10-8 at 30 — 3× lower, more than twice as fast.
  • PatchTST — Real: 8.70×10-6 at 21. Limited Sync: 7.52×10-6 at 5. Unlimited: 7.26×10-6 at 4 — best of all, with no rise later.

The small wiggles you see in the unlimited curve are not overfitting. They just reflect that each epoch's new fake mix is a little different — easier or harder — so the validation loss jiggles instead of climbing.

008Lesson 8 · Seeing inside real data~5 min

What we can now see — and what it unlocks

InfoBoost was trained without ever seeing a single real recording. Yet its extractor can look at a fresh, real trace — an EEG, an electricity meter, a COVID curve — and peel it apart as if it knows the recipe inside.

How? Remember the recipe from Lesson 4: Sync = r_rhyth × Rhyth + r_noise × Noise + r_trend × Trend with r_rhyth+r_noise+r_trend=1. During training the model saw thousands of fake Sync traces together with the exact numbers that made them: which sines, which of the 15 noise types, which trend method, and the three ratios. Real data never comes with those labels. Synthetic data does, by construction.

That lets InfoBoost learn an unmixer. After training, you feed it any window of length N — say 128 points — and it returns estimates of the four things it practised on: the multi-source rhythm (MRD), the noise type (TN), the noise ratio (NR), and the slow trend (TI). In Figure 7 the paper shows this on three very different real datasets: each black input is split into three colored layers, each already multiplied by its predicted ratio so you can see at a glance how much of the final shape came from rhythm versus noise versus trend. No fine-tuning on real data was needed, and the same DLinear extractor worked for all 35 datasets. The paper also tried BiLSTM and PatchTST versions and they worked too, but DLinear looked best.

Why bother peeling? Because two of the peels get in the way of the classic tool. A Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is supposed to turn time into frequencies — tall spikes where a rhythm repeats. But noise adds many fake frequencies and a long trend acts like a huge low-frequency wave that smears the spectrum. After InfoBoost keeps only the rhythmic part, the DFT suddenly shows sharp, clean peaks (Figure 8 on the COVID death data). In other words, the extractor doubles as a cleaner for frequency analysis.

The bigger picture from the paper’s conclusion is this: InfoBoost is the first to do both halves without real data — a non-deep-learning generator that needs zero real examples and zero learnable parameters to make its fakes, and a universal extractor that needs zero fine-tuning to interpret real data. It produces not just better reconstructions (Lesson 6) but an interpretable breakdown you can plot and reason about.

That unlocks more than pretty pictures. If you can separate the parts everywhere, you can choose to keep just the rhythm for better DFT, study the noise level as a data-quality meter, or watch trends that stretch beyond your window. ABCWord bank

Word bank.

time-series

Data recorded over time, like hourly temperature, heartbeats, or stock prices.

generalization

How well a model works on new, unseen data it was not trained on.

synthetic data

Artificial data made by a computer program to stand in for real data.

rhythmic data / MRD

Regular, repeating patterns in a time-series. In InfoBoost: Multi-source Rhythmic Data (MRD) made by adding sine waves.

noise (TN)

Random, irregular wiggles that hide the true signal. InfoBoost simulates 15 types.

noise ratio (NR)

Ratio that says how much noise is in the final synthetic mix.

trend information (TI)

A slow, long-term drift up or down that may be longer than the window you are looking at. InfoBoost calls it TI.

sine wave

A simple repeating wave defined by amplitude (height), frequency (how fast it repeats), and phase (where it starts).

Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem

Rule that to capture a wave you must sample at least twice as fast as its highest frequency. InfoBoost uses it to set f_max = 1/(2t) and f_min ≈ 1/N where t is time between samples and N is window length.

Fourier transform / DFT

Math tools (CFT, DFT, DCT) that turn a time-series into its frequencies, like finding which musical notes make up a chord.

representation learning / extractor

Training a model to pull apart a time-series into its separate parts: rhythm, noise, and trend.

reconstruction

Task where a model sees a time-series and tries to rebuild it exactly. Good rebuilding means it learned useful patterns.

MSE / SDL / DTW / DH

Four ways the paper measures reconstruction quality: MSE (average squared error), SDL = 1 - SSIM (structural difference), DTW (distance allowing time shifts), DH (difference between histograms). Lower is better.

overfitting

When a model memorizes its training data and gets worse on new data.
