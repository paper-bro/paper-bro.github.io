001LESSON 1~8 min

Your Laptop Can't Hold a Giant Brain

Your favorite chat model has billions of numbers inside it — its weights. Think of each weight like a tiny dial that helps the model decide what word comes next. Llama-3.1-8B has 8 billion dials. At full precision each dial uses 16 bits (16 little 0/1 switches), so roughly 8 billion × 16 bits ÷ 8 ≈ 16 gigabytes just for the weights. Llama-3.1-70B is roughly 70 billion × 16 bits ÷ 8 ≈ 140 gigabytes. No laptop can hold that while also trying to think fast.

The bottleneck isn't just storage, it's speed. To answer you, the computer has to stream all those dials from memory. Less memory to move = faster answers. That's why for everyday use — with tools like llama.cpp and Ollama — people routinely shrink models to 2 to 3 bits per parameter (bpp). Bits per parameter just means "average switches per dial."

In this simplified estimate, 3 bpp would be roughly 5× smaller than 16 bpp, and 2 bpp roughly 8× smaller. The paper itself reports bpp numbers — for example GSQ at 2-bit on the 8B model is 2.13 bpp in Table 1 — and notes that bit/param excludes non-quantized tensors. It never reports gigabytes on disk. Real files are a bit larger than params×bpp/8 because some layers are left unquantized. So when you see sizes in the lab below, read them as "roughly, in this demo" — not as a measurement from the paper.

2–3 bpp is powerful, but also dangerous. Rounding each precise dial to just a few possible values adds error. Drop below 3–4 bpp and the model's answers get noticeably worse — the "error wall."

🧱

Wave 1: Simple scalar

One dial at a time. Methods like GPTQ, AWQ, and bitsandbytes round each weight on its own to a small uniform grid. Dead simple, and they come with super-fast unpacking kernels that actually make your laptop 5–6× faster. But they plateau around 3–4 bpp.

🧬

Wave 2: Clever vector / trellis

Groups at a time. Methods like QTIP, AQLM / PV-Tuning, GPTVQ and QuIP# learn a shared codebook for groups of weights. Much more accurate at 2–3 bpp, but the format is complex, hard to scale to trillion-parameter models, and gives only small real speedups over full precision.

That leaves a frustrating gap: the easy, fast way isn't accurate enough, and the accurate way isn't easy or fast.

Is the gap fundamental? The GSQ paper asks: what if most of the vector advantage is just better optimization — picking smarter rounding choices — and a carefully trained scalar quantizer could recover most of it while staying fully compatible with existing scalar kernels and GGUF files?

That's what you'll test in the lab. Move the bpp slider from 16 down to 1.58 and watch two things happen: the rough-estimate memory bars collapse (that's why we want 2–3 bpp) and the 20 example weights snap to fewer and fewer horizontal grid lines, so the average rounding error shoots up below 3 bpp. Toggle Scalar vs Vector to feel the core trade-off, then try to guess where the error wall begins.

002LESSON 2~10 min

Rounding One Number at a Time

Think of a giant language model as a cabinet with billions of tiny dials. Each dial is a single number — a weight the model learned during training. The cabinet works beautifully, but it won't fit in your laptop. Quantization is the idea: round each dial to one of a very small set of allowed values, so you can store it with far fewer bits.

In a scalar quantizer we round every weight by itself. Pick a bit-width $b$, say $b=2$. Then the allowed grid has size $|G_b| = 2^b$ — that's $4$ values for 2-bit, $8$ for 3-bit, $2$ for 1-bit. Call that grid $\mathcal{G}_b$. To cover the real range of weights, we stretch the grid by a number $s$ called the scale:

$$\mathcal{C}_{b\text{-bit}} = \{ \bar{\mathbf{w}} \mid \bar{\mathbf{w}} = s\cdot \mathbf{q};\; s \in \mathbb{R},\; \mathbf{q} \in \mathcal{G}_b^d \}$$

Here $\mathbf{w}$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is the original $d$ weights in one layer, $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ is the rounded version, and each entry of $\mathbf{q}$ is one of those few grid values. For example the paper uses $\mathcal{G}_2 = \{-2,-1,0,1\}$ for 2-bit, so with scale $s=0.3$ the real allowed numbers are $\{-0.6,-0.3,0,0.3\}$.

We want the rounded layer to act like the original. The paper writes that goal as:

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \arg\min_{\bar{\mathbf{w}} \in \mathcal{C}} \left\lVert f(\mathbf{x};\bar{\mathbf{w}}) - f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w}) \right\rVert_F^2$$

$f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w})$ is just the layer's output when you feed it some calibration text $\mathbf{x}$. $\lVert \cdot \rVert_F^2$ means "add up all squared differences" between the original output and the rounded output. We search for the $\bar{\mathbf{w}}$ inside our tiny grid that makes that difference as small as possible.

One scale cannot fit all weights perfectly. If group A has small weights and group B has large weights, the same grid stretch hurts one of them. So GSQ shares a scale over a group of 128 consecutive weights in a row — like sharing one ruler for 128 neighbors. That's group-wise, symmetric quantization. Symmetric means no extra shift knob; the grid is centered at zero. Most baselines in the paper actually use an extra shift called a zero-point, which gives them more freedom than GSQ — yet GSQ still wins by making better rounding choices.

Why does this matter? A scalar grid is dead simple: "each weight picks the nearest line." A vector quantizer groups several weights together and picks a shared codebook entry for the whole group — more expressive, but you need special lookup code to run it, and it hardly speeds up decoding. GSQ keeps the simple scalar form, so it plugs into existing fast kernels for local running.

Now try it yourself: you have two groups of weights with different spreads and one scale per group. Drag the scales and watch every weight snap to the nearest grid line. Turn on "one scale for all" to feel why grouping helps, and toggle the zero-point to see what that extra freedom would have added.

003LESSON 3~12 min

Turning Hard Choices into Soft Mixes

Imagine you must pick one ice-cream flavour from a menu: chocolate, vanilla, strawberry, or mint. That's a hard choice — you get one, not a little of each. Training an AI by trial-and-error on hard choices doesn't work well, because nudging your preference a tiny bit doesn't change which flavour you pick until you cross a threshold. There's no smooth slope to follow — no gradient.

The paper's core trick is to practice with a soft mix first. Instead of picking one grid point for a weight, you make a smoothie: a weighted average of all the allowed points. If your grid is $\{-2,-1,0,1\}$ scaled by $s=1$, the soft weight is

$$ \hat{w}_{\text{soft}} = \sum_{k} p_k \cdot g_k $$

where $g_k$ is a grid value like $-2$ or $0$, and $p_k$ is how much of it you pour in. When you learn to make the mix taste right, you slowly freeze it until only one flavour remains.

How do you get those $p_k$? Give each candidate a trainable score called a logit $\ell_k$. Bigger logit means you prefer that grid point. Add a little random shake $g_k$ drawn from a Gumbel distribution — this simulates actually sampling a choice — divide by a knob called temperature $\tau$, and squash into probabilities with softmax:

$$ p_k = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{\ell_k + g_k}{\tau}\right) $$

$g_k$ is just noise that makes training explore different choices. $\tau$ controls how soft the mix is: when $\tau$ is high (the paper starts at $2.0$), the probabilities spread out and $\hat{w}_{\text{soft}}$ sits between grid lines — gradients flow nicely to every logit, so the model can change its mind. As training anneals $\tau$ down toward $0.05$, the biggest $p_k$ rushes to $1$ and the rest to $0$. The soft mix hardens into a single hard pick — a one-hot choice you can actually deploy.

Key insight: everything becomes differentiable. The soft weight $\hat{w}_{\text{soft}}$ depends smoothly on the logits $\ell_k$ and on the scale $s$ (because the real grid points are $s \cdot g_k$). That means one reconstruction loss — how far the quantized layer's output is from the full-precision output — can update both what you pick and how big the steps are, together.

That's the difference from older scalar methods. They would freeze the rounding first and only then tune the scales $s$. GSQ learns them jointly with gradient descent: logits and scales move together to minimize $\|f(\mathbf{x};\bar{\mathbf{w}}) - f(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{w})\|_F^2$, first with cheap layer-wise losses and later block-wise. The randomness is re-sampled each forward pass, so averaging over micro-batches denoises it — like tasting the smoothie several times.

004LESSON 4~10 min

The Simplest Hard Case: -1, 0, +1

Two bits give you four choices. But the hardest test for shrinking is even simpler: only three choices — $−1$, $0$, or $+1$ — stretched by a single scale number $s$. That’s 1.58 bits per weight, called ternary. If you can make this work, you’ve proven your optimizer is clever, not just your format.

The GSQ paper starts here for a reason. Ternary is tiny enough to learn perfectly, yet every lesson transfers to 2-bit and 3-bit later.

Two switches, not three buttons

The paper doesn’t treat ${-s,0,+s}$ as three equal buttons. It splits each weight into two yes/no switches multiplied together:

$$ \bar{w} = s \cdot m \cdot b, \quad m \in \{0,1\},\; b \in \{-1,+1\} $$

Read it in words: $s$ is the shared scale for your group of 128 weights (like a volume knob for the whole group). $m$ is a mask: $1$ means “keep this weight”, $0$ means “zero it out”. $b$ is a sign: $+1$ or $-1$. Multiply them and you get exactly $-s$, $0$, or $+s$. The paper calls this constraint $\mathcal{C}_{\text{ternary}}$ (Equation 2).

Why two switches? Because now we have $2d$ simple binary choices instead of one 3-way choice. Each binary choice can be learned with the soft-to-hard trick from the last lesson.

For each weight we keep two learnable numbers: a mask logit $\ell^{(m)}$ (“should it be non-zero?”) and a sign logit $\ell^{(b)}$ (“if non-zero, plus or minus?”). Positive means yes to $1$ / $+1$, negative means the opposite. In the binary case the paper uses one logit $\ell$ and $−\ell$ for the other option — that’s just $\text{sigmoid}(2\ell)$ probability.

Start where GPTQ left off — then shake

Learning from scratch would take forever. GSQ therefore warm-starts from an existing simple quantizer called GPTQ, which already gives a decent ternary guess $q_{\text{GPTQ}} \in \{-1,0,1\}$ for each weight.

Equation 3 in the paper turns that guess into starting logits:

$$ \ell^{(m)}_{\text{GPTQ}} = \begin{cases}+1.0 & \text{if } q_{\text{GPTQ}}\neq 0 \\ -1.0 & \text{if } q_{\text{GPTQ}}=0\end{cases}, \quad \ell^{(b)}_{\text{GPTQ}} = \begin{cases}+1.0 & \text{if } q_{\text{GPTQ}}=+1 \\ -1.0 & \text{if } q_{\text{GPTQ}}=-1 \\ 0.0 & \text{if } q_{\text{GPTQ}}=0\end{cases} $$

In plain words: if GPTQ said “keep it”, start the mask logit positive; if “zero it”, start negative. Same for the sign. When GPTQ said zero, the sign doesn’t matter yet, so start at $0$ — it may wake up later if the mask flips on.

But copying GPTQ exactly would get you stuck in the same mistakes. So the paper adds a little exploratory noise (Equation 4):

$$ \ell = \sigma_{\text{init}} \cdot (\varepsilon + \alpha \cdot \ell_{\text{GPTQ}}), \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) $$

Here $\alpha$ controls how much you trust GPTQ (bigger $\alpha$ = stay closer to its guess), $\varepsilon$ is fresh Gaussian noise for each weight, and $\sigma_{\text{init}}$ sets the overall size of the logits. Think of it like tracing over a faint GPTQ sketch, then gently shaking the paper so nearby lines can be tried.

The scale $s$ itself also starts from GPTQ’s scale. Then during training all of them — $s$ plus both sets of logits — are tuned together by gradient descent on the reconstruction error $\lVert f(x;\bar{w})-f(x;w)\rVert_F^2$. As temperature $\tau$ anneals from $2\to0.05$, the soft mixes harden into final $-s/0/+s$ picks.

The same warm-start idea is reused for 2-bit and 3-bit later (Appendix A): for $b=2$ the initial logits are centered at the GPTQ grid value; for $b>2$ the $0$-shift keeps the GPTQ grid index. That’s why ternary is the template.

005LESSON 5~10 min

When Choices Explode, Just Nudge

Imagine a ruler with 8 tick marks. To pick the best tick for one weight, you could compare all 8. For 3-bit quantization that is exactly what a naive learner would do: keep a separate score (a logit) for each of the 8 possible values. For 4-bit it is 16 scores. It blows up fast.

Let's make that concrete. In the last lessons a weight was rounded to a small grid scaled by $s$:

$$\mathcal{C}_{b\text{-bit}}=\{\bar{\mathbf w}\mid \bar{\mathbf w}=s\cdot \mathbf q,\; \mathbf q\in \mathcal G_b^d \}$$

Here $b$ is bits, $\mathcal G_b$ is the ordered grid with $|\mathcal G_b|=2^b$ allowed values, $d$ is number of weights, $s$ is the per-group scale and $\mathbf q$ is the choice of grid point. For GSQ's 2-bit example from the paper, $\mathcal G_2=\{-2,-1,0,1\}$ and the scale $s$ is even allowed to be negative so the grid is not biased toward negative numbers.

If you use Gumbel-Softmax directly, each weight needs $2^b$ trainable logits — one per candidate. That's $4d+1$ parameters for 2-bit (4 logits per weight + 1 shared $s$), $8d+1$ for 3-bit, $16d+1$ for 4-bit. That's exponential in $b$. Since the optimizer has to store those logits alongside the model, memory becomes 2 to 5 times the model itself. Not practical.

Key trick: most weights don't actually jump far from where they started. Start from a good guess (GPTQ), then only learn a tiny nudge.

The paper calls the fix the local-shift formulation. Keep the GPTQ starting index $j_i^0$ — the tick GPTQ picked for weight $i$. Instead of $2^b$ logits, learn only 5 logits for a shift $\delta_i$:

$$\delta_i\in\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\},\quad j_i=\mathrm{clip}(j_i^0+\delta_i,\,1,\,2^b),\quad q_i=(\mathcal G_b)_{j_i}$$

In words: $\delta_i=0$ means "stay where GPTQ put you", $\pm1$ means "move one tick over", $\pm2$ means "move two". $\mathrm{clip}$ just keeps you inside 1 to $2^b$. The set of achievable quantized vectors becomes

$$\mathcal C^{\mathrm{shift}}_{b\text{-bit}}=\{s\cdot\mathbf q\mid q_i=(\mathcal G_b)_{\mathrm{clip}(j_i^0+\delta_i,1,2^b)},\;\delta_i\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\}$$

Now every weight costs 5 logits, not $2^b$. Total is $5d+1$ whether $b=3$ or $b=4$. For 3-bit you go from 8 to 5 per weight; for 4-bit from 16 to 5 — the exponential is gone.

Does restricting to $\pm2$ miss good solutions? The paper checked. They ran the expensive full 8-way search for 3-bit on Llama-3.1-8B and measured how far the final pick moved from GPTQ ($\Delta=|j-j^0|$):

84.635% stayed at $\Delta=0$, 15.357% moved $\Delta=1$, only 0.008% moved $\Delta=2$, and 0.000004% moved $\Delta=3$. That is, 99.999996% of weights landed inside the $\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ window. You get almost all the benefit of looking everywhere by looking only nearby.

006LESSON 6~12 min

Teaching It Without Getting Stuck

Picking the best rounding for each weight is a discrete puzzle — no slope to follow. GSQ makes it learnable with a soft mix, but two practical traps remain: where you compute your error, and whether the gradient is still alive when the mix hardens.

First, where. The ideal loss is simple: keep the block’s output close to the full-precision block, || f(x; bar_w) – f(x; w) ||². The most obvious way is to optimize every quantized layer in a Transformer block together under that one block loss. The paper tried that — call it Block-all — and it was surprisingly weak.

The problem is distance. Early layers like Q and K affect the block output only through attention and the residual path, so the block loss gives them a blurry, indirect signal. Later layers like the MLP get a sharper signal. GSQ therefore stages the block:

  1. Optimize Q and K projections independently, each with its own linear reconstruction loss.
  2. Freeze them, then optimize V and O jointly under the self-attention output loss.
  3. Freeze attention, then optimize the MLP under the full block loss.

After a block is done it is frozen and the already-quantized activations feed the next block, so later blocks learn to compensate for earlier error. On Llama-3.1-8B at 2-bit, Only-linear got 57.68 avg, Block-all 62.02, while staged GSQ reached 65.54 — a 3.5-point gain for staging alone — before any final scale tuning.

Why gradients can vanish: When your soft probabilities are almost one-hot, nudging the logits barely changes the output. The paper proves ||∇a ℓ||∞ ≤ (K-1)G/τ · e−Δ/τ. Here Δ is the gap between the best logit and the runner-up, τ is temperature, K is number of choices (e.g. 4), and G bounds the downstream loss slope. Large Δ/τ → exponentially tiny gradients.

In plain words: as you anneal τ from 2.0 → 0.05, every distribution sharpens and Δ/τ grows. The lemma says the slope you need for learning collapses. AdamW, which scales steps by the size of recent gradients, shrinks to zero and gets stuck — you’d need an uncomfortably huge learning rate to move before it saturates.

Lion fixes this differently. Instead of using gradient magnitude, it updates with sign(momentum) — basically just the direction. When gradients are non-zero but exponentially small, Lion still steps with a steady size. The paper shows Lion beats AdamW by +3.70 points on average at 8B 2-bit (65.54 vs 61.84), with +4.7 on WinoGrande.

One more stabilizer: gradient accumulation. GSQ resamples fresh Gumbel noise g on every forward pass; averaging 4–16 micro-batches averages out that sampling noise, like taking multiple blurry photos and stacking them. Group scales get a smaller lr (5e-5) than logits (1e-4), and training runs 20 epochs on Llama (10 on Kimi) over 4096 × 4096-token sequences while τ linearly anneals 2 → 0.05.

007LESSON 7~10 min

Same File, Better Brain

Think of a backpack with six pockets and a strict weight limit. Some pockets hold fragile glasses — they need padding. Others hold socks — they can be squashed. Same total weight, very different result depending on where you put the protection.

Model weights are like that. Papers have measured it: not every layer hurts the same when you squeeze it. The attention Q/K and the MLP gate are usually picky; a down_proj can be more forgiving. So instead of giving every layer the same 2 bits or 3 bits, you can mix.

GSQ makes that mix practical because its output is plain scalar quantization — one scale s for each group of 128 weights, no codebooks, no special decoder. In the paper that mix is found by a search (called RCO): try combinations of 2-bit and 3-bit per layer until the average hits a target like

$$\text{average bpp} = \frac{\text{bits for layer}_1 + \dots + \text{bits for layer}_N}{N}$$

Say 6 toy layers at [3,2,3,2,2,2] give 14.2 / 6 ≈ 2.37 bpp. Another mix at about 2.62 bpp keeps a bit more quality. Both still run on the same fast scalar kernels (like Humming kernels with vLLM) — the file is just normal quantized weights, just different counts per layer. That's the first idea of this lesson: spend bits where they matter most.

Non-uniform = per-layer choice of 2 vs 3 bits to hit an average. No new format, just a smarter budget.

The second idea is even more down-to-earth: people already ship quantized models as GGUF K-Quant files — the Q2_K and Q3_K_M files you download for llama.cpp and Ollama. GSQ can start from one of those public files instead of from scratch.

How? It reads the current discrete pick for each weight — the index already stored in the GGUF file — and treats it as the starting point j⁰. If the little decision has at most 4 options (like ternary or 2-bit groups), GSQ puts full Gumbel-Softmax logits on all 4. If the decision set is bigger, it uses the same local nudge from the last lesson: learn only a shift δ ∈ {-2,-1,0,1,2} around j⁰, with 5 logits, and clip to stay in range. It jointly tunes those logits and the per-group scales on reconstruction loss, then snaps each soft choice back to a hard integer and writes that integer back into the same GGUF layout.

Nothing in the deployment changes. The file name, the unpacking code, the llama.cpp stack all stay identical — only the numbers inside got better.

On Qwen3-8B this polish matters a lot. The public Unsloth Q2_K averages 50.03 across AIME25, GPQA Diamond and MMLU-Pro; after GSQ the same Q2_K file scores 56.28 — a gain of 6.25 points — and on MMLU-Pro it generates fewer tokens, about 48.78M → 39.69M, so reasoning also gets more efficient. The milder Q3_K_M goes 60.52 → 61.61. On the smaller Qwen3-4B the same Q2_K jumps 35.01 → 48.34. The paper shows similar wins by mixing before polishing; the key point is compatibility: you keep the ecosystem.

Where the paper goes further: Appendix E details the full K-Quant parametrisation (scales, super-blocks, and K-Quant's own internal grouping), the exact RCO search procedure for mixed bit-widths, and extra Qwen3-4B tables. The course simplifies this to “pick per-layer 2 vs 3 bits and re-optimise shifts inside the same file”.

008LESSON 8~10 min

Does Scalar Catch Vector?

So does a well-tuned simple method actually catch the fancy ones? The answer is measured in two numbers the paper keeps side by side: average accuracy on five zero-shot tasks, and bits per parameter.

Accuracy here is just the mean of five small puzzles: ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, PIQA and WinoGrande. The paper quantizes every layer except embeddings to a target $bpp$ and reports $\text{average accuracy} = \frac{1}{5}\sum \text{task score}$. Lower $bpp$ means a smaller file: $3.13$ $bpp$ is about $5\times$ smaller than $16$ $bpp$, $2.13$ $bpp$ about $7.5\times$ smaller.

Key result: at $2$ bits, GSQ almost erases years of scalar-vs-vector gap. On Llama-3.1-8B it scores $68.55\%$, only $1.33$ points behind the best vector method QTIP at $69.88\%$, but $+4.76$ points ahead of the best old scalar EfficientQAT at $63.79\%$. On the larger Llama-3.1-70B it scores $75.57\%$, $1.68$ points behind QTIP $77.25\%$ and $+4.14$ ahead of EfficientQAT. Older scalars GPTQ and QuIP collapse to $37\text{\u2013}39\%$ there.

At $3$ bits the story is even tighter. With a symmetric group-$128$ grid and no zero-point — strictly less freedom than the baselines, which use asymmetric grids with a learned offset — GSQ reaches $72.32\%$ on 8B versus QTIP $72.81\%$, and $77.99\%$ on 70B versus QTIP $78.17\%$. In plain words: essentially tied, with the simpler format that already has fast kernels.

The paper also shows how far you can push this. A ternary model at only $1.58$ bits still gets $63.44\%$ on 8B, already beating every old scalar even when they get a full $2$ bits. And by giving sensitive layers $3$ bits and robust layers $2$ bits, a mixed average of $2.37$ $bpp$ keeps $69.44\%$ on 8B — most of the $3$-bit quality for much less size. That mixing is possible exactly because GSQ stays in the standard scalar format.

Accuracy alone does not explain why this matters. Vector methods save memory but, as the paper notes, give only small speedups over $BF16$ because their decoding is complex. Scalar GSQ plugs straight into existing Humming kernels in vLLM on NVIDIA L40s: $3.13$ $bpp$ runs at $289.6$ tokens per second per GPU ($4.80\times$ over $BF16$ at $60.3$), $2.37$ $bpp$ at $5.46\times$, and uniform $2$-bit at $374.0$ TPS/GPU ($6.20\times$). Same idea scales to huge Mixture-of-Experts: Kimi-K2.5 at $2$-bit keeps AIME25 $95.33\% \rightarrow 93.00\%$ and even improves LiveCodeBench $61.37\% \rightarrow 69.37\%$, with the GPQA drop linked in the paper to math-heavy calibration data.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

weight / parameter

A trained number inside the model. Llama-3.1-8B has 8 billion weights, Llama-3.1-70B has 70 billion.

quantization

Approximating numbers with fewer bits to save memory and make models faster. Weight-only quantization only shrinks weights.

bpp (bits per parameter)

Bits per parameter. 16 = full precision, 3 = about 5x smaller, 2 = 8x smaller, 1.58 = ternary.

quantization grid

The allowed rounding values, e.g. ternary grid {-1,0,1} scaled by s, or 2-bit grid {-2,-1,0,1} scaled by s.

per-group scale

A single number s multiplied to a whole group of quantized values (GSQ uses group size 128, row-wise, symmetric, no zero-point).

scalar quantization

Simple rounding: each weight independently to one grid point. Opposite of vector quantization which codes groups of weights together.

Gumbel-Softmax relaxation

A method that makes a discrete choice (e.g. pick grid point) differentiable by mixing all choices with probabilities from logits + Gumbel noise, controlled by temperature τ.

logits

Trainable scores for each candidate grid value. After softmax they become probabilities p = softmax((logit + noise)/τ).

temperature τ / annealing

Controls sharpness of the soft mix. High τ = soft average over many grid points, gradients flow. Low τ → annealed → collapses to one hard grid point.

ternary quantization

1.58-bit quantization with 3 levels. GSQ writes it as s · m ⊙ b where m in {0,1} is a mask and b in {-1,1} is a sign.

GPTQ initialization

An existing, widely-used post-training scalar quantizer. GSQ warm-starts its logits from the GPTQ solution (+1 if GPTQ ≠0, -1 if 0) plus Gaussian noise σ_init·(ε + α·l_GPTQ).

local-shift formulation

For b>2, instead of learning 2^b logits per weight (8 for 3-bit, 16 for 4-bit), only learn 5 logits for shifts δ in {-2,-1,0,1,2} around the initial GPTQ grid index: j = clip(j⁰+δ,1,2^b).

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)

Large model where most parameters are in many experts (e.g. Kimi-K2.5, ~1 trillion parameters, 4.5 bpp native, 384 experts).

GGUF K-Quants

File formats used by llama.cpp/Ollama for local deployment. GSQ can start from a public Q2_K or Q3_K_M checkpoint, improve its discrete assignments, and project back to the exact same format.

layer/block reconstruction error

Reconstruction loss || f(x; bar_w) - f(x; w) ||²_F . Measures how much the quantized layer/block output differs from full-precision output on calibration data.
