001Lesson 1 · The mystery~10 min

Do Different Eyes See the Same World?

Imagine five people watching shadows on a cave wall. One sees them as pictures, another hears them described in words. They never talk to each other. Would they still end up with the same picture of what's casting the shadows?

This is Plato's old question, turned into an AI question. Modern vision models learn from pixels. Language models learn from text. They are trained separately, on different data, with different jobs. Yet in 2024, researchers Huh and colleagues noticed something strange: as models got bigger, their inner maps — the way they arrange ideas close together or far apart — started to match.

They called it the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, or PRH: the idea that different models are converging toward one shared, “platonic” map of the real world.

The surprise isn't that a vision model learns about images. It's that a text-only model, which never saw a pixel, seems to arrange the ideas of “dog,” “wolf,” and “chair” in a geometry surprisingly similar to a vision model that never read a word.

That observation was exciting, but it was just an observation. No proof said it had to happen. No rule told you when it would fail. And the only recipe it offered was “make everything bigger.” Bigger models, bigger datasets.

The 2026 paper by Balestriero and LeCun asks a sharper question: when do models trained separately on completely different senses provably learn the same representation? And can we make them line up without needing a 70-billion-parameter giant?

Their answer works for any number of senses — images, video, text, audio, even actions — and needs only one idea we will unpack in this course: it all depends on what you tell the model counts as “the same thing.” Those are called positive pairs. Change the pairs, and you change everything. Get them right, and agreement becomes a theorem.

002Lesson 2 · The toolkit~12 min

How Machines Learn to See Alike: Embeddings & JEPAs

To make a computer "see," you give it a map. Every thing it sees becomes a point on that map — a list of numbers called an embedding. Think of it like GPS coordinates, but with k numbers instead of two. Close points should mean similar things. The paper asks: when do two separate machines — one that sees photos, one that reads captions — end up drawing the same map?

Start with the ingredients the paper defines. The world is just a finite set of hidden states z — like "a cat on a couch" or "a bike by a wall" — each with a chance p(z) of appearing. A modality m is a sensor that renders that hidden state as something we can record: x = gm(z). For photos the rendering is pixels; for language it's tokens. The paper first assumes this rendering is injective: every different z gets a different x. That sounds technical, but it just means the rendering is a coordinate change — like writing the same address in English or in French. Nothing is lost.

Learning needs pairs. A positive pair is two inputs the model is told "these should mean the same thing." All those pair choices make a distribution πm. Its table Π says how likely each pair (z, z') is. Rescale it for fairness and you get the paper's normalized graph:

$$K_{\pi}= D^{-1/2}\,\Pi\,D^{-1/2}$$

Here D just holds p(z) on its diagonal. Kπ is a graph drawn on the world: thick lines where positives often link two states, thin lines where they don't. That graph has eigenvalues 1 = μ0 ≥ μ1 ≥ …. The closer μ1 is to 1, the more the graph holds together.

A JEPA learns an encoder f that turns each x into an embedding f(x). Its loss is the paper's Equation 1:

$$\mathcal{L}_{m}(f)= \mathbb{E}_{(z,z')\sim \pi_{m}}\, \ell\big(f(g_{m}(z)), f(g_{m}(z'))\big) + \Omega(f)$$

ℓ pulls the two embeddings of a positive pair together — small when they are close. Ω is the anti-collapse term: without it, the easiest way to make all pairs close is to put every point at the same spot. Ω forbids that by forcing embeddings to stay spread out and centered. Both terms are rotation-invariant: turning every embedding with the same rigid rotation doesn't change the loss.

Finally, how do we tell if two machines drew the same map? The paper uses two checks. Mutual k-NN asks: without fitting anything, do an image and its true caption share the same neighbors? Chance is 10/N — about 0.01 for N=1000. Retrieval after rotation fits one best rigid turn (an orthogonal map found by Procrustes) on a few validation pairs, then asks: is the true caption ranked #1 among test captions? Chance there is 0.1%.

Injective rendering = same problem in different coordinates. So everything about learning comes down to what your positive pairs link — the graph you draw on the hidden world.

003Lesson 3 · The theorem~14 min

Good Views Make Agreement a Theorem

Imagine two students who never meet. One learns only from photos, the other only from captions. Each is told which pairs "belong together" — its positive pairs. If they are told the same pairs about the same world, should they end up with the same map, just turned differently?

The paper says yes — and proves it. First, a simple lemma: an injective rendering — think one-to-one translator like $x = g_m(z)$ — only changes coordinates. Photos and captions may look totally different, but the learning problem written on world states $z$ is the same:

$$\mathcal{L}_m(f)=\mathcal{L}_{\pi}(f\circ g_m)$$

In words: if $f$ is your encoder and $g_m$ is how world state $z$ becomes an image or a text, then training on images with pairs $\pi$ is the same as training on world states with pairs $\pi$, just seen through different glasses.

Theorem 1 — Platonic Representation Theorem (plain English). If every modality uses the same positive-pair distribution $\pi_1=\dots=\pi_M=\pi$ and the best solutions are unique up to a rotation, then there is one world embedding $h^\star(z)$ and each modality learns it up to its own rotation: $$f_m^\star(g_m(z)) = Q_m\,h^\star(z)$$ Any two modalities agree up to a single rotation $Q_m Q_{m'}^\top$. Same world kernel $\to$ same map, turned.

What does a JEPA actually learn? For the clean math case the paper analyzes — squared distance $\ell(a,b)=\|a-b\|^2$ and whitening ($E_p[h]=0$, $E_p[hh^\top]=I_k$, meaning centered with unit spread in every direction so embeddings cannot all collapse to a point) — it learns the top eigenvectors of the graph that positives draw on the world.

Call $\Pi$ the matrix of $\pi(z,z')$, $D=\text{diag}(p)$ the world frequencies, and $K_{\pi}=D^{-1/2}\Pi D^{-1/2}$ the normalized graph, with eigenvalues $1=\mu_0\ge \mu_1\ge \dots$. Then:

$$\min \mathcal{L}_{\pi}=2\sum_{i=1}^k (1-\mu_i)\quad\text{and minimizers are }Q\,\Phi_{\pi}(z),\;\;\Phi_{\pi}(z)=\frac{(v_1(z),\dots,v_k(z))}{\sqrt{p(z)}}$$

That is, take the top $k$ eigenvectors $v_1\dots v_k$ of $K_{\pi}$, scale by $1/\sqrt{p(z)}$, you get the optimal $k$-dimensional world map. Any rotation $Q$ (a turn that keeps distances) is equally good — and if there is a spectral gap $\mu_k > \mu_{k+1}$, that is the only freedom. No gap, no uniqueness: the $k$-th and $(k+1)$-th directions tie and can mix.

Go deeper: why the gap matters. Think of eigenvalues as "how strongly connected" each direction is in the positive graph. A big drop after $k$ means the top-$k$ subspace is well separated and stable. A tiny gap means a small change in pairs could swap which directions are in the top $k$.

Real life pairs differ a little between modalities. The paper bounds that damage (Theorem 2, Eq. 3): after the best rotation, the root-mean-square mismatch is at most

$$\min_Q \Big(E_p\|\Phi_1(z)-Q\Phi_2(z)\|^2\Big)^{1/2} \le \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\delta}\min\big(\sqrt{k}\|K_{\pi_1}-K_{\pi_2}\|_2,\;\|K_{\pi_1}-K_{\pi_2}\|_F\big)$$

where $\delta=\mu_k-\mu_{k+1}>0$ is that gap on modality 2, and $\|\cdot\|$ measures how different the two graphs are. Same idea: mismatch of kernels divided by protection of gap. Leading directions with big gaps are protected; the noisy tail is not.

Finally, how many paired examples do you need to find that one rotation $Q$? Not many — and not zero. With whitening, the average spread tells you nothing about $Q$ (any rotation of a whitened cloud is still whitened). But $k$ paired world states whose embeddings span all $k$ dimensions are enough to pin down $Q$ exactly by orthogonal Procrustes — the rotation minimizing squared error on those pairs.

So: shared positives $\to$ same spectral map up to one rotation per modality (if gap $>0$), different positives $\to$ bounded misalignment, and $k$ pairs $\to$ found rotation. Try the lab to see all three live.

004Lesson 4 · When views go wrong~14 min

When Views Go Bad: The Nuisance Trap

So far “same positives = same map up to one rotation” sounded like a free lunch. This lesson is the catch: each observation is not just the world state. A photo has a viewpoint and lighting. A caption has word choice and style. The paper calls that extra stuff nuisance ν — details that belong to the sensor, not the world.

Write an observation as x = gm(z, ν): world state z plus its own nuisance ν. Two paired image+caption observations of the same scene share z but not ν — your caption’s wording never tells you the photographer’s camera angle. An embedding that wastes dimensions on ν can never be fixed by a rotation.

To make this precise, take a centered, whitened encoder f (mean 0, variance 1 in every direction). Let Cf = Cov( E[ f(x) | z ] ) be how much of the embedding the world state alone explains. Then

$$ \rho(f) = \frac{\text{tr}\,C_f^{1/2}}{k} \in [0,1] $$

is the scene-explained fraction. ρ=1 means f(x) depends only on z; ρ=0.6 means 40% of its dimensions track nuisance. Theorem 3 is sobering:

$$ \min_{Q\in O(k)} \mathbb{E}\,\|Q f_1(x_1)-f_2(x_2)\|^2 \ge 2k\,(1-\min_m \rho(f_m)) $$

Every dimension you spend on your modality’s nuisance adds 2 to the best-possible error after the best rotation. The other modality cannot rescue you.

Who spends dimensions on nuisance? Good-view JEPAs do not. Their positives are (g_m(z,ν), g_m(z',ν')) with independent nuisances and world-related (z,z'). Their kernel is Π \otimes q q^\top and, if the spectrum has a gap μ_k > \max(μ_{k+1},0), they learn a function of z alone: ρ=1. Bad-view JEPAs do. They make two corruptions of one observation, \tilde x, \tilde x' \sim \Gamma_m(\cdot|x), which share its ν.

Write corruption as separate knobs on state and nuisance: Γ_m = A \otimes B_m. Let α_k be how well two corruptions keep the world’s k-th direction, and β_1 how well they keep the best nuisance direction. The corrupted kernel’s eigenvalues are products α_i β_j. Proposition 3: if β_1 > α_k, the top k eigenvalues include a nuisance one — the encoder steals r\ge 1 of its k slots for nuisance, and alignment error with any whitened encoder of the other modality is at least 2r. Two crops that keep viewpoint better than they keep the scene’s fine distinction do exactly this.

More generally, any positives-on-observations law W_m induces a world kernel π_{W_m}: probability that a positive links an observation of z to one of z'. Good views induce the world’s own π by design. Bad views induce whatever their corruptions plus your data happen to connect. Theorem 4 says each JEPA sits within τ_m of its induced kernel’s spectral embedding, and two modalities agree (up to τ_1+τ_2) exactly as those two induced kernels agree. For good views τ_m=0; for bad views it measures how much positives mix state with nuisance.

Data and views interact. On finitely many samples, bad positives only join samples whose corruptions overlap. Weak views fragment each world state into tiny islands; you need dense samples to chain overlaps through the state. In the paper’s synthetic world (five classes × six states, Figure 4) the required reach falls as Δ \propto \log N / N, the biggest gap between samples. Too-strong views connect across classes and cap alignment near 0.95 at any N. Good views need one sample per state and never spill.

Try it yourself below. First, steal a dimension by making β_1 > α_k and watch r and the 2r bound jump. Then sweep N and reach Δ for good vs. weak vs. strong bad views and see when fragments connect — and when they blur.

005Lesson 5 · Language models re-explained~12 min

Why Predicting the Next Word Is Wasteful

Imagine you learn about the world in two different ways. In one, your teacher shows you two different photos of the same dog park and says “these go together.” In the other, she shows you half a sentence and asks you to guess the next word. Both use the idea of “positive pairs” — inputs that should end up close in your mental map — but the pairs are not the same. The paper argues this choice matters more than scale.

This lesson is about the second way. Language models trained to predict the next token look very different from a JEPA, but the paper shows they are a JEPA in disguise.

$$ -\log\frac{e^{\langle h(c), w_y\rangle}}{\sum_v e^{\langle h(c), w_v\rangle}} = \underbrace{-\langle h(c), w_y\rangle}_{\text{prediction}} + \underbrace{\log\sum_v e^{\langle h(c), w_v\rangle}}_{\text{anti-collapse } A(h(c))} $$

Here $c$ is the context — the words seen so far — and $h(c)$ is its embedding, a list of $k$ numbers the model learns. Each possible next token $v$ has its own embedding $w_v$ from a table. $y$ is the true next token. The angle brackets $\langle h(c), w_y\rangle$ mean dot product: multiply the two lists element-wise and add up, large when they point the same way.

The right side splits the loss into two familiar JEPA parts. The first term $-\langle h(c), w_y\rangle$ pulls the context toward its true continuation — a prediction term. The second term $A(h(c)) = \log\sum_v e^{\langle h(c), w_v\rangle}$ pushes the context to consider all other tokens too — an anti-collapse term, the log-partition of the softmax. When you make the softmax very sharp (low temperature $t\to 0$), this becomes $\max_v \langle h, w_v\rangle - \langle h, w_y\rangle$, a margin that is zero only if the true token is already the highest-scoring one.

So the model has two encoders: $h$ for contexts and $w$ for tokens, with positives $(c,y)$ that live inside one document. At its best, $\langle h(c), w_v\rangle = \log p(v\mid c) + b(c)$, the log-probability of the next token plus a per-context shift. Crucially, this solution is only fixed up to any invertible linear map $\boldsymbol{G}$ — rotate and stretch $h$ one way and $w$ the opposite way and nothing changes. A whitened JEPA is fixed up to a pure rotation; next-token prediction is looser, which already makes alignment harder.

Why is that a bad view? A prefix and its continuation share their document’s nuisance: the author’s wording and style. As in Lesson 4, a view that keeps nuisance better than the world’s $k$-th direction will waste dimensions on it. The paper’s heuristic prediction is that next-token encoders spend capacity on style, so they can only align with vision partly, and mostly by seeing a huge amount of text.

Key distinction: good views join all descriptions of a scene at once and never leave it. Next-token positives are narrow bridges within scenes plus leaks across them.

There is a second kind of positive hidden here. Because contexts followed by the same token gather around that token vector $w_y$ — the “neural collapse” effect — any two contexts that share a next token become implicit positives. A single token rarely describes a whole scene, so linking all captions of one scene takes long chains of shared continuations and very dense data. Frequent continuations like “and,” “the,” or “over” also join contexts that have nothing to do with each other, spilling across scenes whatever the amount of data.

Good views fix the world’s own connection pattern by design. Next-token prediction has to discover it through those narrow, leaky chains. That is the paper’s reading of why language models need scale: their positives are weak.

Go deeper: At the optimum, $h$ is identified only up to $\mathrm{GL}(k)$, not $\mathrm{O}(k)$, so even with shared positives the rotation story of Theorem 1 does not directly apply — the paper presents this as a tested prediction, not a corollary.

006Lesson 6 · Views + data law~12 min

More Data or Better Views? A Predictable Recipe

We know which views are better — two different descriptions of the same scene beats two cut-ups of one sentence. Now the practical question: if you have a budget, should you buy more data, more training steps, or better views?

This lesson makes that trade predictable. The paper's team trained on web images from Recap-DataComp. Each web image comes with three texts: one alt-text (often messy) and two cleaner recaptions written by an image captioner. From those they trained the same small text model (BERT-S, 33M parameters) against a frozen vision model (DINOv2 ViT-B). What changed across runs was only three numbers:

  • M = samples seen: how many training examples the model has looked at, including repeats. 3M to 133M in the test.
  • N = unique images: how many different pictures the texts came from. 0.4M to about 51M.
  • q = fraction of good views: when the model picks a positive pair, how often does it get two texts from the same image's scene (good) versus two crops of one single text (bad). q = 0 means all bad, q = 1 means all good.

Across 45 different (q, M, N) settings they saw a simple pattern. At any fixed q, retrieval accuracy (R@1 — how often the true caption is ranked #1 after one rotation) grows linearly with the logarithm of M. In other words, you need to double, then double again, for steady gains. But the slope depends on q: more good views means a steeper line. Unique images N helped only weakly.

$$ \mathrm{R@1}= a + d\log M + b\log\left(1+ \frac{q^{\alpha} M^{\beta} N^{\gamma}}{c}\right) $$

That's the scaling law the paper found (Eq. 7). Read it as two parts. The first, $a + d\log M$, is a bad-view floor: even with q = 0 you improve slowly just by seeing more. The second, $b\log(1+ \dots)$, is the good-view bonus: it only turns on when you have some q, and then it grows with more samples M and more images N, but with exponents that say q matters most. The letters $a,b,c,d,\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ are just fitted constants — the shape is what matters.

How good is it? It fit the 45 training cells with error (RMSE) 0.007. Then it predicted 85 brand-new runs before they were trained — random choices of q and M on 10.2M images — with error 0.0093. That's about as close as training the same run twice with a different random seed (0.0096). For a messy real-world measurement, that's remarkably predictable.

The law also tells you what you can trade. Follow a line of constant R@1 and you get $q \propto M^{-0.30}$: to keep the same score, you can make up for fewer good views by training longer, but you need a lot more samples — cutting q by half needs $2^{1/0.30} \approx 10\times$ more M going the other way. Bad views alone barely benefit from more unique images: from 0.4M to 50.9M images with q = 0, WIT alignment stayed around 0.054–0.057, while with good views it rose from 0.090 to 0.115.

Key insight: views and data together set what is learned. Good views don't just add a constant — they change how fast more data helps.

Where does it break? At 320M samples — four times farther than it was fitted, 31 passes over the same 10.2M images — the law over-predicted good-view runs (predicting 0.455, getting 0.418 at q = 1). It has no term for "you're seeing the same images again and again." Bad-view runs still matched (0.338 vs 0.337 predicted). The exponents are also uncertain, and the law is a summary of this one recipe, not a theorem like Lesson 3.

In the lab below, try the recipe yourself. Move the q and M sliders and watch the predicted R@1 update on the log-M plot — compare the flat bad-view line to the steep good-view line, and see how many bad-view samples you'd need to match a modest amount of good views.

007Lesson 7 · Results at scale~14 min

Small but Smart: Beating 70-Billion-Parameter Giants

Think of two students memorizing for the same quiz. One copy-pastes the textbook word-for-word. The other rewrites each idea in her own words, then checks that the rewrites still match. Both work hard — but the second student will recognize the idea in a new question, while the first gets stuck on wording.

That is exactly what the paper tested on Flickr. They kept everything the same — the same 29,000 training images, the same captions, the same small BERT text encoder — and only changed the positives, the pairs told to get similar embeddings:

  • Good-view JEPA: 8 views each taken from a different caption of the same image, then trimmed and lightly masked.
  • Bad-view JEPA: 8 masked spans taken from the same single caption (two corruptions of one observation).
  • Next-token: a causal BERT predicting the next word in one caption.

On 1,000 held-out Flickr image→caption pairs, they fit one rigid rotation (the only map allowed, learned on validation pairs) and asked: is the true caption ranked #1? Chance is 0.1%. Results: good-view $R@1 = 0.044$, bad-view $0.027$, next-token $0.021$. The gaps are several times the wobble between random seeds. Even giving next-token two captions of the same image as input barely moved it ($0.023$).

Why? Remember from Lesson 4 the scene-explained fraction $\rho(f) = \mathrm{tr}\,C_f^{1/2}/k$, the share of the $k$ embedding directions that depend on the world state $z$, not on wording $\nu$. For the top $k=32$ whitened directions, $\rho$ was $0.804$ for good-view, $0.655$ for bad-view, $0.608$ for next-token. Theorem 3 turns that into a hard lower bound on the best-rotation error: $\min_Q \mathbb{E}\|Qf_1(x_1)-f_2(x_2)\|^2 \ge 2k(1-\min\rho)$. That gives bounds $12.6$, $22.1$ and $25.1$ (chance would be $2k=64$). The measured errors $33.5$, $39.4$ and $40.3$ respect them and follow the same order — bad views literally waste dimensions on wording.

At web scale the same pattern explodes. On the PRH paper's WIT-1024 benchmark (1,024 Wikipedia image-text pairs, no model trained on them, mutual 10-nearest-neighbors, chance $0.01$), a 109M-parameter good-view JEPA reached $0.184$, beating Qwen2.5-72B at $0.154$ and CLIP's text encoder at $0.124$. The lead held with every vision backbone (DINOv2-S/B/L/g, CLIP ViT-H, MAE, I-JEPA), with CKA instead of mutual-NN, and on 5,000 COCO pairs.

The most striking controlled comparison used identical web texts — each image's alt-text plus two LLaVA recaptions from Recap-DataComp. With $21.6$ million training samples, the good-view JEPA hit WIT $0.109$. Next-token prediction on the exact same corpus needed $900$ million samples to hit $0.111$. That's $42\times$ fewer samples ($14\times$ fewer text tokens, $435\times$ fewer unique images) for the same alignment. With those $900$ million samples, good-view rose to $0.184$.

So a 109M text JEPA that saw $116$ billion tokens beat every language model up to $72$ billion parameters trained on $15$–$18$ trillion tokens — over $600\times$ more parameters and over $100\times$ more tokens — and beat CLIP text trained on $400$ million image-text pairs.

Two caveats make the story honest, not weaker. One of four good-view seeds at 940M images scored only $0.121$ on WIT (average over four was $0.168$, still above every LM). And two of the three texts per image were written by a captioner that itself saw images through CLIP ViT-L/14 — some image-text signal is distilled into the data. Also, $43$ of the $1{,}024$ WIT captions appear verbatim among alt-texts; removing them changes every model by at most $0.002$. Crucially, all view comparisons above used identical texts, so those caveats can't explain why good beats bad.

In the lab, test efficiency, not just the top score. Drag training samples from $21$M to $900$M and watch good-view pull away from next-token. Switch vision encoders — DINOv2, CLIP-H, MAE — and hover bubbles to see $0.184$ vs $0.154$ and how much bigger the giants had to be to lose.

008Lesson 8 · Build with it~12 min

One Rotation Is Enough: Captioning Without Training Together

Training a vision model and a language model together on millions of image–caption pairs works, but it’s expensive. The Platonic result says you may not need to. If each side already maps its modality to the same world shape — just turned — then one rotation is the only bridge you have to fit.

The paper turns that into a captioner with no joint training at all. Image encoders (frozen DINOv2 and DINOv3) and a text JEPA are trained completely separately. A small decoder — a Qwen2.5-1.5B model — is then trained on text alone: it learns to rebuild a caption from the frozen text-JEPA embedding of that caption. Nothing in the decoder ever sees a pixel.

The catch is a mismatch in crispness. A real caption embedding and its image embedding don’t overlap perfectly; after the best rotation their average cosine is $c \approx 0.3$ in the paper’s setup. If you train the decoder on perfect embeddings, it expects perfection and stumbles on blurry image embeddings. So the decoder is trained on noisy embeddings.

$$\text{noisy embedding} = \text{clean embedding} + \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\cos] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^2}}$$

Matching the two cosines gives the noise to use: set $(1+\sigma^2)^{-1/2} = c$, so $$\sigma = (c^{-2}-1)^{1/2} \approx 3$$ when $c\approx 0.3$. In plain words: make the fuzzy text embedding about as fuzzy as a real image is distant from its caption. On Flickr that rule peaks — not tuned — and the captioner reaches CIDEr $0.431$ (from $0.5$ would be near-human on this scale). On the 5,000 COCO test images, the same rotation fitted only on Flickr pairs reaches $0.668$, and with the stronger frozen encoders (DINOv2-g + DINOv3-L concatenated), the pretrained decoder, and picking the consensus of 32 samples, $0.838$.

Why insist on a rotation? An unrestricted linear map or a two-layer MLP fits the 30,000 Flickr training pairs a little better on Flickr (CIDEr $0.531$ vs $0.504$ for the rotation) but drops badly on COCO ($0.635$ vs $0.802$). A rotation can only turn shared geometry; it can’t memorize Flickr style. That’s what makes it transfer.

The same trick works for video with zero video training. Average the rotated embeddings of 8 frames of a clip and decode the mean — the average washes out what changes frame to frame, just like good views wash out nuisance. On MSR-VTT it reaches CIDEr $0.246$ and retrieves the right clip first $16.5\%$ of the time (CLIP trained jointly on 400M pairs gets $31.2\%$).

And you can go one step further: no pairs at all. Using an off-the-shelf geometry-matching method — cluster each cloud and refine an orthogonal map by optimal transport — the 109M good-view JEPA is alignable from unpaired images and captions alone. On COCO it gets FOSCTTM $0.018$ (0 is perfect, 0.5 chance) and zero-shot $86.0\%$ on CIFAR-10, $67.2\%$ on CIFAR-100 top-5, $64.4\%$ on ImageNet-100 — beating every zero-shot accuracy the benchmark had reported up to 8B parameters. The same method with next-token prediction on the identical texts collapses ($0.287$, $25.5\%$). On Flickr it retrieves the right caption first for a median $16.4\%$ with zero pairs, versus at most $0.3\%$ for CLIP’s text encoder or the bad-view models. When the geometry matches, the turn can be found without a single labeled pair.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH)

The idea that models trained on different senses — like images and text — will end up learning the same inner map of the world as they get bigger. This paper turns it from a guess into a proven theorem for certain models.

Modality & Rendering

A way of sensing the world: images, text, video, audio, actions are different modalities. The paper treats each as a different 'rendering' of the same underlying world states.

Embedding & Embedding space

A list of numbers a model uses to describe something, like coordinates on a map. Similar things should have close coordinates. Size is k = number of dimensions.

JEPA

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture. A model that learns by pulling the embeddings of its 'positive pairs' together, while an anti-collapse term stops all embeddings from collapsing to one point.

Positive pair

Two inputs the model is told should mean the same thing and should get similar embeddings. Which pairs you choose is the whole recipe.

Good views

Positives that are two separate observations of the same scene — e.g., two different photos of the same place, or two different captions of the same image. They share only world information.

Bad views

Positives that are two corruptions of one single observation — e.g., two crops of the same photo, two masked versions of the same sentence, or a prefix + its continuation. They share the wording/style of that one observation too.

Nuisance

Extra details that belong to the sensor, not the world: the viewpoint of a photo, the word choice of a caption, an author's style. No rotation can match nuisance across modalities.

Rotation (orthogonal map)

A rigid turn (and possibly flip) of space that keeps all distances and angles the same. The paper proves matching modalities needs at most one such turn: f_m*(g_m(z)) = Q_m h*(z).

Mutual 10-NN & R@1

Score that checks if an image and its caption share neighbors without fitting any map. Chance is 10/N = 0.01 for N=1000. Retrieval R@1 is how often the true caption is ranked #1 after fitting one rotation on validation pairs (chance 0.1%).

Next-token prediction

Training by guessing the next token (word piece) given previous ones using a softmax. The paper shows this has the same structure as a bad-view JEPA: positives are (prefix, continuation) inside one document.

Scaling law

Formula that predicts alignment from training: R@1 = a + d log M + b log(1 + q^α M^β N^γ /c), where M=samples seen, N=unique images, q=fraction of good views. It predicted 85 new runs within seed noise (.0093 error).
