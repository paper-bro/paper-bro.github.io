001Lesson 1~6 min

Can a Computer Imagine a Photo from Static?

Can a computer imagine a brand-new photo — one no camera ever took? A generative model tries to do exactly that. Think of all real photos as coming from some hidden distribution q(x0) — the chance of seeing any particular image x0. The model wants to learn its own distribution p(x0) that is close to q, so that when we sample a new x0 from p, it looks real but isn't a copy.

Before 2020 researchers had several families for this, each with trade-offs. GANs pitted a generator against a discriminator and made sharp images in one shot, but could be unstable. Autoregressive models built images pixel-by-pixel. Flows and VAEs gave nice likelihoods but trickier samples. Energy-based and score-matching models also made striking images, yet sampling was hard.

This paper — Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models by Jonathan Ho and colleagues — tried a different idea. Take a photo, and slowly add tiny amounts of Gaussian noise for 1,000 steps until it becomes pure static. That's the forward process — a Markov chain where each step depends only on the last. Then train another Markov chain going the other way: start from pure static and learn to remove a little noise at each step. The training uses variational inference, a way to bound how well p matches q.

The big claim: this gradual denoising chain — simple Gaussian steps learned with a U-Net — can make high-quality photos, sometimes better than the famous GANs of the day.

How much better? On tiny 32×32 unconditional CIFAR-10 images (no class labels given) their best model got an FID of 3.17 — FID is low-is-better, like a distance to real photos — and 5.24 when compared to the test set, plus an Inception Score of 9.46 (high-is-better, meaning sharp and diverse). Both beat most published models then, even conditional ones. On large 256×256 LSUN churches and bedrooms, their samples looked similar to ProgressiveGAN, the GAN to beat at that scale.

Why does going slow help? Trying to clean heavy static in one giant leap is very hard — the network has to guess too much at once. Taking 1,000 tiny, easy denoising steps is learnable. In the lab below this is shown as a simplified analogy: a "one big jump" rescue stands in for a difficult one-shot generator, while "many small steps" stands in for diffusion.

002Lesson 2~6 min

Quick Background: Pictures as Numbers with a Shake of Noise

To a computer, a photo isn't magic — it's just numbers. In the paper, every pixel starts as an integer from 0 (black) to 255 (white). The authors immediately stretch that range to -1 to 1:

$$x_{scaled}=\frac{x}{127.5}-1$$

So 0 becomes -1, 127 becomes about 0 (middle gray), 255 becomes 1. Why bother? Because the "pure static" they want to end with is the famous bell-curve noise called $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ — a standard Gaussian centered at 0 with spread 1. If photos live on the same -1 to 1 scale, that static is the same size as the photos. The neural network then always sees inputs on a consistent scale.

What's a Gaussian anyway?

Imagine you spin a wheel that picks a random number, but not equally. It prefers numbers near some center. $\mathcal{N}(\text{mean}, \text{variance}\cdot I)$ means: for each pixel, independently pick from a bell curve centered at mean with width set by variance.

Small variance = tall narrow bell = "almost always near the mean." Large variance = short wide bell = "could be far away." Writing $\beta_t I$ just means "same $\beta_t$ variance for every pixel, and pixels don't correlate."

Key idea: the forward chain is fixed — no learning. It is defined as a Markov chain: the next noisy version depends only on the current one, not the whole history. In symbols from the paper (Eq. 2):
$$q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}|\mathbf{x}_0)=\prod_{t=1}^{T} q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}),\qquad q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1})= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\, \beta_t I)$$
In plain English: to get the next frame, shrink the current image a tiny bit by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ and then add a little Gaussian noise with variance $\beta_t$.

Why shrink by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$?

If you only added noise, the numbers would grow bigger and bigger — like turning up volume every step. The $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ factor gently pulls the image toward 0 just enough to keep the total energy constant. Think of it as: fade the photo by 1% while you add 1% static, so the picture stays the same overall brightness while getting grainier.

Because $\beta_t$ is chosen tiny (the paper will use $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$), this fade is very gentle: for $\beta_t=0.01$, $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\approx 0.995$. That smallness matters. When the noise per step is tiny, the reverse step — trying to undo it — is also a simple Gaussian. That lets the network be a simple "predict the mean of a Gaussian" model rather than something exotic. If you added a huge blast of noise at once, undoing it would be hard and not Gaussian at all.

003Lesson 3~8 min

The Forward March: From Photo to Pure Static

Imagine your favorite photo printed on sheer paper, then photocopied again and again — each copy a little dimmer and sprinkled with TV snow. After one copy you can still see everything. After 1,000 copies only snow is left.

That is the forward process. The paper writes it as one step:

$$ q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\, \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\, \beta_t \mathbf{I}\big) $$

This is just a recipe for one gentle nudge. \(\mathcal{N}(\text{mean}, \text{variance})\) means “pick each pixel from a bell curve.” The mean is the old image shrunk a tiny bit by \(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\), and the spread is \(\beta_t\). \(\beta_t\) is the variance schedule — how much new snow to add at step t. The chain is Markov: step t only looks at t-1.

Doing this 1,000 times sounds tedious. The clever part is you can jump straight to any time t without simulating the steps in between. Define

$$ \alpha_t = 1-\beta_t \qquad\text{and}\qquad \bar{\alpha}_t = \alpha_1 \cdot \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t $$

\(\bar{\alpha}_t\) is “how much original photo is still left after t steps.” Then the distribution of a noisy image given the clean start has a simple closed form (Eq. 4 in the paper):

$$ q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\, \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\big) $$

In plain code: \(\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\) where \({\boldsymbol\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) is pure static. When \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) is near 1 you see the photo; when it is near 0 you see noise.

Which schedule makes pure noise at the end? The authors fixed — not learned — a very gentle linear schedule: T=1000, with \(\beta_1 = 0.0001\) up to \(\beta_{1000}=0.02\). That is tiny compared to images scaled to [-1,1], which is exactly why the reverse can also be Gaussian. Multiply all those \(\alpha_t\) together and \(\bar{\alpha}_{1000}\) falls to almost zero. The amount of original signal left is negligible.

The paper checks this: L_T = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_T\mid\mathbf{x}_0)\,||\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})) \approx 10^{-5}\) bits per dimension at T=1000. In other words, \(\mathbf{x}_T\) is essentially indistinguishable from pure Gaussian static — so starting generation from \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) later is fair.

Try it below. Drag t from 0 to 1000 and watch the toy picture dissolve. The right plot shows \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) sliding from 1 to 0 as \(1-\bar{\alpha}_t\) grows. Switch to a constant large \(\beta\) and notice how quickly you would destroy everything — the paper’s slow ramp keeps early steps learnable.

Learning to Undo: Teaching the Machine to Denoise

We’ve turned a photo into pure static by adding a tiny shake of Gaussian noise 1000 times. Now comes the hard part: learning to undo it — to go from static back to a face.

Think of rewinding a movie of ink spreading in water. If the spreading steps are tiny, rewinding one frame is almost predictable. The paper does exactly that. It defines a reverse chain that starts from pure noise and takes 1000 small cleaning steps:

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T}) = p(\mathbf{x}_T)\,\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t),\quad p(\mathbf{x}_T)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),\quad p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\,{\boldsymbol\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t),\,{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t))$$

In words: $p(\mathbf{x}_T)$ is standard normal noise — every pixel drawn from a bell curve centered at 0. Each reverse step $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)$ is also a Gaussian — a bell curve. The network’s job is to choose its center ${\boldsymbol\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)$ and spread ${\boldsymbol\Sigma}_\theta$. Here $\theta$ just means “the network’s weights.”

But what should that center be? The paper gives us a perfect teacher to compare against — but only if we cheat and peek at the original photo $\mathbf{x}_0$. The forward process plus the original image tells us the true undo distribution:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\,\tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0),\,\tilde\beta_t\mathbf{I})$$

That says: if you know both the noisy image $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the clean original $\mathbf{x}_0$, the ideal previous step is a Gaussian whose mean is a weighted average:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t = \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}_{\text{weight on }\mathbf{x}_0}\mathbf{x}_0 + \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}_{\text{weight on }\mathbf{x}_t}\mathbf{x}_t,\quad \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t$$

Recall from Lesson 3: $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_1\cdots\alpha_t$ is how much signal remains after $t$ steps. Early in the chain ($t$ small, image still clean) the weight on $\mathbf{x}_t$ dominates — you barely need $\mathbf{x}_0$. Late ($t$ large, almost static) the weight on $\mathbf{x}_0$ grows — you need real knowledge of the photo to denoise correctly.

Training is then simple to state. We want our learned Gaussian $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)$ to match that ideal Gaussian $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)$. The paper rewrites the usual variational bound as:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\,D_{\rm KL}(q(\mathbf{x}_T|\mathbf{x}_0)\|p(\mathbf{x}_T)) + \sum_{t>1} D_{\rm KL}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)\|p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)) - \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0|\mathbf{x}_1)\Big]$$

$D_{\rm KL}$ is just a score for “how different are these two bell curves?” For Gaussians it has a closed formula. Minimizing $L$ pushes the network’s center ${\boldsymbol\mu}_\theta$ toward $\tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t$ at every $t$.

Two practical choices made this stable. First, the paper fixes the variance — ${\boldsymbol\Sigma}_\theta = \sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}$ with no learning — either $\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$ or $\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t$. Both gave similar results, and learning the variance made training unstable. Second, the network is shared across all $t$: one U-Net sees the timestep $t$ as an extra input.

That network matters. It’s a U-Net backbone like PixelCNN++ built on a Wide ResNet — an encoder that shrinks the image then a decoder that expands it, with skip connections. The paper uses group normalization, 2 residual blocks per resolution (4 resolutions for $32\times32$ images, 6 for $256\times256$), self-attention at the $16\times16$ feature map, and the Transformer’s sinusoidal position embedding to tell the network which $t$ it’s on. Same weights for all 1000 steps. That’s 35.7 million parameters for CIFAR10, 114 million for LSUN and CelebA-HQ ($256\times256$), and a large Bedroom variant with about 256 million.

The Simple Trick That Makes It Work

By lesson 4 we know the training goal: at every noise level t make our reverse Gaussian pθ(xt-1|xt) look like the true noisy posterior q(xt-1|xt,x0). That posterior is a Gaussian with mean tildeμt and we could just train a network to predict it.

The most direct loss is then just "how far is our guess?":

$$L_{t-1}=\mathbb{E}_q\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\tilde{\boldsymbol\mu}_t(\mathbf x_t,\mathbf x_0) - {\boldsymbol\mu}_{\theta}(\mathbf x_t,t)\big\|^2\right]+C \tag{8}$$

Here C doesn't depend on the network, σt2 is the fixed variance (the paper fixes it to βt or tildeβt — both worked about the same), and μθ is what the U-Net outputs. It looks simple: predict the mean.

So why not do that? You can — and the paper tried. Predicting tildeμt works okay if you train with the full math-heavy variational bound. But there's a clever rewrite that works much better in practice.

Remember the forward trick from Lesson 3 (Eq. 4): we can write any noisy image directly as

$$\mathbf x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,{\boldsymbol\epsilon},\qquad {\boldsymbol\epsilon}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I) \tag{4 reparam}$$

ε is just the exact static we added. Plug that into Eq. 8 and the ideal tildeμt becomes (1/\sqrt{αt} )( xt - βt/\sqrt{1-\barαt} \, ε ) (Eq. 10). In words: to denoise, you only need to know the noise ε that was added.

So the paper proposes: don't predict the mean, predict the noise. Set

$${\boldsymbol\mu}_{\theta}(\mathbf x_t,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf x_t,t)\right) \tag{11}$$

where εθ(xt,t) is a U-Net that takes the noisy image and t and guesses "what static was added?". Same power — it's just a different parameterization of μθ — but the loss now looks like:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf x_0,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}}\left[\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\,\big\|{\boldsymbol\epsilon} - {\boldsymbol\epsilon}_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\boldsymbol\epsilon},\,t)\big\|^2\right] \tag{12}$$

That's exactly denoising score-matching at many noise levels, and sampling with Eq. 11 looks like annealed Langevin dynamics — the paper's key connection. The ugly prefactor in front is just a per-t weight: huge when t is small (almost no noise, 1-\barαt near zero), tiny when t is large (very noisy).

The simplification

For the final model the authors simply drop that weight:

$$L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf x_0,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}}\,\big\|{\boldsymbol\epsilon} - {\boldsymbol\epsilon}_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\boldsymbol\epsilon},\,t)\big\|^2 \tag{14}$$

t is chosen uniformly 1...1000. No weighting, no σt. For t=1 this matches the discrete decoder Eq. 13 (an integral over the little 1/255 bin) approximated by the Gaussian density times bin width.

Why does ignoring math help? The weight in Eq. 12 forces the network to focus on easy steps — barely-noisy images at small t. L_{\text{simple}} down-weights those easy steps and spends capacity on hard, very-noisy steps at large t. Samples look much better, even though the true codelength (bits/dim) gets slightly worse.

The ablation in the paper shows it clearly: predicting ε with L_{\text{simple}} gives far better sample quality than predicting tildeμt with mean-squared error. Learning the variance Σθ even made training unstable. And training on the true weighted bound did give better lossless bits/dim, as expected, but worse FID — a tradeoff you'll explore next.

From Static Back to Picture

We have a photo, we turned it into static, and we trained a network to guess the noise at every level. Now we get to run the film backwards — start from pure static and watch a brand-new face appear.

The sampler the paper actually uses is short enough to write on one line. Call the static image $x_T$. For $t = T, T-1, \dots, 1$ you do:

$$ x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right) + \sigma_t\, z $$

Let's unpack it. $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \alpha_1\cdots\alpha_t$ are the same schedule from Lesson 3 ($T=1000$, $\beta_t$ linear $10^{-4}\to0.02$). $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ is the network: it looks at the noisy picture $x_t$ and guesses the noise $\epsilon$ that was added. The big fraction simply converts that noise-guess into a guess of the mean to step to — it's exactly Eq. 11 from Lesson 5. $z$ is fresh static, $z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, scaled by $\sigma_t$. The paper fixes $\sigma_t$ to $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ or $\sqrt{\tilde{\beta}_t}$ with $\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\beta_t$ — both gave similar results, and fixing it (not learning it) kept training stable. At the very last step $t=1$ you don't add $z$; you just display the mean $\mu_\theta(x_1,1)$ with no noise, and for discrete images the paper's decoder (Eq. 13) turns that into pixel bins.

This step looks like Langevin dynamics — a physics-style walk that rolls a little downhill toward more likely images (the $\epsilon_\theta$ term acts like a learned score) and shakes a little randomly ($\sigma_t z$). Many tiny learned steps beats one big jump.

You don't have to wait until $t=0$ to see what the model thinks. At any intermediate $x_t$ you can make a one-shot guess of the clean photo:

$$ \hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}} $$

This is Eq. 15. It just inverts the forward formula $x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon$. Replace the true $\epsilon$ with the network's guess and solve for $x_0$. In Figure 6 the green-framed row is the noisy $x_t$ getting cleaner left to right; the row above it is this $\hat{x}_0$ — big face shape appears early, eyes and hair later, tiny texture last. That's the coarse-to-fine property of diffusion.

Figure 7 shows something even stranger. Freeze the chain at some $t$, say $t=750$, and draw four different completions from $p_\theta(x_0|x_t)$ that all share that same $x_t$. At $t=1000$ they are totally different people. At $t=750$ they share gender, hair color, pose and eyewear but differ in details. At $t=250$ they are almost the same person — only freckles or strands of hair change. In other words, the intermediate static $x_t$ already encodes high-level meaning, even though you can't see it.

More Than Pretty Pictures

So diffusion models make nice faces — but the paper argues they're more than a prettier GAN. Two ideas make them surprisingly flexible: you can mix ideas in their noisy latent space, and you can send a photo piece by piece, big shapes first, like a progressive download.

Mixing works like this. Take two real photos, $\mathbf{x}_0$ and $\mathbf{x}'_0$. Don't mix the pixels. Instead, run the forward noisy process from Lesson 3 for $t$ steps to get noisy latents:

$$ \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}, \qquad \mathbf{x}'_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}'_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}' $$

Now mix those with a slider $\lambda$ between 0 and 1:

$$ \bar{\mathbf{x}}_t = (1-\lambda)\mathbf{x}_t + \lambda\mathbf{x}'_t $$

Then run the reverse (denoise) chain to clean $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_t$ back to an image $\bar{\mathbf{x}}_0$. The reverse acts like a photo-cleaner that removes the double-exposure artifacts.

Choice of $t$ is a coarse-to-fine knob. The paper's Figure 9 shows it: $t=0$ is just pixel blending — a ghostly overlay. $t=500$ — used in Figure 8 — gives a smooth, single face where pose, skin tone, hairstyle and expression glide from one source to the other (but not eyeglasses — those pop on or off). $t=1000$ destroys all signal, so the mix is pure noise and decoding it just invents a brand-new person unrelated to either source.

Progressive compression: think “rate = how many bits you've sent, distortion = how wrong your guess still is.” The paper splits its training loss $L$ as $L_1+\dots+L_T$ = rate for sending $\mathbf{x}_T,\dots,\mathbf{x}_1$, plus $L_0$ = remaining error in $\mathbf{x}_0$. At any time the receiver can already guess the photo with: $$ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \left(\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\right) / \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} $$ which is just solving the forward formula for $\mathbf{x}_0$ using the network's noise prediction. Early bits fix big shapes; later bits fix invisible details.

How many bits is that? For their best CIFAR-10 model, rate is $1.78$ bits per dimension and distortion is $1.97$ bits per dimension — on a $0$ to $255$ scale that's only $0.95$ RMSE error. More than half the file is spent on details you can barely see. Table 4 makes it concrete: at $1000$ steps received, rate $1.776$ and distortion $0.95$ (nearly perfect); at $500$ steps, rate $0.007$ but distortion $38.0$ (very blurry); at $100$ steps, rate $0.000$ and distortion $67.6$ (almost nothing). The curve drops steeply at first — a few bits buy a huge quality jump, just like progressive JPEG.

Finally, there's a neat bridge to the old way of making images one pixel at a time. The paper rewrites the loss as:

$$ L = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_T)\|p(\mathbf{x}_T)) + \mathbb{E}_q\left[\sum_{t\ge 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)\|p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t))\right] + H(\mathbf{x}_0) $$

If you made $T$ equal the number of pixels and defined the forward step as “mask the next pixel,” and started from a blank image, training this is exactly training an autoregressive model that predicts pixel $t$ given pixels $t+1\dots T$. Gaussian diffusion is the same idea but with a generalized ordering — it doesn't have to go pixel-by-pixel. That's why the paper can use $T=1000$ even though a $32\times32\times3$ image has $3072$ numbers (and $256\times256\times3$ has $196{,}608$). You can make the chain shorter for speed or longer for quality, and noise blends are more natural for photos than masking squares.

Did It Work?

So did the slow, step-by-step trick actually beat the fast GANs everyone used in 2020? Short answer: yes — on looks. No — on pure codelength. And that split is why this paper mattered.

On unconditional CIFAR-10 — tiny 32×32 photos, no class hints — the diffusion model got FID = 3.17 when compared to the training set, and 5.24 when compared to the held-out test set. Lower FID means "looks closer to real photos," so lower is better. At the time, that 3.17 was state-of-the-art, better than most class-conditional models that even got a cheat-sheet label. Its Inception Score — higher is better, meaning sharp and diverse — was 9.46. Big photos worked too: on LSUN 256×256 the same simple setup got Church 7.89, Bedroom 6.36 with the 114-million-parameter model and 4.90 when they made it bigger (~256M), and Cat 19.75. Compare that to Table 3 in the paper: ProgressiveGAN was at 8.34 / 6.42 / 37.52, StyleGAN at 2.65 / 4.21 / 8.53, StyleGAN2 at 3.86 / 6.93. Diffusion wasn't winning every LSUN category, but it was suddenly playing in the same league — from pure static, with no adversarial game.

The twist: sample quality ≠ best log-likelihood. Trained on the true variational bound, the model has slightly better lossless codelength (bits per dimension), but uglier samples. Trained on the paper's simplified loss Lsimple = E[|| ε − εθ(...)||2] — just "predict the noise" with no fancy weighting — the codelength gets a little worse, but the pictures get much better. And trying to learn the reverse variance made training unstable and worse. The ablation's lesson: predict ε + use Lsimple + keep variance fixed.

Was it just memorizing? The paper checks: the gap between train and test codelength is ≤ 0.03 bits/dim — tiny — and the pixel-space nearest neighbors look different from the samples. So it's generalizing, not copying.

It wasn't cheap either. CIFAR-10 took 800k steps at batch 128 — about 10.6 hours at 21 steps/sec on a TPU v3-8 — and sampling 256 images meant 1000 network passes and still took 17 seconds. The 256×256 models ran at 2.2 steps/sec, and sampling 128 of them took ~300 seconds. Details that helped: dropout 0.1 on CIFAR (0 elsewhere), random horizontal flips, EMA decay 0.9999, and the linear beta schedule from 1×10⁻⁴ to 0.02 we saw earlier.

The bigger impact was conceptual. One simple reverse chain — trained with variational inference — turned out to be also a denoising score-matcher, also an annealed Langevin sampler, also a progressive lossy compressor that draws big shapes first then details, and also a generalized autoregressive decoder that doesn't need to go pixel-by-pixel. Proving that one idea could beat GANs on quality opened the door to Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2, and the rest. The trade-off the paper left us with is still with us: stunning quality, but you pay in 1000 sampling steps.

Go deeper: the authors note the downsides too — 1000 evaluations make sampling slow (later work distills this), and like any powerful generator the tool can be misused for deepfakes or can amplify dataset bias. On the upside, they point to compression and representation learning as positive uses.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

generative model

A type of computer program that learns to create new data that looks like its training data, for example new photos of faces that were never in the dataset.

Gaussian / Normal distribution

A bell-shaped random distribution. N(mean, variance*I) means each pixel gets a random nudge centered on mean with spread controlled by variance.

Markov chain

A sequence where the next step depends only on the current step, not the whole history. Like a random walk.

forward process / diffusion process

The process in the paper that gradually adds a little Gaussian noise to an image over many steps until it becomes pure static. Equation 2: q(x_t|x_{t-1}) = N(sqrt(1-β_t) x_{t-1}, β_t I)

reverse process

The process the neural network learns: start from pure noise and step-by-step remove noise to make a new image. p_θ(x_{t-1}|x_t) = N(μ_θ(x_t,t), Σ_θ(x_t,t))

variance schedule (β_t)

A schedule of small numbers β_1...β_T that controls how much noise is added at each forward step. Paper uses T=1000, linear from β_1=10^-4 to β_T=0.02.

alpha_bar (ᾱ_t)

α_t = 1-β_t and ᾱ_t = α_1*α_2*...*α_t . ᾱ_t tells you how much of the original image is still left after t steps of noise.

variational bound (ELBO)

A training target that is an upper bound on -log p(x_0). Minimizing it trains the model. Paper uses L = L_T + sum L_{t-1} + L_0, each term a KL divergence between Gaussians.

KL divergence

Compares two probability distributions. For Gaussians it has a simple formula and can be computed exactly.

L_simple (noise-prediction loss)

The paper's simplified training loss: L_simple = E[||ε - ε_θ( sqrt(ᾱ_t) x_0 + sqrt(1-ᾱ_t) ε , t)||^2] . The network just tries to predict the noise ε that was added.

Langevin dynamics / annealed Langevin

A way to sample images that is like rolling a ball downhill on a learned landscape, with a little random shake. The paper's sampler resembles this.

score / denoising score matching

Gradient of log probability – which direction to move to make an image more likely. Predicting noise turns out to be the same as predicting this score.

FID and Inception Score

Scores for image quality. FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) low is better – measures how close generated images look to real ones. Inception Score high is better – measures sharp and diverse. CIFAR10 FID 3.17 in paper was state-of-the-art.

U-Net backbone

A U-Net shaped neural network (like a Wide ResNet with down- and up-sampling) with group normalization and self-attention at 16x16, conditioned on timestep t via Transformer sinusoidal position embedding. Same weights used for all t.

autoregressive model

A model that generates an image one pixel (or coordinate) at a time, conditioned on previous ones. The paper shows diffusion is like a generalization with a 'bit ordering' not limited to pixels.
