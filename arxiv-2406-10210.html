001The counting failure~5 min

Why does 'draw six cats' give 4 cats?

Ask an image model for “a photo of six cats” and you might get four. Ask for Goldilocks and the three bears — a classic test in the paper — and you might get only two bears. The picture looks beautiful, but the count is obviously wrong. That’s the puzzle: today’s best generators are amazing artists and terrible counters.

Why is counting so hard for an AI painter? Think of drawing seven identical apples that overlap on a table. You have to keep a separate idea of apple #1, apple #2 … apple #7 even though they all look the same, and you have to place them so the whole scene still makes sense. In AI language that’s two jobs: keep object identity (this blob is a different apple than that blob) and handle global spatial relations (all apples together fit naturally). Text-to-image models like SDXL weren’t really taught to do either.

The failure is easy to see and not rare: on the paper’s CoCoCount test (200 prompts like “a photo of N {object}”) SDXL got the number right only 28% of the time by automatic YOLO check and 26% by human check. On a second set, T2I-CompBench (218 prompts), it was 29% by humans.

You might try a quick fix. Trick 1: repeat the word — write “a cat and a cat and a cat” instead of “three cats.” It sounds logical, but in the paper it made things worse: accuracy fell from 28% to 17%. Trick 2: ask a big language model like GPT-3.5 to write a layout (“cat at x=0.1, cat at x=0.5…”) and force the painter to follow it. That gave layouts that look randomly scattered and still scored low — about 21% YOLO for the method called “Reason Out Your Layout.” The model needs layout help that actually respects how cats or bottles naturally sit together.

CountGen’s idea is different: don’t bring a layout from outside. Peek inside the diffusion painter while it’s still drawing and use the layout that is already emerging there. The model already has a rough idea at mid-draw of where objects will go. If we can read that layout, count it, and fix it, we can then guide the painter to finish with the right number — keeping the prompt-dependent arrangement instead of random boxes.

In the lab below, be the evaluator: ask for 2, 5, 7, or 10 objects and generate 20 pretend images. Watch how accuracy collapses as the number grows — that’s exactly what the paper’s Figure 9 shows for every model, even DALL·E 3. Notice how often the mistakes are under-counts (missing one or two).

002How the painter works~6 min

Inside the AI painter: noise, steps, and attention

Ask an AI for a photo and it doesn't paint like you do. It starts from TV static — pure noise — and slowly cleans it up, step by step, until an image appears.

Researchers count those clean-up steps with a number called t. At t = 1000 the picture is all noise. At t = 0 it's sharp and finished. The paper hits pause halfway, at t = 500, while the image is still blurry. Surprisingly, the layout — where each cat or bottle will sit — is already mostly decided by then.

Inside the cleaner lives a U-Net. Think of it as a long hallway with many layers. At every layer two kinds of attention talk to each other:

  • Cross-attention: words → pixels. For the word “cat”, the model lights up the pixels where it thinks a cat should be. It's the prompt telling the canvas what to draw where.
  • Self-attention: pixels → pixels. Pixels compare notes — “are we part of the same thing?” — and share information to keep an object consistent.
Both attentions exist in almost every U-Net layer. Cross-attention says where the prompt wants something. Self-attention decides what holds together as one object. Counting fails when the second one smears two identical cats into one.

Cleaning from noise is cheap: SDXL alone takes about 8 seconds on a powerful A100 chip. CountGen, which stops to inspect and fix the layout, takes about 36 seconds. An older layout-guided method called Bounded Attention takes about 55 seconds. So fixing the count costs time, but not as much as you might think.

Here's the plan the paper will follow: 1) at t = 500 find the blobs that are forming and count them, 2) fix the layout to have the right number, 3) steer the rest of the denoising to obey that new layout.

003Finding object-ness~6 min

Where in the brain is 'a separate cat'?

Imagine you peek inside the AI while it's still painting — halfway through, at step t=500 (noise t=1000 → clean image t=0). The canvas is blurry, but the layout is already decided. Can you tell how many kittens it thinks it's drawing?

This is tricky. The model's eyes — its self-attention features — connect pixels to each other. If two pixels belong to the same kitten, their features should be similar. If they belong to two different kittens, their features should be different — even if the kittens look identical. That property is what researchers call "objectness": keeping a separate identity for each instance.

No one knew if SDXL actually does this. So the authors did a safari. They took every self-attention layer, at t=500, squashed its many numbers down to 3 with PCA (a way to turn high-dimensional data into a color: similar numbers → similar color), and looked.

Most layers failed. Early and late layers painted all kittens the same muted color, or only separated "cats vs. floor." But one layer was different: layer lup52. There, 5 kittens got 5 distinct hues — like fingerprints. The same held for bottles, elephants, and other objects.

Timing matters too. At t=900 (very noisy) the colors are still mixed. Around t=500 the rainbow snaps into place. That's early enough to intervene before the image is finished — which is exactly what CountGen will do next.

In the lab below, hunt for that layer yourself. Switch the layer dropdown and watch the blob colors: only l_up_52 gives each object its own color. Try different scenes — the separation is robust. Hit Shuffle colors to see it's not a lucky palette; the model really keeps instances apart in that one hidden spot.

004Finding and counting~7 min

Catching cats mid-draw: masks to clusters

Here is the clever timing trick in CountGen: it doesn't wait for the finished picture to count cats. Halfway through the denoising — at timestep $t=500$ — the layout is already decided, even though the image is still blurry. If we can count now, we can still fix it.

Counting at $t=500$ takes two filters, back-to-back.

Filter 1 — Where are the objects at all? The paper aggregates the cross-attention maps $A^{cross}_{l,t}$ for the object word (like "cat"). Think of this as a heatmap: bright where the model thinks "cat should be here." Then it runs Otsu thresholding — an automatic rule that picks the best cutoff between bright and dark. Call the result the foreground mask $M$:

$$M = \text{Otsu}(A^{cross}_{l,t})$$

$M$ is just a black-and-white map: white pixels are "something object-y here," black is background. No counting yet — just "object vs not."

Filter 2 — Which pixels belong to the same cat? Inside that white area, the paper grabs the self-attention features $A^{self}_{l^{up}_{52},t=500}$ — the special layer from the last lesson where different instances got different colors. Call those foreground features $p_k$. Then it clusters them with DBSCAN, which groups nearby dots and ignores loners:

$$\text{Clusters}=\text{DBSCAN}(p_k,\epsilon),\quad \epsilon \in [0.1,0.2] \text{ with cosine distance}$$

Each cluster becomes one instance mask. The whole layout is just the union:

$$L = \bigcup_{C\in\text{Clusters}} C$$

Counting is now easy: number of clusters = number of objects being drawn. Too few? Under-generation. Too many? Over-generation. All of this happens early, so the ReLayout fixer can step in next.

Key idea: two different attentions do two jobs — cross-attention says where objects are, self-attention at $l^{up}_{52}$ says which pixels are the same individual.

Try it below. You are the counter at $t=500$: set the Otsu threshold to grow or shrink the foreground mask, then tune epsilon to split or merge the colored clusters. Watch how the verdict flips between under, correct, and over.

005Fixing too few~7 min

ReLayout: the Lego-fixer model

The hard case is "too few". Say SDXL wanted six cats but only drew four. We need to add two more cats — but where? A random spot looks fake: a cat floating above the row, or squashed on top of another.

CountGen's fixer is called ReLayout. Think of it like Lego instructions. Input: a layout of k binary masks (W × H × k). Output: a layout with k+1 masks (W × H × (k+1)). It handles up to 9 in, 10 out. Need to go from 4 to 6? Run it twice.

How did it learn "natural" placement? The authors made ~10,000 training pairs from the model's own imagination. Same seed, same prompt except the number: "a photo of two cats" vs "a photo of three cats". Often the composition stays almost identical — five kittens sitting in a row stay in a row, just with one extra kitten. They kept only pairs where the counts were correct (using the counter from last lesson), so each pair is (Mk, Mk+1) with the same scene plus one.

The matching trick: To train, we must know which kitten in image 1 became which kitten in image 2. The Hungarian algorithm finds the pairing that minimizes total movement — like matching dancers between two photos with the least walking. The unpaired mask in Mk+1 is the new object.

Training uses two losses. Dice loss asks "does each predicted mask overlap its target?":

$$L_{Dice}=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\left(1-\frac{2\sum_p M_{k+1}^i(p)\cdot \hat M_{k+1}^i(p)}{\sum_p M_{k+1}^i(p)+\hat M_{k+1}^i(p)}\right)$$

Overlap loss asks "do different masks stay separate?" Total: L = LDICE + 0.25 · Loverlap (Dice weight 1, overlap weight 0.25). At test time, if we need to add many objects, ReLayout gradually adds padding around the layout — a slow "zoom-out" to make room.

What about "too many"? That's easier: deterministically remove the smallest masks until the count is right.

006Forcing the painter to obey~7 min

Getting the final image to keep its promise

ReLayout just gave us the right blueprint — say, four neat cat masks where we previously had three. Now the hard part: will the painter actually respect the blueprint? Out of the box, SDXL often doesn't. It's been trained to make pretty pictures, not to obey dashed lines.

CountGen fixes that after the fact, while the image is still being denoised. Remember we paused halfway at t=500. To finish the image, CountGen adds two guardrails that work together.

1. A loss that says “put cats here” — 10 times louder inside

For every pixel $i$, we know from the mask $m_i$ whether it should be cat or not: $m_i=1$ means “inside a cat mask”, $m_i=0$ means background. We also have $c_i$, the model's cross-attention score — basically how strongly the word “cat” is trying to draw at that pixel.

$$L(c,m)= -\sum_i w_i\,\big(m_i\log c_i + (1-m_i)\log(1-c_i)\big)$$

That's weighted binary cross-entropy. In plain words: if a pixel should be cat but $c_i$ is low, pay a big penalty; if it should be background but $c_i$ is high, also penalized. The trick is $w_i$: $w_i=10$ inside the mask, $w_i=1$ outside. So being wrong inside costs 10× more. The model optimizes this at test time for $t$ in [1000,500] in all U-Net layers — nudging $c$ to be 1 inside the masks and 0 outside.

Alone, this pulls objects into the masks — but some can still leak out.

2. Cutting the gossip between background and foreground

Self-attention is how pixels talk to each other: “are we part of the same thing?” If background pixels can listen to foreground pixels, they can quietly start forming another cat just outside the blueprint.

CountGen mutes that link:

$$S^{*(l)}_t[i,j]=\begin{cases}0 & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{B}^{(l)} \text{ and } j \in \mathcal{F}^{(l)} \\ S^{(l)}_t[i,j] & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

$S_t[i,j]$ is the normal self-attention weight from background pixel $i$ to foreground pixel $j$. $S^*$ sets it to zero. No information flows from cats to background. This is applied early, $t$ in [1000,900], in the decoder layers — just when layout is deciding “will I make something here?”

You need both. No loss → objects scatter and ignore the masks (low recall and low IOU). No masking → objects obey the masks but extras appear in the background (low precision). Together they give balanced, on-layout generation — exactly the ablation in Figure 7.

Built on the Attend-and-Excite code, this finishes ReLayout's corrected blueprint into a real image that finally keeps its promise.

007Does it work?~6 min

How much better? And what's still broken?

So does all that plumbing — find objects mid-draw, fix the layout, force the painter to obey — actually move the needle?

To check, the team built two scoreboards. First, CoCoCount: 200 simple prompts like “a photo of 7 apples on the grass” using 20 common COCO objects, with counts 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, and 10. Because they are COCO objects, a detector called YOLOv9 can count automatically. Second, T2I-CompBench-Count: 218 existing prompts with numbers 2 to 10. For both, they also asked people: three raters per image, majority vote, and they only counted if the object was present and well-formed.

On CoCoCount, a person checking found SDXL right only 26% of the time — YOLO said 28%. With CountGen on the same SDXL base, that jumped to 54% by people and 50% by YOLO. On T2I-CompBench it went 29% → 48%. Roughly a doubling. The next best at the time, DALL·E 3, got 38% and 36% — better than vanilla SDXL, but still behind.

Numbers alone hide a pattern. Accuracy collapses as you ask for more. In the paper’s Figure 9, for n = 2 or 3, CountGen and DALL·E 3 are neck-and-neck (DALL·E 3 even a touch ahead). For every n > 3, CountGen stays clearly on top while SDXL, counting-guidance, and LLM-layout methods fall fast. Ask for 10 and even CountGen is only about 30% right in this simplified view — much better than ~8% for SDXL, but still far from perfect.

Where does the boost come from? They swapped parts. Start from a baseline that puts random non-overlapping boxes down and uses Bounded Attention: ~30% human on CoCoCount. Add CountGen’s learned ReLayout (the “fixer” trained on the model’s own pairs) → about 44%, so +14 points just from a natural layout. Add the layout-guided generation (weighted loss + masking) in the other direction → also ~42%. Together they reach 54%. And quality didn’t collapse: when raters compared CountGen vs SDXL for naturalness, only 23 out of 200 times did most prefer SDXL.

Still broken. The authors are blunt: sometimes CountGen squeezes two kittens into one mask slot; sometimes it makes the background plain to avoid mistakes; it was only tested for one object type at a time up to 10; and errors grow with n. The confusion matrix (Figure 17) shows most mistakes are near-misses — asked for 6, got 5 or 7 — not wild guesses.

008Why it matters~5 min

Lessons for tomorrow's image makers

Think of CountGen as three Lego moves you can teach any AI painter.

1. Find: halfway through denoising, at step $t=500$, the model has already decided where things go. In one special layer, $l^{up}_{52}$, its self-attention features act like invisible name tags: all pixels of kitten #1 share one fingerprint, kitten #2 shares a different one. Most layers don't do this — this one does, discovered by PCA coloring in the paper.

2. Fix layout: we count those fingerprints with clustering. If we asked for 6 and see 4, we run ReLayout — a small U-Net that learned from ~10,000 pairs the model drew itself: same seed, prompts differing by one ("2 cats" vs "3 cats"). It keeps the composition and adds one in a natural spot. If we saw 7 instead of 6, we just remove the smallest mask. ReLayout is trained with $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{DICE}}+\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\text{overlap}}$ where $\lambda=0.25$, so masks match targets and don't overlap each other.

3. Guide: we restart the painting but force it to obey the fixed masks $m$. A weighted loss $L(c,m)=-\sum_i w_i [m_i\log c_i+(1-m_i)\log(1-c_i)]$ with $w_i=10$ inside $m$ and $1$ outside pulls objects inside, while masking self-attention between background and foreground stops extra copies from sprouting outside. One without the other fails — exactly what the ablation shows.

Why this matters: earlier fixes asked an outside LLM to invent boxes. CountGen uses the painter's own sense of space — so layouts stay prompt- and seed-dependent (a row of bottles stays a row; a herd of elephants stays a herd) instead of looking randomly placed. Result on CoCoCount: from 26% correct for SDXL to 54% human-evaluated, and from 28% to 50% by YOLO detector; on T2I-CompBench from 29% to 48% — beating DALL·E 3, counting guidance, and random-layout baselines.
Scope, honestly: the paper tests up to 10 instances of one object class, on SDXL (~36 sec vs 8 sec for plain SDXL on an A100). It can still put two objects in one slot, or make plainer backgrounds, and accuracy still falls as $n$ grows. The confusion matrix (Figure 17) shows errors are usually near-misses. The authors released CoCoCount (200 prompts over 20 COCO classes, counts 2,3,4,5,7,10) so future work can check counts automatically with YOLOv9.

That same idea — find, fix, guide using the model's own prior — should travel beyond counting: to any spatial rule ("the cat left of the chair"), or to video where objects must stay consistent over time. ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Diffusion model / SDXL

An AI model that starts from pure static (noise) and slowly removes noise step-by-step until a clear image appears. SDXL is one famous open model that does this.

Timestep (t)

The step-by-step denoising process. Timestep t=1000 means very noisy, t=0 means final image. The paper checks what the model is thinking already at t=500, halfway.

Cross-attention map

The model's way of linking words to image regions. High cross-attention for the word 'cat' means 'the cat should be here'.

Self-attention features

The model's way of linking image regions to each other. If two pixels have similar self-attention features, the model treats them as part of the same object.

PCA visualization

A trick to squash many numbers into 2D colors so humans can see patterns. In the paper, each separate kitten gets a different color only in one special layer.

Otsu thresholding

A way to automatically decide what is foreground vs background. Otsu looks at the brightness histogram and finds the best cutoff.

DBSCAN clustering

A clustering algorithm that groups nearby dots and ignores lonely noise. It needs a distance limit called epsilon (0.1 to 0.2 in the paper) and uses cosine similarity.

Hungarian algorithm

An algorithm that perfectly pairs up objects from two images to minimize total movement - like matching dancers between two photos.

U-Net

A U-shaped neural network good at turning one image/mask into another. CountGen's ReLayout is a U-Net that turns a k-object layout into a k+1-object layout.

Dice overlap loss

How well two shapes overlap. 1 = perfect match. The paper uses Dice loss to train ReLayout to match target masks, plus an overlap loss to stop objects from overlapping each other.

Layout / mask

A map of where each object should go: a colored mask for each instance, like stickers on a page before drawing.

Weighted cross-entropy loss

A test-time rule that punishes the model if it doesn't put the object inside the mask. The paper gives 10x weight to pixels inside the mask vs 1x outside.

Self-attention masking

Cutting connections inside the model so background pixels cannot copy information from foreground pixels, preventing extra cats from appearing in the background.

CoCoCount & T2I-CompBench

The paper's two test sets: CoCoCount (200 prompts like 'a photo of 7 apples' built from COCO objects, checkable by YOLOv9 detector) and T2I-CompBench-Count (218 prompts with numbers 2-10).
