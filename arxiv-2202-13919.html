001Lesson 1 — the hook~10 min

Why the Universe's oldest light wears sunglasses

Imagine taking a baby photo of the entire Universe. That photo exists. It is the cosmic microwave background — the CMB — the faint glow left over from when the Universe was a hot fireball.

About 13 to 14 billion years ago, space was so hot — above about 3,000 K — that electrons roamed free and light kept bumping into them. Space was opaque, like thick fog. When it cooled below that, electrons joined protons and helium nuclei to make neutral atoms. The fog cleared. Light could suddenly travel freely. We have been catching that freed light ever since.

As it travelled, expanding space stretched its wavelengths, like drawing on a balloon and then inflating it. The light kept a perfect heat-glow spectrum but cooled. Averaged over the whole sky today, that temperature is $T_0 = 2.725$ K — just 2.7 degrees above absolute zero, with its peak in microwaves. That is why we call it the cosmic microwave background. Different directions are not exactly $2.725$ K: some are a tiny bit hotter, some colder, by about $\Delta T/T_0 \sim 10^{-5}$, or roughly one part in 100,000. Those flickers are the seeds that grew into galaxies.

But there is more than brightness. That old light also wears invisible sunglasses: it is linearly polarized. This just means that at the surface where the light last scattered — about redshift $z_{LS}\approx 1090$ — the light's electric field wobbled preferentially in one plane. In the paper's Figure 1 you see a $10^{\circ}\times 10^{\circ}$ patch cut from Planck's full-sky map. Colour shows $\Delta T$ — redder hotter, bluer colder. The little lines show polarization: the length of a line is how strongly polarized the light is there, and the direction it points is which way the light wiggles.

Why care about tiny lines? Because our best model of the cosmos, $\Lambda$CDM, needs new physics beyond the Standard Model of particles. Something makes up 80% of the matter — dark matter — and something we write as $\Lambda$ pushes space to expand faster today — dark energy. And something planted the initial flickers themselves. Polarization may answer: what that dark stuff really is, and how the first ripples were made.

In 20 years the fuzz in our pictures dropped 1,000 times, from WMAP to Planck and ground telescopes like Polarbear, SPTpol, ACTPol and BICEP/Keck. In the next decade Simons Observatory, South Pole Observatory, CMB Stage-4 on the ground and LiteBIRD in space plan to drop it another 10 times. At that point the biggest problem is not making the picture less fuzzy, but knowing exactly which way is up — whether a line tilted by $0.3^{\circ}$ is real, or just your camera twisted a hair.

Before we can hunt for that twist, we need to read the baby photo itself. In the lab below, explore the same kind of patch as Figure 1. 002Lesson 2 — building blocks~10 min

How an electron makes light wiggle one way

Sunlight bouncing off a car windshield looks glary because it is polarized — it mostly wiggles in one plane instead of all planes. A rainbow is polarized too. In both cases light becomes one-way because it came from a particular direction and then scattered.

The early Universe had no windshields, just a sea of photons and free electrons. So how did its light get polarized? The trick is the same: around each electron, the incoming light has to be a little lopsided.

🔦

Scattering has a rule

For Thomson scattering (light bouncing off an electron), the chance to scatter depends on wiggle direction: dσ/dΩ ∝ (ε · ε')². If the incoming wiggle ε is perpendicular to the outgoing wiggle ε', the product is zero — that scatter is forbidden.

↕️ ↔️

What that means

Vertically wiggling light coming along z can scatter to x and z, but cannot scatter to y. Horizontally wiggling light can scatter to y and z, but not to x. The direction picks which polarization survives.

Now send in unpolarized light — a mix of both wiggles. Light coming from z and scattered to x comes out vertically polarized. Light coming from y and scattered to x comes out horizontally polarized. That's Fig. 6 b-1 in the paper.

So what if unpolarized light comes from both y and z at once, with equal brightness, and we look at light scattered to x? You get equal amounts of vertical and horizontal — they cancel. Net result: no polarization at all. The paper calls this b-2.

Only when the two directions are unequal in brightness do you get leftover polarization. Hotter from y than from z → net vertical. Hotter from z → net horizontal. That imbalance — two hot spots and two cold spots around the electron — is called a quadrupole. The Universe is isotropic on average, but locally each electron saw a tiny quadrupole, and that's what got frozen in as CMB polarization.

Before atoms formed, photons and electrons were glued together, colliding constantly in a "cosmic hot soup." Everything got smeared isotropic, so no quadrupole, no polarization. Only when the temperature fell to ~3000 K and electrons became rare did the light finally travel a bit between hits, feel the hot-cold differences, build a quadrupole, and polarize at the last-scattering surface.
Go deeper: the formula dσ/dΩ = 3σT(ε·ε')²/8π is for Thomson scattering, where σT is the Thomson cross-section. ε is the incoming polarization vector, ε' the outgoing one. The dot product ε·ε' being zero is what forbids the scatter in panels a-1 and a-2.

003Lesson 3 — the code~12 min

The secret language: E and B

Think of wind over a wheat field. You could describe every stalk individually — which way it bends — but it is easier to name the pattern. A ripple rolling north makes stalks lie north-south in the crests and east-west in the troughs. That is one kind of pattern. Another ripple makes them lie diagonally, at 45°. Both use the same stalks, just organized differently.

CMB polarization is the same. At each spot on the sky we measure two numbers for linear polarization, called Stokes Q and U:

$$ Q = |E_x|^2-|E_y|^2,\quad U = 2\mathrm{Re}(E_x^*E_y) $$

It is convenient to combine them as $$ Q \pm iU = P e^{\pm 2 i \beta} $$ where $P$ is how strongly polarized the light is, and $\beta$ is the angle of the little line you see in the maps. In the CMB convention used in the paper, $\beta>0$ is a clockwise turn on the sky; the astronomy convention (IAU) defines it opposite. That sign matters when we later claim a $0.3^\circ$ twist.

Any map of those lines can be split into two fundamental patterns:

〰️

E-mode: even parity

Lines are parallel or perpendicular to the wave's direction $\boldsymbol{\ell}$. If you flip the sky $\hat{n}\to -\hat{n}$, the pattern comes back to itself: $E_{\ell m}\to (-1)^\ell E_{\ell m}$.

🌀

B-mode: odd parity

Lines are tipped by $\pm45^\circ$ to $\boldsymbol{\ell}$. Under a flip it picks up an extra minus: $B_{\ell m}\to (-1)^{\ell+1} B_{\ell m}$. It is the mirror image test.

Formally on a small patch the paper writes $$ Q(\boldsymbol{\theta})\pm iU(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int\frac{d^2\boldsymbol{\ell}}{(2\pi)^2}\,(E_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\pm iB_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\,e^{\pm2i\phi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}+i\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\theta}} $$ and on the full sphere the same idea uses spin-2 spherical harmonics, $ _{\pm2}Y_\ell^m(\hat{n})$. Think of $E_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ and $B_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$ as the amounts of the two wheat patterns for a wave going direction $\phi_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}$.

Why we average: The Universe has no preferred direction, so we average over all orientations $m$ at each angular size $\ell$ (big $\ell$ = small spots). That gives power spectra:
$$ C_\ell^{EE}=\frac1{2\ell+1}\sum_m|E_{\ell m}|^2,\quad C_\ell^{BB}=\frac1{2\ell+1}\sum_m|B_{\ell m}|^2 $$ parity-even, and
$$ C_\ell^{EB}=\frac1{2\ell+1}\sum_m\mathrm{Re}(E_{\ell m}B_{\ell m}^*),\quad C_\ell^{TB}=\frac1{2\ell+1}\sum_m\mathrm{Re}(T_{\ell m}B_{\ell m}^*) $$ parity-odd. If mirror symmetry holds, $C_\ell^{EB}$ and $C_\ell^{TB}$ must be zero.

Measurements show $C_\ell^{EE}$ has clear peaks — sound waves in the hot early "soup" — while $C_\ell^{BB}$ so far matches only what you expect when gravity bends E into B (lensing). A primordial gravitational-wave background would add extra $B$ power with two characteristic bumps: a reionization bump at $\ell\lesssim10$ and a recombination bump at $\ell\approx80$. Crucially, ordinary density (scalar) ripples make only $E$ at linear order — so any intrinsic $B$ is a clean flag for something else: gravity waves, lensing, or a parity-violating twist.

That twist needs two ingredients. Rotating every line by $\beta$ mixes $E$ and $B$, giving $C_\ell^{EB,\mathrm{o}}\propto \sin(4\beta)\,(C_\ell^{EE}-C_\ell^{BB})$. If $C_\ell^{EE}=C_\ell^{BB}$, random lines stay random and rotation does nothing. Our Universe luckily has $C_\ell^{EE}\gg C_\ell^{BB}$, so even that $0.3^\circ$ crystal-like turn becomes detectable — exactly what you will test next.

Try it: drag the wave direction $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ and slide the E↔B mix. Watch sticks snap from aligned (E) to diagonal (B). See the three bars: pure E or pure B alone gives no EB; you need both and $C_\ell^{EE}\neq C_\ell^{BB}$ for EB to light up.

004Lesson 4 — new physics I setup~12 min

When space behaves like a crystal

Imagine looking through a clear crystal. The crystal doesn't block light — it just makes light that wiggles one way travel a hair slower than light that wiggles the other way. When the two are combined, the final wiggle has turned a little. That's birefringence.

This paper asks: what if all of space behaved like that faint crystal, because it's filled with something that breaks mirror symmetry?

In particle physics that something would be a pseudoscalar field, called χ (chi). "Pseudo" means it flips sign in a mirror, like a left hand becoming a right hand. A familiar example is the pion π⁰ — it decays to two photons precisely because it couples to light in a mirror-breaking way. A hypothetical axion would do the same. The paper lets dark matter or dark energy be made of such a χ.

The coupling is written as a Chern-Simons term in the energy:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{CS}= -\frac{\alpha}{4f}\, \chi\, F\tilde{F} \quad\text{with}\quad F\tilde{F}= -4\,\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{E} $$

Here F\tilde{F} is just −4 times B dot E — it changes sign in a mirror, so χ must too for the whole thing to be allowed. α is a dimensionless number (how strongly they talk) and f is an energy scale (how stiff χ is). For the real pion, α = N_c e^2/6π^2 and f ≈ 184 MeV. For cosmology we treat α/f as unknown to be measured.

Why tiny becomes visible: Light of wave-number k (short wavelength = big k) obeys
$$ A''_{\pm} + \left(k^2 \mp k\,\frac{\alpha\chi'}{f}\right) A_{\pm}=0 $$
for its two circular helicities ±. The prime is change with conformal time. The extra term is tiny: phase speed
$$ \frac{\omega_{\pm}}{k} \approx 1 \mp \frac{\alpha\chi'}{2kf} $$
differs from light-speed c=1 by only ~ wavelength / size of visible Universe. But CMB photons traveled ~13 billion years. The phase difference accumulates.

Count the wiggle difference between the two helicities over the journey:

$$ \beta = -\frac12\int (\omega_+ - \omega_-)\,d\eta = \frac{\alpha}{2f}\int \chi'\,d\eta = \frac{\alpha}{2f}\,[\chi_{\text{today}}-\chi_{\text{last scattering}}] $$

In words: the plane of linear polarization rotates by half the coupling times the total change in the field from last scattering to today. That's β = α/2f · Δχ. If χ is perfectly constant, nothing happens — you need an evolving dark energy or a lumpy dark matter field. More distance = more rotation, which is why CMB beats any lab crystal. Fig. 3 shows it: black lines born at last scattering arrive as orange, twisted by β.

If χ varies across the sky, each direction gets its own β(\hat{n}) = α/2f\,[\chi_0 - \chi_{LS}(\hat{n})]. Generally the gradient p_μ = ∂_μ χ picks a preferred direction in spacetime and breaks Lorentz symmetry.

How to tell this apart from ordinary magnetic Faraday rotation? Check frequency. Faraday twists as β ∝ ν^{-2}. A pseudoscalar crystal gives β ∝ ν^{\,n} with n=0 — same at 30 GHz and 353 GHz to first order. Lorentz-violating quantum-gravity ideas predict n=1 or 2.

005Lesson 5 — new physics I detection~14 min

Catching a third of a degree

Imagine you measured every little line on the sky and saw a faint preference: lines tipped a hair clockwise. Is it new physics, or did you just mount your camera crooked?

That is exactly the situation with cosmic birefringence. A uniform twist by an angle β mixes the two parity types:

$$E^{o}_{\ell m}=E_{\ell m}\cos(2\beta)-B_{\ell m}\sin(2\beta),\;\; B^{o}_{\ell m}=E_{\ell m}\sin(2\beta)+B_{\ell m}\cos(2\beta)$$

In plain words: a bit of what was E becomes B, and vice versa. The superscript $o$ means "observed" after traveling 13 billion years. If the field $\chi$ changed quickly while the last scattering was still happening, we must integrate over its visibility $V(\eta)$ (Eq.17–20 in the paper) — rapid wiggling washes out. For a slowly drifting $\chi$, this simple rotation is right.

We do not look at $E$ and $B$ one by one; we look at their power spectra $C_\ell$, the average squared wiggle at each angular size $\ell$. Mixing gives:

$$C_{\ell}^{EB,o}=\tfrac12\sin(4\beta)\,(C_{\ell}^{EE}-C_{\ell}^{BB})+C_{\ell}^{EB}\cos(4\beta)$$

Notice the crucial factor $(C_{\ell}^{EE}-C_{\ell}^{BB})$. If $E$ and $B$ had equal power — like random scribbles — rotating everything would keep it random and $EB$ would stay zero. In our Universe $C_{\ell}^{EE}$ is much larger than $C_{\ell}^{BB}$ (Fig.2: $EE$ shows tall sound-wave peaks, $BB$ is only a faint lensed signal). That asymmetry is the lever. The paper also writes it using only observed spectra: $C_{\ell}^{EB,o}=\tfrac12(C_{\ell}^{EE,o}-C_{\ell}^{BB,o})\tan(4\beta)+C_{\ell}^{EB}/\cos(4\beta)$ (Eq.25).

A tiny angle still matters. $\beta=0.3^\circ$ gives $\sin(4\beta)\approx 0.021$, so $C_{\ell}^{EB}\approx 1\%$ of $(C_{\ell}^{EE}-C_{\ell}^{BB})$. With Planck's precise $EE$, that is detectable.

Now the headache: an instrument miscalibration $\alpha$ — not knowing exactly where "up" is on your focal plane — does the same thing: $Q^o\pm iU^o \rightarrow (Q\pm iU)e^{\pm 2i\alpha}$. Alone you only measure the sum $\alpha+\beta$. For years $\beta$ results were scattered just for this reason.

The 2019 trick by Minami and colleagues uses our own Galaxy as a calibrator. Dust foreground light traveled only thousands of light-years, not billions, so cosmic $\beta$ barely touched it. It was turned only by $\alpha$, while CMB was turned by $\alpha+\beta$:

$$E^o = E^{FG}\cos2\alpha + E^{CMB}\cos(2\alpha+2\beta)+...$$

and similarly for $B^o$ (Eq.26–28). Since we know $C_{\ell}^{EE,CMB}$ very well, we can fit both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ at once. No need to know the foreground $EE$ or $BB$ — only its possible $EB$.

That last point became important. Planck's high-frequency data (100, 143, 217, 353 GHz) gave $\beta=0.35^\circ\pm0.14^\circ$ [31], then with the cleaner NPIPE reprocessing $\beta=0.30^\circ\pm0.11^\circ$ [32]. Adding low frequencies (30,44,70 GHz) and testing $\beta(\nu)\propto \nu^n$ gave $n=-0.35^{+0.48}_{-0.47}$, consistent with $n=0$ (frequency-independent, as $\chi$ predicts) and $\beta=0.33^\circ\pm0.10^\circ$, more than $3\sigma$ [33]. Faraday rotation would need $n=-2$; quantum-gravity Lorentz violation would give $n=1$ or $2$. Using a dust-filament model for the foreground's own $C_{\ell}^{EB}$, the result stayed $\beta=0.36^\circ\pm0.11^\circ$.

What does $0.33^\circ\approx 0.0058$ radians mean? From $\beta = \alpha/2f\,[\chi_{today}-\chi_{LS}]$, the field moved by $\Delta\chi \approx 10^{-2}f/\alpha$ (Eq.29). If $\alpha$ is like the fine-structure constant (~1/137), then $\Delta\chi \sim f$, just what an axion-like cosine potential naturally does. Crucially, a non-zero evolving $\chi$ would rule out a pure cosmological constant $\Lambda$. No significant $\beta(\hat n)$ fluctuations across the sky have been seen yet.

It is still a hint, not yet a discovery, and it lives or dies on that $\pm0.10^\circ$ error bar. 006Lesson 6 — new physics II setup~12 min

Echoes from when space exploded

Imagine freezing the Universe when it was a trillionth of a trillionth of a second old. Everything that would become galaxies was still smaller than an atom — just faint quantum fuzz jittering empty space. Then space exploded. In a flash, that microscopic fuzz was stretched to billions of light years across.

That explosion is called cosmic inflation. The paper describes it with two simple ideas. The size of space, a(t), grew almost exponentially, a ∝ exp(∫ H dt), because its expansion rate H — how fast space doubles — changed very slowly. Physicists measure that slowness with εH = -ḢH/H². Inflation means εH ≪ 1.

That violent stretching did two jobs at once. It turned quantum wobbles in density into the seeds for stars and galaxies — and it also shook space itself, making gravitational waves: ripples where distances stretch one way and squeeze the other way, keeping total volume the same.

You can write that shake as a little 3×3 table with only two independent wiggles:

$$ h_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix} h_{+} & h_{\times} & 0 \\ h_{\times} & -h_{+} & 0 \\ 0&0&0 \end{pmatrix} $$

Here h+ stretches x while squeezing y, and h× does the same but rotated 45°. They obey a wave equation, □ hij = -16πG a⁻² Ttij. For the pure vacuum — no matter doing anything, Tt=0 — the solution is famous:

$$ h_{p,k}(\eta)= i\frac{H}{M_{\rm pl}} \frac{e^{-ik\eta}}{k^{3/2}}(1+ik\eta) $$

Don't panic about symbols. k is just how wiggly the wave is (big k = short wavelength). Deep inside the horizon the wave buzzes as e^{-ikη} and fades as 1/a. Once inflation stretches it far larger than the horizon (k|η| ≪ 1), the ikη term dies and h freezes to H/Mpl — a fossil. We still see that fossil today.

Because H was nearly constant, every logarithmic chunk of wavelengths got the same power:

$$ \sum\langle h_{ij}^2\rangle \simeq \int \frac{dk}{k}\, \frac{8}{M_{\rm pl}^2}\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^2 $$

We call the power per log-$k$ the tensor power spectrum Pt(k)=At(k/k*)nt with

$$ A_t = \frac{8}{M_{\rm pl}^2}\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^2,\quad n_t = -2\epsilon_H,\quad r = \frac{A_t}{A_s}=16\epsilon_H $$

As is the scalar seed we already measured: As≈2.1×10⁻⁹. Current $B$-mode maps say r < 0.036. That caps At<7.6×10⁻¹¹ and H <1.9×10⁻⁵ Mpl ≈4.7×10¹³ GeV — about a billion times the 14 TeV reached at CERN's Large Hadron Collider. That's why finding r is finding ultra-high-energy physics. A beloved model, Starobinsky's $R^2$ inflation (adding $R^2/6M^2$ to Einstein's action), predicts r=12/N²≈0.0046 for N≈51 doublings — the next target for Simons Observatory, CMB-S4 and LiteBIRD.

The payoff is huge range. The same vacuum whisper should exist at every frequency, from waves as big as the visible Universe (10⁻¹⁸ Hz) to waves as small as a building (10³ Hz). In Fig. 4 the solid line shows today's energy fraction ΩGW(f)= (1/ρcrit) dρGW/d ln f across 21 decades in f.

No single detector can cover that. Laser interferometers (LIGO, ~100 Hz) caught black holes; LISA in space will hear ~10⁻³ Hz; pulsar clocks feel ~10⁻⁹ Hz wobbles; and the CMB — the whole sky as a detector — feels ~10⁻¹⁸ Hz. The apparent gap between CMB and pulsars could even be filled by tiny distortions of the CMB spectrum. Play with the lab below: move r and nt and watch the vacuum line rise, fall and tilt, and see where the Starobinsky dot sits relative to the current bound.

007Lesson 7 — new physics II sourced~14 min

Can fields forge their own ripples?

The simplest story of the early Universe is beautifully empty. Space itself jitters quantum-mechanically, and exponential expansion stretches that jitter to billion-light-year ripples. On its own that gives a gentle, almost flat gravitational-wave power spectrum: the same strength at every wavelength, left-handed and right-handed ripples equal, and the statistics Gaussian — like random static. In equations that story is hp,k → i H/Mpl e-ikη/k3/2 (1+ikη) freezing when k|η| ≪ 1, giving 𝒫t=At(k/k*)nt with nt=-2εH <0 and r=At/As=16εH.

But what if 'stuff' was there too? Normally it cannot help much. If the background Universe looks the same in every direction — isotropic — a mathematical rule called scalar-vector-tensor decomposition says different kinds of wiggles evolve independently at linear order. A plain light-like field, for example, would only make gravitational waves at second order through its energy Ttij ~ EiEj+BiBj. Worse, a free massless vector is conformally coupled: it does not feel expansion, so E ∝ a-2 and B ∝ a-2 dilute away. You need to break that conformal invariance to keep the field alive.

A useful analogy: imagine three violin strings tuned to the same note. If you pluck them separately you get a faint hum. If you pour energy into one string exponentially, its overtones can make the whole violin body shake at second order — unless you bolt a weight to the bridge so the body shakes directly.

The paper shows two ways a pseudoscalar χ can do the pouring, using the same Chern-Simons coupling that gave birefringence: -(α/4f) χ F F̃. In the Abelian U(1) example — a spectator axion that does not drive inflation — the mode equation becomes

$$A''_{\pm}+\left[k^{2} \mp \frac{2k\xi}{(-\eta)}\right]A_{\pm}=0, \quad \xi\equiv \frac{\alpha \dot\chi}{2fH}$$

with ξ>0. Then ω+2=k2-2kξ/(-η) can turn negative: the + helicity grows like eπξ. The exact growing solution is a Whittaker function. Those amplified fields then source tensors as □hij ~16πG (EE+BB)TT — still second order, so the result is highly non-Gaussian. And it is chiral. In a simplified version:

$$\mathcal{P}_{t,R}\simeq \frac{4}{M_{\rm pl}^{2}}\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\left[1+8.6\times10^{-7}\frac{H^{2}}{M_{\rm pl}^{2}}\frac{e^{4\pi\xi}}{\xi^{6}}\right],\quad \mathcal{P}_{t,L}\simeq \frac{4}{M_{\rm pl}^{2}}\left(\frac{H}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\left[1+1.8\times10^{-9}\frac{H^{2}}{M_{\rm pl}^{2}}\frac{e^{4\pi\xi}}{\xi^{6}}\right]$$

R dominates over L by more than 200 times. If ξ(t) grows as χ speeds up, the spectrum tilts blue toward small scales — opposite to the vacuum's gentle red tilt.

Non-Abelian SU(2) can cheat isotropy. Take three vectors Aai=aQδia pointing along x,y,z. Rotating space breaks isotropy, but you can undo it with an SU(2) gauge rotation because the adjoint of SU(2) is the same as 3-D rotations SO(3). So a homogeneous background survives, and fluctuations tai source waves linearly: δTtR/L≈ -(2/a)(aQ)·t'R/L+2gAQ2(gAaQ tR/L ∓ k tR/L) and t''R/L+[k2+2/η2(mQξ∓(-kη)(mQ+ξ))]tR/L=0 with mQ=gAQ/H. Amplification now in a band 0.6 mQ < -kη <3.6 mQ.

The result is again chiral, 𝒫t,R/L≈4/Mpl2(H/2π)2[1+Q2/2Mpl2|𝒢|2eπ(mQ+ξ)] with |𝒢R|≫|𝒢L|, and non-Gaussian through self-coupling gA. Because mQ(t) and ξ(t) evolve, the spectrum can be bumpy rather than smooth: in a simplified spectator model

$$\mathcal{P}^{\rm source}_{t,R}(k)=r_{*}\mathcal{P}_{s}(k)\exp\left[-\frac{\ln^{2}(k/k_{p})}{2\Sigma^{2}}\right]$$

For example the AX2 model in the paper has r*=0.15, kp=1011 Mpc-1, Σ=8 peaking where interferometers look, and AX3 has r*=50, kp=106 Mpc-1, Σ=4.8. Both violate all three vacuum predictions: not scale-invariant, not Gaussian, not achiral. That is why testing those three properties once B-modes are found will tell us whether empty space or stuff forged the waves.

Try it yourself: switch between vacuum and the two sourced models, crank up ξ or mQ and watch the right-handed bar shoot up by hundreds, the non-Gaussian meter turn red, and the spectrum change from flat to blue or to a peaked bump you can slide across 16 orders in k.

008Lesson 8 — finale~12 min

The next decade of polarized discovery

Stretch space itself, and light stretched with it gets a little hotter in one direction and colder in the other. That's how a gravitational wave makes polarization.

Picture the paper's Fig.7: a plus-polarized wave passing across the sky stretches distances on the x–y plane — longer in x, shorter in y (area stays the same). Photons riding that patch have their wavelengths stretched and squeezed the same way. Around an electron that looks like two hot spots and two cold spots opposite each other — a quadrupole, the grey and white balls in Fig.8.

When the electrons last-scattered those quadrupoles toward us, we see a pattern that depends on viewing angle. Look straight along the wave and the little polarization sticks line up parallel or perpendicular to the wave direction — that's an E-mode. Peek 45° away and the same physics looks tipped by ±45° — a B-mode. The real sky is a sum of many such waves going every which way. Their combined B power, $C_\ell^{BB}$, has two tell-tale bumps: a reionization bump at $\ell \lesssim 10$ (very large angles, light re-scattered at redshift $z\sim 10$) and a recombination bump at $\ell \approx 80$.

How do we tell that from cosmic birefringence? It rotates an existing E into B: $C_\ell^{BB,\mathrm{o}} = \sin^{2}(2\beta)\, C_\ell^{EE}$. $C_\ell^{EE}$ has tall acoustic peaks out to $\ell \sim 600$ (Lesson 3), so birefringence makes B that traces the EE shape and dominates at $\ell \gtrsim 200$. A primordial wave, whether from empty-space vacuum or from axion-gauge fields, makes bumps at low $\ell$.

The paper's Fig.5 puts them side by side after subtracting lensing. Red dashed is birefringence with $\beta =0.3^{\circ}$. Orange dashed is a sourced gravitational wave from an SU(2) spectator axion plus the vacuum of Starobinsky $R^{2}$ inflation — the example chosen has $r_{*}=0.023$, $k_{\mathrm{p}}=3.4\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}$, $\Sigma=1.1$ and boosts the low-$\ell$ reionization bump by a factor of several. Green is the sum. The bands are what a full-sky, nearly noiseless experiment like LiteBIRD would see ($\ll 5\,\mu\mathrm{K}$ arcmin, $f_{\rm sky}=1$).

Where are we today? No primordial $B$ yet: $r < 0.036$ (95% C.L.), so vacuum amplitude $A_{\mathrm{t}} < 7.6\times10^{-11}$. Lensing $B$ has been seen (Fig.2) and the scalar $E$ and temperature match single-field slow-roll ($n_{\mathrm{s}}-1 \simeq -0.035$). But there is a $3\sigma$+ hint of parity breaking: $\beta =0.33^{\circ}\pm0.10^{\circ}$, the same at 30 GHz and 353 GHz ($n=-0.35^{+0.48}_{-0.47}$), which would rule out a pure cosmological constant $\Lambda$ and point to a slowly rolling pseudoscalar $\chi$ — favoured by recent Swampland quantum-gravity ideas, and possibly oscillating today if $\chi$ is dark matter.

To go from hint to discovery we need angle calibration much better than today's $\sim0.5^{\circ}$. Birefringence itself and the spurious $B=\sin^{2}(2\alpha)C_\ell^{EE}$ from a miscalibration angle $\alpha$ look identical. The requirement for $B$ science is $\alpha \lesssim 0.2^{\circ}$, ideally $\pm0.05^{\circ}$ (3 arcmin), otherwise we must again borrow the Galactic foreground to tell $\alpha$ from $\beta$. For tensors we need the same precision to test whether the wave is chiral ($C_\ell^{EB}\neq0$) and non-Gaussian, and we need end-to-end simulations that keep those tests in mind.

Space and ground are complementary: space sees the $\ell \lesssim 10$ reionization lever — which also gives tomography because those late photons traveled less and suffered less rotation — ground sees the $\ell \gtrsim 200$ birefringence tail. Put together with pulsars and interferometers across 21 decades in frequency (Fig.4), the shape and handedness of $\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)$ could become the ultimate Voyage 2050 test of where our Universe came from. Try it yourself: can you make a sky where you'd need both a satellite and a mountaintop to be sure?

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)

The leftover heat from the fireball early Universe, now a cold sea of microwave light at average temperature T0 = 2.725 K in every direction. Its tiny temperature flickers ΔT/T ~ 10^-5 show the baby picture of structure.

Polarisation

Direction the electric field of a light wave wiggles. Sunlight reflecting off a car windshield becomes linearly polarized — it wiggles mostly in one plane.

Thomson scattering

Light bouncing off free electrons. Formula is dσ/dΩ = 3σT (ε·ε')² /8π. In the early Universe it made CMB polarization only when light around the electron was anisotropic.

Stokes parameters Q and U

Two numbers that fully describe linear polarization: Q = |Ex|²-|Ey|² and U = 2Re(Ex*Ey). Combined as Q ± iU = P e^{±2iβ} where P is strength and β is the plane angle.

E-mode

The part of CMB polarization with even parity — stretches look parallel or perpendicular to the wave direction. From sound waves in the hot early soup.

B-mode

The part of CMB polarization with odd parity — stretches look tipped by ±45° to the wave direction. Not made by ordinary density ripples; lensing makes some, primordial gravitational waves would make more.

Parity

Mirror test: do laws look the same after flipping spatial coordinates (x→-x). E is even, B is odd; a mixture EB or TB means parity is broken.

Angular power spectrum Cℓ

The strength of wiggles at each angular size. Cℓ^EE = 1/(2ℓ+1) Σ|Eℓm|² and similarly Cℓ^BB, Cℓ^EB, Cℓ^TB. Big ℓ = small angles. Observed EE shows sound-wave peaks; BB is so far only from gravitational lensing.

Cosmic birefringence angle β

Space filled with dark matter/dark energy acting like a crystal that slowly turns the plane of CMB linear polarization by angle β as photons travel 13-14 billion years from last scattering (z≈1090).

Chern-Simons coupling and axion-like field χ

What makes birefringence: a pseudoscalar field χ (like pion or axion) coupled to light as LCS = -(α/4f) χ F·tildeF where F·tildeF = -4 B·E is parity-odd. α is coupling, f is energy scale. If χ changes in time/space, β = α/2f [χ(today)-χ(last-scattering)] in direction n̂.

Galactic foreground

Microwave glow from dust and synchrotron in our own Galaxy. Its polarization is rotated only by instrument error α, while CMB is rotated by α+β, letting us separate the two. Its own EB can mimic β if we ignore it.

Cosmic inflation

Period where space expanded exponentially, a(t) ∝ exp[∫H dt], with slow-roll parameter εH = -Ḣ/H² <<1 stretching microscopic quantum wiggles to billions of light years.

Primordial gravitational waves, r and nt

Ripples of spacetime itself. Vacuum fluctuations give scale-invariant power Pt(k)=At(k/k*)^nt, At = 8/Mpl² (H/2π)², nt = -2εH, tensor-to-scalar ratio r = At/As. Observed As≈2.1×10⁻⁹, r <0.036 (95% C.L.) → H<1.9×10⁻⁵ Mpl≈4.7×10¹³ GeV. Starobinsky R² inflation predicts r≈12/N²≈0.0046 for N≈51.

GW energy spectrum ΩGW

Present-day fractional energy per log frequency ΩGW(f)=1/ρcrit dρGW/d ln f. Inflation predicts it across ~21 decades in frequency — probed by CMB, pulsars/stars, and laser interferometers like LISA.

Chirality and non-Gaussianity

Gravitational waves where right- and left-handed (helicity ±2) amplitudes differ. Gives non-zero Cℓ^EB. Normal vacuum gives equal amplitudes; sourced models give chiral, non-Gaussian, non-scale-invariant signals.
