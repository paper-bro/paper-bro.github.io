001The big shift~5 min

Evaluators have skin in the game too.

Imagine you spend weeks memorizing test-taking shortcuts for a math exam, or adding trendy buzzwords to a resume so a hiring bot picks it up. Who gets criticized when that happens? Almost always the test-taker. People call it gaming the system or cheating.

For years, computer scientists studying algorithms and fairness modeled testing as a two-player game. One player is the decision subject—the student, job applicant, or athlete trying to score well. The other player is the evaluator—the school, company, or organizer running the test.

In that two-player picture, researchers quietly assume the evaluator is a neutral referee whose test reflects fair, public goals. Under that assumption, any clever workaround by a candidate looks shady. Why else would someone try to beat the test?

In a 2023 paper, Benjamin Laufer, Jon Kleinberg, Karen Levy, and Helen Nissenbaum argue that this two-player picture misses half the story. Real evaluators have skin in the game too. A company interviewing for "cultural fit" may favor applicants who remind managers of themselves, even if that undermines fair hiring. A university handing out easy A's—known as grade inflation—can earn happier teaching reviews and bigger alumni donations, while students gladly take the higher grades.

To capture these clashing incentives, the authors add a third player to the model:

🧑

Decision Subject

The person being judged—trying to win a job, grade, or medal using their available effort.

🏛️

Evaluator

The institution designing the test—balancing its official task against its own interests like prestige, donations, or comfort.

⚖️

Society

Laws, regulators, and shared public values—what the evaluation is supposed to uphold for everyone.

The big shift: Once evaluators are treated as strategic players too, a test-taker's clever strategy isn't automatically selfish cheating. Sometimes the institution itself is the one gaming the goal.

002Where tests break~6 min

Three gaps between a test score and the truth.

Suppose you sit beside a highway and count how many yellow cars drive past. You have made an empirical measurement, yet nobody would call that an evaluation. Why? As the paper points out, counting yellow cars records a fact without judging anyone's merit, worth, or significance.

An evaluation takes a measurement and turns it into a value judgment—deciding who gets admitted, hired, or awarded a medal. Because society cannot personally inspect every applicant, it delegates the task to an evaluator such as a school, employer, or sports body. Between what society actually values and the score printed on a test, three separate breaks can happen. The paper calls each break a source of slippage.

😴

1. Subject vs. Test

A test score misses someone's true ability—for instance, a strong math student sleeps badly before an exam, or a test-taker spends energy on test-taking tricks without learning the subject.

🎯

2. Aim vs. Design

No practical test reads minds directly. Evaluators rely on a proxy—a measurable clue standing in for a murky quality like "grit" or "merit"—which adds random noise or unintended bias.

🧭

3. Aim vs. Society

Because evaluators are strategic too, an institution may care about something different from society's goal and build a test that rewards its own preference instead.

Why mixing up the three gaps matters: When an unqualified person passes or a talented person fails, it is tempting to blame the test-taker (Gap 1). Yet flawed proxies (Gap 2) and self-interested evaluators (Gap 3) can scramble outcomes before a single candidate picks up a pencil.

003The formal model~7 min

Four circles on a map of everything you could be.

Suppose you want to judge a student’s skill at multiplying numbers, or a sprinter’s speed in a 100-meter dash. A single test score leaves out almost everything that actually happened—how the student learned the skill, how the athlete trained, and even the conditions on race day. To keep track of all those hidden details, the authors imagine an enormous collection of every possible full description of a person and their situation. They call this the state space, written $S$.

Because states in $S$ record such fine-grained differences, no brief test can read a person’s exact state. Instead, picture $S$ as a giant map with four overlapping regions—called subsets—drawn on top of it:

  • $I_s$ (Society’s goal): The states that genuinely hold the quality society values, such as true math mastery or fair athletic excellence.
  • $I_e$ (The evaluator’s goal): The states the test-maker actually wants to reward, which can diverge from $I_s$ if the institution has its own agenda.
  • $P$ (Passing the test): The states that earn a passing score. Because tests rely on imperfect clues and strategic rules, $P$ need not match $I_s$ or $I_e$.
  • $R$ (Reachable states): The states a person can actually move to. Starting from an initial state $s_0$, you spend a limited budget of time, money, and effort to land at a new state $s_1$. Every state within reach sits inside $R$.

When is it possible for someone to pass the evaluation? Only when at least one state sits inside both what they can reach ($R$) and what passes the test ($P$). In math notation, passing requires:

$$R \cap P \neq \emptyset$$

Here $\cap$ means the intersection (overlap) of two sets, and $\neq \emptyset$ means “is not empty.” Notice which letters are missing from that rule: $I_s$ and $I_e$. A person can pass without landing inside society’s region $I_s$—or even the evaluator’s region $I_e$—just as long as $R$ and $P$ overlap.

Sixteen possible worlds. Pick any state on the map and ask four yes-or-no questions: is it inside $I_s$, inside $I_e$, inside $P$, and inside $R$? Two states that get the exact same four answers are indistinguishable across these four properties and belong to the same equivalence class. With four yes-or-no choices, there are $2^4 = 16$ possible combinations, though some may be empty in practice. Remember that this is a simplified picture: in the paper, $S$ has far more hidden dimensions than a flat screen can show.

004Case study: hiring~7 min

When learning golf is actually fighting bias.

Picture a strong law school graduate applying to an elite law firm. First, she clears an automated resume screener. Next comes the face-to-face interview—where the conversation drifts to a recent golf tournament, and candidates who seem at home on the course get the offer. Most research on hiring fairness stops at that first algorithmic screening stage, leaving later interview stages largely unexamined.

In practice, interviews often judge candidates on “cultural fit.” That sounds harmless, like checking whether someone is easy to work with. Yet sociological studies find that cultural fit frequently acts as a hidden proxy for race, wealth, and class privilege. Because golf has historically been linked to affluent, mostly white, male circles, expecting golf fluency quietly favors applicants from elite backgrounds.

🔍

Two-player view

Assumes the firm's interview is a neutral test of merit. A student who takes golf lessons just to fit in looks like she is gaming the system rather than showing real legal skill.

⚖️

Three-player view

Notices that the firm's preference for wealthy insiders ($I_e$) clashes with society's goal of legal merit ($I_s$). Learning golf becomes a way to push back against an unfair barrier.

That is why some law schools—including a women's golf program at Arizona State University's law school—sponsor golf workshops for students. Participants explained that “golf is an access issue” and they “didn't want to be left out” of career-shaping networking.

On our four-set map, a brilliant graduate from a non-privileged background already sits inside society's target set $I_s$ (strong legal skill), even though she sits outside the firm's insider preference $I_e$. Without golf literacy, she fails the interview ($P$). By taking the school's golf lessons, she uses her effort budget $R$ to step into the overlap $R \cap P$—passing the test without becoming the wealthy insider in $I_e$.

Why this flips the moral story: Strategic preparation isn't automatically cheating. When an evaluator's hidden goal $I_e$ unfairly excludes qualified outsiders, strategic “gaming” by the candidate can pull hiring outcomes closer to society's goal $I_s$.

005Case studies: grades & sports~7 min

When both the test-maker and test-taker win, but society loses.

Usually, we picture a test as a tug-of-war between two rivals: the test-taker tries to clear the bar, while the test-maker tries to keep the bar honest. But what happens when both sides discover they can win by lowering—or bending—the bar together?

Take grade inflation in universities. In a study of 200 U.S. colleges, Rojstaczer and Healy (2012) found that “A” grades jumped from 15 percent of all letter grades in 1960 to 43 percent in 2009—a trend especially strong at private schools. Why doesn’t anyone inside the classroom put a stop to it?

In the three-player model, high grades put students in a sweet spot where reachable effort meets a passing reward ($R \cap P$). Meanwhile, the evaluator wins too: professors earn warmer teaching evaluations (which help with tenure), and colleges see happier alumni who donate more and graduates with shinier GPAs for job applications. So the evaluator stretches its preferred set $I_e$ to include lots of easy-to-reach states.

The loser is society ($I_s$). If nearly everyone walks away with an “A,” grades lose their power to tell employers or graduate schools who truly mastered the material, and students may feel less reason to push their learning. Both the subject and the evaluator prosper while society’s measurement breaks down.

Now look at sports, where the “evaluator” itself splits into rival actors. At the 2021 Tokyo Olympics, meet hosts invested heavily in a springy rubber track surface designed to boost shock absorption and help runners shatter world records. Technology could have pushed the bounce even further, stopping only at safety risks and a notional line separating track running from bouncing across a 400-meter trampoline. Here, local event hosts ($I_e$) wanted thrilling records, while World Athletics ($I_s$) enforced strict material-science limits to protect the sport.

👟

Rule divergence ($I_e$ vs. $I_s$)

When hosts build an overly springy track, or when Nike engineered pacers and wind-blocking conditions for its Breaking2 sub-2-hour marathon, a fast result sits in $P \cap I_e$ yet falls outside official rules $I_s$. Notice the contrast: shoe makers help athletes, while track hosts are supposed to guard the event itself.

⏱️

Reachability limits ($R$)

Under official marathon rules, organizers and World Athletics both agree a sub-2-hour run counts ($P \cap I_e \cap I_s$). So far, though, no runner’s physical budget $R$ has reached it under standard conditions—leaving $R$ as the missing circle.

Even governing bodies feel pressure. Once we leave the two-player view, we don’t have to stop at three players: global bodies like World Athletics also respond to media attention and public expectations when deciding how dramatic an event should be.

006The 8-case grid~6 min

Eight ways any test can turn out.

Suppose a hiring test produces a surprising result: a brilliant candidate walks out without an offer, or an unqualified applicant lands the job. Before deciding who is to blame, you can ask three simple yes-or-no questions about the candidate’s state at the moment they are judged ($s_1$):

  1. Does passing serve society? ($s_1 \in I_s$ — the candidate truly has the skills society cares about.)
  2. Does passing serve the evaluator? ($s_1 \in I_e$ — hiring them fits what the firm privately wants.)
  3. Does the candidate actually pass? ($s_1 \in P$ — they clear the test.)

In the full model, there is a fourth question about reachability ($R$). If we temporarily set reachability aside, three yes-or-no answers give $2^3 = 8$ combinations. Instead of guessing what can go wrong in an evaluation, those eight rows mechanically list every possible story.

To make all eight rows concrete, the paper uses a deliberately extreme, stylized version of the elite law-firm example: imagine a firm that privately wants affluent applicants who grew up playing golf ($I_e$), even though society only wants strong lawyers ($I_s$), and tests candidates by inviting them to play a round of golf during the interview ($P$).

Society ($I_s$)Evaluator ($I_e$)Passes ($P$)Example scenario in the stylized golf interview
YYYStrong record and grew up playing golf; passes the interview.
YYNStrong record and grew up playing golf; fails after getting sick on interview day.
YNYStrong record and hadn’t played golf; passes thanks to a law-school golf program.
YNNStrong record and hadn’t played golf; fails from few opportunities to network.
NYYWeak record and grew up playing golf; passes after networking over golf.
NYNWeak record and grew up playing golf; fails due to a positive drug test.
NNYWeak record and hadn’t played golf; passes after lying about her experience.
NNNWeak record and hadn’t played golf; fails the interview.

Splitting outcomes into these eight boxes gives you two separate diagnostic tools. First, look for rows where $I_s$ and $I_e$ disagree—one is Yes and the other is No. Those mismatches reveal evaluator bias: the institution is rewarding or penalizing traits that clash with society’s values.

Second, look for rows where $I_e$ and $P$ disagree. Since the evaluator designs the test itself, any gap between what it wants and who passes (without outside rules forcing it) points to test noise or error—like an applicant getting sick or slipping through by lying. In a good evaluation, passing matches society’s goal directly: $s_1 \in P$ if and only if $s_1 \in I_s$.

007Ethics & oversight~6 min

Why blaming the test-taker isn't enough.

Words like cheating, gaming the system, and honest effort carry moral blame, and we usually aim that blame straight at the person taking the test. Even nuanced rules in computer science—like the test from Miller and colleagues (2020), which labels an action improvement if it directly causes the measured skill to rise and gaming otherwise—still treat the evaluator's target as fixed and automatically fair.

Once you recognize that evaluators also act strategically, that assumption falls apart. When an institution's real goal ($I_e$) drifts away from society's goal ($I_s$), calling a test-taker's workaround "gaming" misses who wrote the unfair rules. You cannot judge the ethics of a subject's strategy without first checking whether the evaluator's aims are legitimate.

🔍

Open divergence

Nike's Breaking2 marathon openly advertised its rotating pacers and wind-blocking formations. Because those changes to $I_e$ were transparent, everyone could see why the resulting times did not count as official world records under $I_s$.

🌫️

Covert misalignment

Opaque processes like "holistic review" in college admissions can hide self-serving goals—such as favoring friends of trustees—behind excuses about unavoidable measurement noise or contested ideas of merit.

Secrecy also flips accountability upside down. Legal scholars Cofone and Strandburg (2019) point out that institutions often cite potential "gaming" by applicants as a reason to keep scoring rules secret, silently assuming their own goals match society's. When $I_e$ stays hidden, an evaluator can disguise biased gatekeeping as ordinary test error.

Two open questions from the paper: Moving beyond three players, the authors point to a vertical expansion—asking who watches the watchperson when every evaluator may need its own outside auditor—and a horizontal expansion—unpacking "society" ($I_s$) from one neat set into multiple, competing public values.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Strategic behavior

Changing your habits, appearance, or resume to get a better score on a test or evaluation.

Gaming the system

Boosting your test score without actually getting better at the real skill the test is supposed to measure.

Decision subject

The person being scored or judged by a test, algorithm, or interview.

Evaluator

The person or institution—like a company, university, or race organizer—that designs and runs the test.

Society

The third player in the paper's model: laws, regulators, and shared public values about what a fair evaluation should achieve.

Slippage

A gap between what an evaluation actually rewards and the deeper quality society wanted to measure.

Proxy

An easy-to-measure clue (like golf etiquette or test speed) used as a stand-in for a hard-to-measure quality (like legal talent).

State space ($S$)

The huge collection of all possible full descriptions of a subject, written as $S$ in the paper.

Reachable set ($R$)

The set of states a subject can actually move to from their starting state $s_0$ given their time, money, and effort budget.

Equivalence class

A group of states that look identical in whether they belong to each key set ($I_s$, $I_e$, $P$, and $R$).

Grade inflation

A trend where average course grades rise over time even when student mastery has not risen to match.

Cultural fit

How well a job candidate blends into a company's social norms and habits—often used in interviews in ways that favor privileged backgrounds.
