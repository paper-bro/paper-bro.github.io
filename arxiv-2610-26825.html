001Why does this matter?~6 min

1. AI That Forgets vs AI That Remembers

Imagine you hire an assistant to plan exhibitions. You tell them "last week I went to Suzhou to set up a show and left my blue sample book with Chen Wei." A week later you ask "who has my sample book?" What should they do?

Today's big AI models have two kinds of memory, and neither is ideal for this.

The first is textual memory: just paste all the old chat back into the prompt every time. If your history is $N$ tokens long, the work and storage grows like $O(N)$ — that just means "proportional to $N$". Double the history, double the cost. The models in the paper hit a hard wall at about 256,000 tokens and then literally throw away the oldest text. That's why long conversations get slow, expensive, and forgetful.

The second is the model's weights — the numbers learned during training. Call the weights at turn $t$ by the symbol $\theta_t$. The weights know general facts, but they are frozen after training and don't automatically update when you tell them something new in chat.

Memory-native evolution = make remembering built-in. The paper's bet is that remembering should be an intrinsic capability, like reasoning or seeing images — not an add-on where we keep pasting text. The model itself should maintain a tiny evolving state.

MemT-1.0 tries the other path: native memory. Instead of keeping the original text, it learns to squeeze each turn into a fixed-size set of numbers called $M_t$. At the next turn it answers only from that state, plus your new question — no old chat pasted. For a fixed setup the size never grows with $N$, so it's $O(1)$: constant.

The payoff the authors actually measured for their small model (MemT-1.0-Nano, built on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B) at 128,000 tokens of history: about $1.86\times$ faster end-to-end and about $66\times$ smaller stored state per conversation than keeping the full KV cache (the cache of old keys and values that full-context needs), while still answering without seeing the original text.

In the lab below, try both strategies yourself. Pick a fact to remember, drag history longer, and watch when pasting overflows and why a constant-size memory matters. Then switch modes and see what stays flat.

002A quick foundation~7 min

2. The Stage: Turns, Blocks, and Hidden States

Think of a chat as a play in acts. Each message you send starts a new act, called a turn. The paper labels the turn number as $t = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ Inside one turn, the model writes one word at a time. The $k$-th word in turn $t$ is $y_{t,k}$.

That gives two short formulas. First, how it writes the next word:

$$ y_{t,k} \sim P(\cdot \mid X_t, Y_{t,<k}; \theta_t) $$

This just says: pick $y_{t,k}$ from a probability $P$ that looks at your new prompt $X_t$, the words already written in this turn $Y_{t,<k}$, and the current dials inside the model $\theta_t$. Think of $\theta_t$ as all the numbers that define the model at turn $t$.

Second, how those dials can evolve after the turn:

$$ \theta_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid X_t, Y_t; \theta_t) $$

After it finishes the whole answer $Y_t$, the model may change to $\theta_{t+1}$ for next turn. In a normal model $\theta_t$ stays frozen. In MemT-1.0 part of $\theta_t$ will grow a memory.

Current AI pastes the whole script each time: $S_{t,k} = X_t || Y_{t,<k}$ plus all old turns. MemT wants $\theta_t$ itself to remember, so it does not need to re-read the script.

Inside one turn, writing works like an assembly line of $L$ Transformer blocks. Your words first become numbers:

$$ S_{t,k} \rightarrow \mathbf{H}^{(0)}_{t,k} $$

where $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}$ is the first hidden state, a table of size $L_{t,k} \times d$ (tokens by hidden width $d$). Then each block updates it:

$$ \mathbf{H}^{(l)}_{t,k}=\text{TransformerBlock}(\mathbf{H}^{(l-1)}_{t,k}) \quad \text{for } l=1 \dots L $$

Finally the last table $\mathbf{H}^{(L)}$ is turned into probabilities over every word in the vocabulary $|\mathcal{V}|$ with a softmax to pick $y_{t,k}$.

A single block is also two steps. First it looks around:

$$ \mathbf{A}^{(l)}_{\text{ori},t,k}=\text{Attention}(\text{Pre-Attention}(\mathbf{H}^{(l-1)}_{t,k})) $$

Then it mixes that look back in:

$$ \mathbf{H}^{(l)}_{t,k}=\text{Post-Attention}(\mathbf{A}^{(l)}_{t,k}, \mathbf{H}^{(l-1)}_{t,k}) $$

MemT-1.0 keeps all of this — Attention, Pre-Attention, Post-Attention — exactly as it was in the backbone and frozen. For MemT-1.0-Nano the backbone is Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (40 layers, width 2048); for MemT-1.0-Flash it is a prepared DeepSeek-V4-Flash (43 layers, width 4096). The trick is it adds a second lane right next to the original one, which we open in the next lesson.

003The core trick~8 min

3. Adding a Parallel Memory Lane

Imagine a highway that splits into two parallel lanes and merges again. One lane looks only at the cars directly around you — your current sentence. The other lane reads from a notebook you’ve been scribbling in the whole trip.

That is the core trick of MemT-1.0. For each of a few chosen Transformer blocks (10 layers for Nano, 20 for Flash), it keeps the original frozen lane and adds a parallel memory lane:

$$ \mathbf{A}^{(l)}_t = \mathbf{A}^{(l)}_{\text{ori},t} + \mathbf{A}^{(l)}_{\text{mem},t} $$

Here $\mathbf{A}_{\text{ori}}$ is the normal attention — "what does the current text say?" And $\mathbf{A}_{\text{mem}}$ is $\operatorname{MemoryAttention}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{(l)}_t ; \mathbf{M}^{(l)}_t)$ — "what can I pull from memory using the current text as a query?" The input $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ is just the hidden state after pre-normalization, the same starting point both lanes share. After adding, the result goes through the normal post-attention work unchanged.

Two kinds of memory, clearly separated. Local memory $\mathbf{M}_t^{(l)}$ is the notebook itself — a small array of numbers that changes every turn, size $H_{kv} \times d_k \times d_v$. It starts as all zeros at the start of a new chat. Hyper memory $\phi$ is everything the model learned during training about how to write and read that notebook: the projection matrices $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V$ and the gate weights. The big backbone $\theta_b$ stays frozen.

How big is that notebook? For Nano it’s 16 heads of $256 \times 256$ numbers on each of 10 layers — about 10 million numbers, ~40 MiB. For Flash it’s 64 heads of $512 \times 512$ on 20 layers — about 336 million numbers. Tiny compared to pasting 128,000 tokens of history, and it never grows even if the chat does.

Writing to it and reading from it are two separate jobs. Storage happens once per turn after you finish answering. Utilization happens token-by-token while you generate the next answer. Let’s zoom into utilization — the read path:

  1. Ask: $\mathbf{Q} = \operatorname{L2Norm}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}} \tilde{\mathbf{W}}_Q)$. Your current hidden state is turned into a query.
  2. Retrieve: $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}_t / \sqrt{d_k}$. Multiply that query by the notebook matrix. This is like looking up "which stored notes match my query?" Then $\operatorname{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{R})$ steadies it.
  3. Gate: $\mathbf{G} = \tilde{\mathbf{H}} \mathbf{W}_{g/d} \mathbf{W}_{g/u}$. From the same hidden state, make a gate that decides how much of the retrieved memory to trust for this token.
  4. Merge: $\mathbf{A}_{\text{mem}} = \operatorname{Concat}_h[ \operatorname{RMSNorm}(\mathbf{R}_h) \odot \operatorname{SoftCapSiLU}(\mathbf{G}_h) ]$ across all heads $h$.
What does SoftCapSiLU do? First $\operatorname{SiLU}(x)=x \cdot \sigma(x)$ where $\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})$ — a smooth step between 0 and 1. Then $\operatorname{SoftCapSiLU}(x)=\tau \tanh(\operatorname{SiLU}(x)/\tau)$ with $\tau=2$ by default. In plain words: it lets through helpful memory smoothly, but caps it at $\pm 2$ so one wild memory can’t blow up the answer. The $\odot$ means multiply element-by-element.

004Making the update safe~8 min

4. The Stable Token-by-Token Writer

Imagine a notebook you never rip pages from. Every new word is written by first fading the old ink a little, then erasing a tiny spot, then writing the new word into that spot. Do it 64,000 times and two things can go wrong: the ink blows up into a black smear, or the spot you erased was averaged away before you wrote anything useful.

MemT-1.0 writes exactly like that, but token by token. For each token i in turn t, it first makes a memory key and value from the hidden state tilde H:

$$K_{i} = \text{L2Norm}(\tilde H_{i} W_{K}),\quad V_{i}= \tilde H_{i}W_{V}$$

K is a direction with length 1, V is what you want to store. Three gates decide how to mix it in, all between 0 and 1:

$$\beta_{i}=\sigma(\tilde H_{i}W_{\beta}),\quad \omega_{i}=\sigma(\tilde H_{i}W_{\omega}),\quad \lambda_{i}= -\exp(s)\,\text{Softplus}(\tilde H_{i}W_{f}W_{\lambda}+b_{\lambda})$$

sigma is sigmoid (squashes to 0–1). beta = how much to erase, omega = how much to write, lambda is negative, so D = Diag(exp(lambda)) is a gentle fade slightly below 1 each token — the model’s “forget a little”.

The stable key: U = sqrt(beta) * K. That square root is the safety trick.

With U and D the per-token update is, in a simplified version:

$$\tilde M_{i}= [I - U_{i}^{\top}U_{i}]\, D_{i}\,\tilde M_{i-1} + U_{i}^{\top}(\omega_{i}V_{i})$$

Read it as: fade the old memory by D, erase the spot U by [I - U^T U], then add the new content U^T (omega V). You start with tilde M_0 = M_t and after the last token L_t you get M_{t+1}=tilde M_{L_t} for the next turn.

Why the sqrt(beta)? The older GDN-2 erased with I - K^T (beta*K), which is not symmetric. On some directions it does not just erase — it stretches by a factor 1 + gamma^2, where gamma measures how misaligned erase and write are. Repeated, the memory norm explodes. MemT-1.0 rewrites it symmetrically as I - (sqrt(beta)K)^T(sqrt(beta)K). That acts separately on parallel and orthogonal directions: it contracts along U and leaves the rest unchanged, never expanding. The paper works out that without this fix you need an unnaturally fast fade (half-life ~5 tokens) to stay safe; with the fix you don’t.

How much does it matter? In ablations on MemT-1.0-Nano (average over 11 memory benchmarks, full score 46.97): removing the stable mechanism → 46.43, a small drop of 0.54. Substituting plain GDN → 46.72, drop of 0.25. But switching from token-level to turn-level averaging is huge: 40.98, a drop of 5.99 points. Turn-level computes D = Diag(exp( mean lambda )) and 1/L_t U^T U and 1/L_t U^T (omega V) once per turn. Averaging blurs opposite updates inside the same turn and forgets to let later tokens revise what earlier ones just wrote.

005Making memory bigger~7 min

5. Mixture-of-Storage: Scaling Up

One notebook can only hold so much. If you try to cram a year of chat into a single fixed-size memory, details start to overwrite each other. The paper's answer is to use several notebooks at once and learn how to split thoughts across them.

That is Mixture-of-Storage, or MoS. Instead of one dynamic memory state $M_t^{(l)}$ per layer, you keep $E$ of them:

$$\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}_{t,\text{MoS}}^{(l)} = \{\mathbf{M}_{t,e}^{(l)}\}_{e=1}^{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{E \times H_{kv} \times d_k \times d_v}$$

Each $\mathbf{M}_{t,e}^{(l)}$ is a full memory with its own $H_{kv}$ channels of $d_k \times d_v$ numbers. For MemT-1.0-Nano that is $16 \times256\times256$ per expert across 10 layers. For Flash it is $64 \times512\times512$ across 20 layers. Every expert also gets its own copy of the learnable $\phi$ weights that know how to read and write, so capacity grows in both stored facts and know-how.

How do you decide which expert gets what? With a shared-address router. Learn a small address book:

$$\mathbf{C}^{(l)} = [\mathbf{c}_1,\dots,\mathbf{c}_E]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{E \times d_{\text{addr}}}$$

Think of each $\mathbf{c}_e$ as the address label on expert $e$'s notebook. For the current tokens $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{(l)}$, you compute two sets of routing probabilities with a softmax (a function that turns scores into positive weights that sum to 1):

$$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u,t}^{(l)} = \text{Softmax}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{(l)} \mathbf{W}_{\text{route/u}}^{(l)} \mathbf{C}^{(l)\top}),\quad \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s,t}^{(l)} = \text{Softmax}(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_t^{(l)} \mathbf{W}_{\text{route/s}}^{(l)} \mathbf{C}^{(l)\top})$$

$\boldsymbol{\alpha}_u$ is for utilization (reading), $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_s$ for storage (writing). Both are size $L_t \times E$: one weight per token per expert. They share the same address book $\mathbf{C}$ but learn separate projections $\mathbf{W}_{\text{route/u}}$ and $\mathbf{W}_{\text{route/s}}$, so the model can store a fact with one pattern and read it with another.

Dense, not sparse. In a normal Mixture-of-Experts for speed, the router picks 1 or 2 experts and ignores the rest to save compute. MoS does the opposite: it is dense — every expert is active on every token and the weights decide the share. That makes sense here: the goal is not to save compute but to add memory space.

Reading fuses experts with a weighted sum. Each expert first does its own memory attention $\mathbf{A}_{\text{mem},t,e}^{(l)}$ (the $\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{G}$ gated read from Lesson 3). Then:

$$\mathbf{A}_{\text{mem},t,i}^{(l)} = \sum_{e=1}^{E} \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{u,t,i,e}^{(l)}\, \mathbf{A}_{\text{mem},t,i,e}^{(l)}$$

Writing scales the gates before the per-expert update from Lesson 4: $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t,e}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s,t,e}\odot\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t,e}$ and $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{t,e}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{s,t,e}\odot\boldsymbol{\omega}_{t,e}$ (the forget $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ is left alone). A token with high $\alpha_{s,e}$ erases and writes strongly in expert $e$; with low weight it barely touches it. The update itself is still the stable token-level $\mathbf{U}=\sqrt{\tilde{\beta}}\odot\mathbf{K}$ rule, now run $E$ times in parallel.

Does more notebooks help? The paper tested $E=2$ vs $E=4$ during post-training. At 200 steps the 4-expert model led by only +0.41 points on average (+1.42 on LoCoMo). After more training the gap widened: +2.01 points at 3,400 steps (+3.25 on LoCoMo) and +2.69 points at 5,500 steps (+2.20 on LoCoMo). In a simplified way: extra capacity pays off more once the routers have learned to use it.

006Learning to remember~7 min

6. Training: First Learn Language, Then Learn to Remember

Imagine you’re given a 64,000-word novel but you’re only allowed to see 2,000 words at a time, through a tiny peephole. How do you still tell the story? You learn to write useful notes as you go. That’s the first stage. Later, a teacher who did read the whole novel checks your notes: "with just these notes, can you answer like I do?" That’s the second stage.

MemT-1.0 trains in exactly those two stages, on a total of 34 billion tokens. In simplified terms: 20B tokens for Stage 1 to learn language through memory, plus 14B tokens for Stage 2 to learn to remember. Stage 2 data itself mixes three jobs — keeping language natural, explicitly storing and then using info, and longer-memory tasks — all in the same format the model will use later: first store a context $C_{<t}$, then answer a query $X_t$.

The training goal is a weighted mix of two familiar losses. For each position that should be learned (marked by $m^{(b)}_{t,k}=1$) and total supervised tokens $N_{spt}$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\phi)=\frac{1}{N_{spt}}\sum_{b,t,k} m^{(b)}_{t,k}\,\big[\,\lambda_{KD}\,\ell^{KL}_{t,k} + \lambda_{CE}\,\ell^{CE}_{t,k}\,\big]$$

Here $\ell^{CE}_{t,k} = -\log p_{t,k}(y_{t,k})$ is ordinary next-token cross-entropy: the student’s probability $p$ for the true next token $y$ should be high. $\ell^{KL}_{t,k} = T_d^2 \sum_{v\in\mathcal{K}_{t,k}} \hat{q}_{t,k}(v)\log\frac{\hat{q}_{t,k}(v)}{p^{T_d}_{t,k}(v)}$ is distillation: the full-context teacher’s distribution $q$, renormalized over only its Top-$K=64$ most likely tokens $\mathcal{K}_{t,k}$ at temperature $T_d=1.0$ (so $\hat{q}$ sums to 1 on those 64), should match the student’s $p$. In words: CE says "predict the right word," KL says "copy how the teacher who saw everything would spread its bets."

Two stages, two losses: Continued Pre-Training (CPT) uses only CE, $\lambda_{CE}>0, \lambda_{KD}=0$. A 64K-token packed sequence is cut into consecutive 2K-token segments; within a segment the model does causal language modeling, but earlier segments are visible only as incoming memory $\mathcal{M}_t$. Batch is 64 packs, AdamW learning rate warms to $1\times10^{-4}$ in 600 steps and stays there. Post-training uses only KL, $\lambda_{KD}>0, \lambda_{CE}=0$. The student sees query $X_t$ plus $\mathcal{M}_t$; the frozen backbone teacher sees the real textual history $C_{<t}$ plus the same query, and its Top-64 becomes the target. LR warms to $1\times10^{-4}$ in 500 steps then cosine-decays to $5\times10^{-6}$ over the final 1,000 steps.

Why both? The paper’s ablations on MemT-1.0-Nano make it concrete (average over 11 memory benchmarks is 46.97). Skip CPT and train only post-training: 44.85, a drop of 2.12 points. Skip post-training and keep only CPT: 36.28, a drop of 10.69 points. CPT alone teaches the model to keep language coherent when history is compressed; post-training teaches it to actually recover the right historical detail. You need both, and the bulk of the memory gain comes from the second, teacher-guided stage.

This also explains the paper’s choice to freeze the backbone and only train the memory parameters $\phi$: the backbone already knows language, the memory modules learn how to store and read.

007Speed and size~8 min

7. System Tricks That Keep It Cheap

Keeping every old message sounds safe — until you pay for it. In normal chat AI, to answer you the model keeps a KV cache: a saved copy of the math for every past token so it doesn't have to re-read everything. If your history doubles, that saved copy doubles too. We write that as O(N), "grows with N".

MemT-1.0 chooses the opposite trade. It keeps a fixed-size memory state that never grows, written as O(1), "stays constant". The paper makes this precise:

$$ N_{\text{state}} = \sum_{l} E_{l}\, H_{kv,l}\, d_{k,l}\, d_{v,l} $$

In plain words: for each memory layer l, multiply experts E × memory heads H_{kv} × key size d_k × value size d_v, then add across layers. That's the count of numbers stored for one conversation. The shared brain theta_b + phi is the same for everyone; only this M_t is per-trajectory.

Plug in the paper's numbers: MemT-Nano has 10 memory layers, 16 heads, 256×256, no extra experts — 10,485,760 numbers ≈ 40 MiB in FP32. MemT-Flash has 20 layers, 64 heads, 512×512 — 335,544,320 numbers ≈ 1280 MiB. That's it, whether history is 1K or 128K tokens. At 128K, that's about a 66× smaller stored state than keeping the full KV cache.

Why fixed can still be useful: a fixed state can't keep every word perfectly — the paper is honest about this. But because each turn updates the same state, new info gets compressed into what was learned before. Think of it as a notebook you keep rewriting more cleverly, not a photocopier.

Two system tricks that make fixed cheap and fast

1. Chunked updates + snapshot reads. Writing to memory is a chain of tiny rank-1 updates, one per token. Doing them one-by-one is slow on a GPU. MemT groups them with a math trick called chunked-WY into bigger matrix multiplies and triangular solves — same result, much faster. While writing, all queries inside the same turn read the same starting snapshot M_t, so reads can be batched together. Gradients still flow back from later reads to earlier writes, so the model learns what to keep.

2. Windowing for Mixture-of-Storage. With E=4 experts every token would project through all four — that's 4× the live tensors. Instead MemT processes a few complete steps at a time (never cutting a turn in half), finishes their writes and reads, then frees the tensors before the next window. Smaller window → less VRAM, a bit more launching overhead.

The paper measures this on one MoS block with a 32K-token pack. Whole-pack execution peaks at 47.83 GiB. With a 1-step window (2048 tokens) it drops to 6.03 GiB (87.4% saved) for +6.6% time. With a 2-step window (4096 tokens) it drops to 9.49 GiB (80.1% saved) for only +1.6% time. You choose the tradeoff.

What that means for waiting and paying

Because Nano doesn't scan history at query time, its time to first token (TTFT) stays flat around 104–112 ms even at huge histories. Full-context TTFT grows with history: about 257 ms at 128K and 445 ms at 256K. End-to-end (preparing history + generating 128 tokens) is about even at 32K, then MemT pulls ahead: 1.20× at 64K, 1.86× at 128K, 3.99× at 256K on BEAM with a single request on two GPUs. If you reuse a prepared state for many queries, that upfront preparation cost gets amortized.

In short: O(N) storage and rising latency vs O(1) storage and flat latency — with a window knob to trade VRAM for speed.

008The payoff~8 min

8. What MemT-1.0 Can (and Can't) Do Yet

So does the built-in memory actually work? On 11 memory tests — from short chats under 16K tokens to huge ones over 64K — MemT-1.0 is the best of the parametric models, the ones that answer without seeing the old text again. MemT-1.0-Flash averages 50.6 and the smaller Nano 47.0. The previous best parametric, Temp-LoRA, only gets 38.1. That's a lead of 8.9 to 12.5 points.

But there's still a gap to the systems that do paste history back in. The top textual system, MemOS, averages about 68.6. That gap gets bigger on the longest histories (>64K: LongMemEval-S, MemoryAgentBench, BEAM-1M). On those, Flash falls to ~30.5 and Nano to ~27.9, while textual MemOS stays around 61. A fixed-size state — Nano keeps just 40 MiB (10,485,760 numbers), Flash 1280 MiB — must squeeze more and more turns into the same space. Some detail is lost. The paper calls this the memory fidelity challenge.

What about normal smarts when distracting memories are loaded? The team pre-loaded irrelevant memories (the "noise setting") and re-tested general skills like MMLU-Pro and coding. Flash barely moved: 0.0 change on average, with its biggest win +2.0 on BigCodeBench-Hard. Nano dipped -0.9 on average: it gained +3.3 on IFEval but lost -5.8 on LiveCodeBench-v6. In short, remembering didn't break reasoning, but it's not perfectly invisible either — some tasks feel the noise more.

Key idea: native memory wins among models that remember alone, but fixed size means long, detail-heavy histories still favor pasting text. The trade-off is size and speed (66× smaller, ~1.86× faster at 128K) versus perfect recall.

Where native memory shines is as a reusable skill book. The team turned 41,057 successful coding runs into 1,040 reusable experiences — short guides like "when condition X holds, do Y and verify with Z." For a new bug fix, the agent retrieves a guide and MemT-1.0-Nano writes it into native memory in one separate forward pass, without pasting it into the chat.

On SWE-bench Verified (500 real GitHub issues), that lifted average success from 66.07% with no experience to 69.33% with native memory, matching pasted textual experience (68.87%). Consistency jumped more: tasks solved in all three tries (pass^3) rose from 54.0% to 61.60% — an 8.8-point gain. If the harness injects the guide mid-run when it spots failures, native even edges textual: 69.73% vs 69.13%. On SWE-bench Multilingual (300 tasks, 9 languages) the same pattern held: 60.0% → 62.89% before execution, 63.56% when triggered mid-task.

The catch? Retrieval is still fuzzy — the median similarity gap between the #1 and #5 candidate guides is only 0.029 — so better experience and better picking matter as much as better memory. Future work is about closing the long-history fidelity gap and letting agents collect and consolidate experience on their own, without a hand-built bank.

The model saving its past key-value calculations so it can answer quickly without re-reading. It grows with history length O(N) and needs ~66x more storage than MemT-1.0 at 128K tokens.

Gate

A number between 0 and 1 that decides to erase, write, or slowly forget. MemT-1.0 uses beta (erase), omega (write), lambda (forget).

Mixture-of-Storage (MoS)

Expanding one memory block into E parallel mini-memories (experts). A router decides how much to read/write to each expert.

Routing probability (alpha)

Softmax(H * W_route * C^T) - how strongly the current word connects to each of E experts. There are separate routers for storing and using.

Distillation (KD)

Training by copying a teacher that sees the full history. The loss is KL divergence over the teacher's top K=64 tokens at temperature Td=1.0.

Persistent state size (N_state)

Number of memory numbers kept per conversation: sum over layers of E * Hkv * dk * dv. For Nano it's 10,485,760 numbers (40 MiB), for Flash 335,544,320 (1280 MiB). It does NOT grow with history.
