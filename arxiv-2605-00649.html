001Lesson 1 — The puzzle~8 min

A thousand choices, one wallet

Imagine you have to pack a library into a suitcase. Each shelf is a group — say, one layer of an AI model. For each shelf you can pick one of a few suitcase sizes: 2 bits, 3 bits, 4 bits, all the way to 8 bits. Smaller takes less space but loses more detail.

You have N groups and K choices per group. Written N × K. A real example from the paper: Qwen3-8B has N = 252 layers and K = 7 bitwidths (2 to 8). Another model, Qwen3-Coder-Next, has 512 experts per layer — each expert is a group that can be kept or pruned. The total number of possible suitcases is K to the power N: KN. For 8 layers and 7 choices that's already 5.7 million combos. For 252 layers it's astronomically larger. You can't try them all.

You have one wallet — a total budget B. Each group has a weight wi (how big the layer is, like number of parameters) and each option has a cost ck (like bits per parameter). If you knew exactly which option you picked, your total cost would be sum of wi × cpicked. But while you're searching you keep soft, mixed choices. That's where the paper's formulas start.

They keep a score logit called αik for "how much group i likes option k". Turning logits into probabilities with softmax gives pik — a soft vote that sums to 1 per group. Then the expected cost is:

$$C(\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{ik} c_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \langle p_i, c \rangle$$

In plain words: for each group, take the average cost weighted by its probabilities, multiply by the group's size, and add up. Your wallet is happy exactly when C(α) = B.

And here's what you're trying to minimize: L(α), the model's loss — for example how far its answers drift from the full-size model (measured as KL divergence). The catch: L depends on ALL groups together. Changing layer 1 changes what the best choice for layer 20 is. It's not a sum of separate per-layer scores.

Why that's hard: Dynamic programming (DP) is the classic exact tool for "pick one per group under a budget," but it only works if the goal splits into per-group pieces — a decomposable objective. Real model loss doesn't. So DP can only optimize a fake proxy (like per-layer sensitivity), which breaks at high compression. Evolutionary search does evaluate the real loss, but it's like guessing randomly for hundreds of full model runs with no gradients. And a penalty like λ(C-B)² says "try to be close" — it needs careful tuning of λ, never lands exactly on budget, and wobbles around.

The paper's goal is to optimize the real joint loss with gradients while staying exactly on C = B at every step — no fudge factor, no tuning dial for the budget.

002Lesson 2 — Soft choices~10 min

Making discrete choices smooth

Imagine every light switch in your house was either fully on or fully off, nothing in between. Picking the best combination of on/off for 250 switches would mean trying an impossible number of combos. What if instead each switch became a dimmer you could slide smoothly?

That is exactly what this paper does for AI compression. Instead of a hard pick like “layer 7 is 2 bits, layer 8 is 8 bits,” it gives each layer a set of soft preferences called logits.

A logit, written αik, is just a slider score. i is which group you are looking at (for example, a layer), k is which option in that group (for example, 2 bits, 4 bits, 8 bits). N groups times K options gives N×K sliders in total.

Soft probabilities come from the softmax: it turns those sliders into percentages that add to 100%. Roughly, it exponentiates each slider and divides by the sum, so a higher slider means a higher chance. We write

$$ p_{ik} = \text{softmax}(\alpha_i)_k $$

for the probability group i picks option k. Two nice facts: every pik is always a little above 0% (never truly zero), and if you nudge a logit up a tiny bit, its probability nudges up smoothly too.

Now we can define how expensive a soft mix is. Each group has a size wi — think “how many parameters are in this layer” — and each option has a cost ck — think “bits per parameter”. The paper’s expected-cost formula is:

$$ C(\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{ik}\, c_k $$

In words: for each group, take its average cost — probability times cost, summed over options — then weight by the group’s size and add up all groups. C(α) is the budget in the soft world, and the hard budget is exactly C(α) = B.

Quick number check. Say N=2, w=[100, 200], c=[2, 4] bits, and your sliders give Group A probabilities [0.7, 0.3] and Group B [0.45, 0.55]. Then average bits are 0.7×2+0.3×4=2.6 for A and 0.45×2+0.55×4=3.1 for B. Total expected cost is 100×2.6 + 200×3.1 = 260 + 620 = 880 bits.

For a solution to exist at all, the average budget per parameter B / ∑ wi has to lie strictly between the cheapest and most expensive option: min ck < B/∑wi < max ck. If you ask for 1 bit on average but the cheapest option is 2 bits, no soft mix can get there.

The payoff: hard discrete choices are jumpy and not differentiable, but this expected cost C(α) is smooth everywhere because softmax is smooth. That smoothness is what lets us later use gradients to slide along the budget exactly.

003Lesson 3 — A hidden surface~10 min

The budget as a geometric surface

Imagine you have 100 dials, each can be turned to pick a cheaper or pricier option. You have one rule: the average expected cost must add up to exactly B — not about B, exactly B. That rule slices through the huge dial-space and leaves a smooth, thin surface. Learning to see that surface is the key trick of this paper.

In lesson 2 you learned the setup: for each group i you keep numbers called logits αik. Softmax turns them into probabilities pik = exp(αik) / sumj exp(αij) — always bigger than zero and summing to 1. The expected cost is then

$$C(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{ik}\,c_k$$

Here wi is how big group i is (like how many parameters a layer has), ck is the price of option k (like 2 bits vs 8 bits), and pik is how likely you are to pick it. The budget asks for C(α)=B.

Collect every setting that satisfies the budget and you get a set the paper calls M:

$$\mathcal{M}=\{\,\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^{N K} : C(\alpha)=B\,\}$$

Proposition 1 says: when the cheapest and priciest options are different, M is not jagged or broken. It is a smooth Riemannian manifold — a fancy way of saying “a smooth curved surface where you can measure lengths and angles.” It lives in NK numbers (all logits), but one equation removes one degree of freedom, so its dimension is NK − 1. Like a 2D sheet inside 3D space, or a 1D curve inside a 2D plane.

Why smooth? A surface defined by C(α)−B = 0 stays smooth as long as the gradient ∇C — the arrow that points steepest uphill in expected cost — never becomes zero. The paper computes it in closed form (Proposition 2, Eq. 2):

$$\bigl(\nabla C(\alpha)\bigr)_{ik}= w_i\,p_{ik}\,\bigl(c_k - \mathbb{E}_{p_i}[c]\bigr)$$

Read it piece by piece: for group i and option k, take how far that option’s price ck is from the group’s average price Epi[c] = sumk pikck, multiply by how likely that option is pik, and by the group weight wi. Cheap options (below average) get a negative entry, expensive ones positive.

This vector is normal to the surface — it sticks straight out, pointing in the direction that changes the budget fastest. Moving along it breaks the budget; moving at 90 degrees to it keeps you on the surface to first order. That right-angle direction is the tangent plane.

💡 The magic is that pik > 0 always (softmax never truly hits zero). So each group has a little spread in price, its variance sum pik(ck−E[c])² > 0, and adding them with weights shows ||∇C||² > 0 everywhere. No flat spots, no pinches — the level set is a proper (NK−1)-dimensional surface. If all ck were equal, there would be no surface at all.

Notice the normal never disappears unless you make the two costs equal — that is the geometry that lets the optimizer slide exactly on budget.

004Lesson 4 — How to stay on it~12 min

Three cheap tricks to never leave the budget

Imagine your budget is a tightrope. Every mix of compression choices is a point in space, and only the points that spend exactly $B$ are on the rope. Take a normal optimization step and you wobble off. The paper's trick is three cheap moves wrapped around the optimizer Adam that keep you on the rope every time.

Move 1: project the gradient flat onto the rope. At your current logits $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, the direction that changes cost fastest is the normal vector $\mathbf{n}=\nabla C$. The paper gives it in closed form (Eq.2):

$$ (\mathbf{n})_{ik}= w_i\,p_{ik}\,(c_k - \mathbb{E}_{p_i}[c]) $$

Here $w_i$ is how big group $i$ is (like number of parameters), $p_{ik}$ is its current probability for option $k$, $c_k$ is that option's cost (like bits), and $\mathbb{E}_{p_i}[c]$ is the average cost for that group. Cheap options push one way, expensive the other.

Any raw gradient $\mathbf{g}=\nabla L$ (the direction that would lower loss) is split. The part along $\mathbf{n}$ would change the budget. So we subtract it:

$$ \mathbf{g}_{\text{tan}} = \mathbf{g} - \frac{\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{n}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{n}\|^2}\,\mathbf{n} $$

This is one inner product — super cheap. What's left, $\mathbf{g}_{\text{tan}}$, lies in the tangent plane: the flat that just touches the rope. Moving a tiny $\eta$ along it changes cost only like $\eta^2$, not like $\eta$. Without projection the violation would be $\eta\cdot |\langle \mathbf{n},\mathbf{g}\rangle|$ — first-order big. With projection the first-order term is zero, so the error is second-order tiny and scales with curvature. The paper does this before the Adam update, so Adam's momentum $\mathbf{m}$ only ever stores budget-preserving directions.

Why before Adam? If you project after Adam, momentum would slowly drift toward the forbidden $\mathbf{n}$ direction and be wasted by the next snap-back. Projecting first keeps momentum clean.

Move 2: snap back by sliding along the cost vector. Even a tangent step plus Adam's per-coordinate rescaling nudges you off the curved surface a little. To get back, shift every logit by the same amount $t$ times its cost:

$$ \alpha'_{ik}= \alpha_{ik}+ t\,c_k $$

Why does this always work? Because of a beautiful fact (Proposition 3):

$$ \frac{d}{dt}C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+t\mathbf{c}) = \sum_i w_i\,\text{Var}_{p_i(t)}[c] > 0 $$

$\text{Var}$ is variance — how spread out the costs are under the current probabilities. Since softmax always gives $p_{ik}>0$ and costs aren't all equal, variance is positive. So expected cost goes strictly up as $t$ grows: cheaper $t$ pushes probability to cheap options, larger $t$ pushes to expensive ones. Monotone means for any budget $B$ between cheapest and most expensive there is exactly one $t$ with $C=B$.

Find it with binary search: try middle $t$, check if $C$ is too low or high, halve the interval. Each step halves the error, so it converges exponentially fast. For a bracket width $R=100$ and tolerance $10^{-8}$ theory predicts $\lceil\log_2(R/\varepsilon)\rceil=34$ iterations; the paper measured at most 45 across thousands of calls. One search costs only softmax evaluations.

Move 3: carry momentum with you. After snapping to $\boldsymbol{\alpha}'$, the tangent plane has tilted. Its new normal is $\mathbf{n}'=\nabla C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}')$. Adam's first moment $\mathbf{m}$ must be tilted too, otherwise it would point off the rope again. Since the surface has only one forbidden direction (codimension one), this is another single inner product:

$$ \mathbf{m} \leftarrow \mathbf{m} - \frac{\langle \mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}'\rangle}{\|\mathbf{n}'\|^2}\,\mathbf{n}' $$

Only the first moment is transported; the second moment $\mathbf{v}$ is just per-coordinate scaling with no direction, so it stays. Adam's scaling does leak a little budget violation ($\propto \|D-\bar d I\|$), but the snap-back fixes it exactly each step.

Together: project → Adam step → binary-search retract → transport. No $\lambda$ to tune, one inner product each for project/transport, ~34 softmaxes to retract.

005Lesson 5 — Back to discrete~10 min

Turning probabilities into real assignments

Soft probabilities are great for learning, but you can't ship a model that's "70% 2-bit and 30% 4-bit" on the same layer. You need one hard choice per layer. The puzzle is: how do you keep the smooth world that gives you gradients, and still end up with a real, discrete model that never breaks the budget?

This lesson is the bridge. The paper keeps two budgets at once. In logit space, the expected cost $C(\alpha)=\sum_i w_i \sum_k p_{ik}c_k$ from Lesson 2 is kept exactly on $B$ by the geometry you just saw. In the real world, the discrete cost $\sum_i w_i c_{\text{picked}_i}$ must be $\le B$. RCO makes both true on every step.

Roll dice, then pack the knapsack exactly

Here's the forward pass (the "do" part). For each layer $i$ and each option $k$ (say $c=[2,3,5]$ bits), take its logit $\alpha_{ik}$ and add a little random Gumbel noise $G_{ik}$:

$$\hat{\alpha}_{ik}= (\alpha_{ik}+G_{ik})/\tau$$

Think of $G_{ik}$ as a fair die roll that jitters every option. $\tau$ (tau) is temperature. When $\tau$ is near $1.0$, the jitter dominates and you explore many combos. When $\tau$ cools to $0.01$, the best logit wins almost always and you exploit. The paper anneals $\tau$ exponentially from $1.0$ to $0.01$ over $T$ steps.

Now solve one classic puzzle with those noisy scores:

$$z^* = \arg\max_{z} \sum_{i,k} z_{ik}\hat{\alpha}_{ik}\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\sum_i w_i\sum_k z_{ik}c_k \le B,\;\sum_k z_{ik}=1$$

$z_{ik}$ is $1$ if you pick option $k$ for group $i$, else $0$. This is the Multiple-Choice Knapsack Problem, and unlike the LLM loss, it is decomposable, so Dynamic Programming (DP) solves it exactly in $O(NKB')$ time. $B'$ is just the budget in integer units (total bits, or total experts kept). No penalty, no "close enough" — DP returns a feasible assignment that respects $\le B$ by construction.

Exact vs rounded: $C(\alpha)=B$ is exact in continuous space. When you decode, per-group costs are rounded to that integer grid, so the final shipped model can be off by a sliver — the paper reports e.g. $2.251$ vs $2.25$ bits. That's rounding, not cheating.

Learning through a brick wall

$z^*$ is discrete, so you can't differentiate through it. The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) trick: keep $z^*$ for the forward evaluation, but on the backward pass pretend you had used the soft probabilities $\hat{p}_{ik}=\text{softmax}(\hat{\alpha}_i)_k$ of those same noisy logits. Gradients flow through $\hat{p}$, the bias shrinks as $\tau\to 0$, and because the noise is inside the softmax, each sample gives a different Jacobian.

One sample is very noisy. RCO averages $g$ independent Gumbel samples per step before it updates. Ablations on Qwen3-8B at 2.5 bits show why this matters most:

  • $g=1 \to 4$ cuts perplexity by $0.85$ (biggest single win)
  • $g=32$ for $50$ steps matches $g=16$ for $200$ steps at half the forward passes — diverse samples beat more steps
  • Learning rate $0.05$–$0.2$ and $\tau_{\min}=0.005$–$0.05$ are all fine. $g$ is the knob that moves the needle.

When you're done, throw away the noise and decode once with $\hat{\alpha}_{ik}=\log p_{ik}$. DP cost is cheap: about $14\%$ of a step, and for the 2-option prune/keep case it collapses to a top-$k$ sort in $O(N\log N)$.

In the lab, roll the Gumbel dice yourself. Watch how every discrete pick stays under $B$ thanks to DP, how lowering $\tau$ sharpens the picks, and how averaging more independent samples $g$ steadies the result — exactly the effect the paper measures.

006Lesson 6 — Many budgets at once~10 min

When one budget is not enough

One wallet is hard enough — but real models often have two or three at once. Imagine you must prune 25% of experts and keep the average bits at 2.5, on the same experts. Or you must give every single layer exactly the same expert count so the file can be exported — that's 48 separate wallets, one per layer. A penalty would need a tuning knob $\lambda$ for each wallet, and the knobs fight each other.

RCO handles any number $q$ of wallets with no knobs at all. Write each budget as

$$C_j(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = b_j, \qquad j=1,\dots,q$$

Here $\mathbf{c}_j$ is the cost vector for budget $j$ (for example $\mathbf{c}_1=(1,0,0,0,0)$ for "pruned" and $\mathbf{c}_2=(0,2,3,4,5)$ for bitwidth), $w_{ji}$ is how much group $i$ counts toward budget $j$, and $b_j$ is the target. Each budget has its own normal vector $\mathbf{n}_j = \nabla C_j$ with the same closed form as before, $w_{ji}\,p_{ik}(c_{jk}-\mathbb{E}_{p_i}[c_j])$. Stack them into a matrix $\mathbf{N}=[\mathbf{n}_1 \dots \mathbf{n}_q]$.

The budget-preserving directions are now "perpendicular to all normals at once." The projection that does it is a tiny $q\times q$ solve:

$$\mathbf{g}_{\text{tan}} = \mathbf{g} - \mathbf{N}\,(\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{N})^{-1}\,\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{g}$$

When $q=1$ this is exactly the one-inner-product formula from Lesson 4. When $q=16$, it is one $16\times16$ solve per step — cheap when $q$ is much smaller than the thousands of logits. No $\lambda$ appears.

🧩

Disjoint wallets

Each budget touches different groups. Example: 48 per-layer budgets for GGUF export, 512 experts per layer. Then $\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{N}$ and the retraction matrix are diagonal — 48 independent binary searches, one per layer.

🔗

Shared wallets

Two budgets touch the same groups. Example: sparsity and bitwidth on the same 1024 experts. Then off-diagonal terms appear and you need a coupled 2-D Newton step.

To snap back after an Adam step, RCO shifts each group along all its active costs, $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_i \mapsto \boldsymbol{\alpha}_i + \sum_l t_l\,\mathbf{c}_l$, and finds the vector $\mathbf{t}=(t_1,\dots,t_q)$ with Newton’s method. Its Jacobian has a clean formula too:

$$J_{jl}=\sum_{i} w_{ji}\,\text{Cov}_{p_i}[\mathbf{c}_j,\mathbf{c}_l]$$

Variance on the diagonal, covariance off it. In practice Newton converges in 2–4 iterations. When the budgets are disjoint, $J$ is diagonal and each $t_j$ is found by its own monotone binary search.

What if the best answer is under budget?

Sometimes the cheapest good solution costs less than $B$. Forcing equality would waste budget on worse options — the paper’s "cheap optimal" MCKP test loses 49% this way. The fix is one extra number $s$, a slack variable, with

$$\hat C(\boldsymbol{\alpha},s)=C(\boldsymbol{\alpha})+s^2 = B$$

$s^2$ is always $\ge 0$, so $C(\alpha)$ can be any value $\le B$. If $C>B$ the retraction sets $s=0$ and binary-searches back to $B$; if $C<B$ it sets $s=\sqrt{B-C}$. You get an inequality $C\le B$ without a new hyperparameter.

The payoff: in the joint 2-budget experiment on OLMoE-1B-7B (1024 experts, targets pruned 0.25 and 2.5 bits) RCO held both budgets within $10^{-4}$ with no coefficients. Every penalty $\lambda$ in $\{0.1,1,10\}$ missed both — the best $\lambda=10$ was still 42% short on pruned fraction and gave $1.11\times$ worse KL (paper Table 11). And feasible targets must satisfy $\min c_k < B/\sum w_i < \max c_k$ — outside that range no assignment can reach the budget at all.

Key takeaway: $q$ budgets = one $q\times q$ projection and a $q$-dim Newton retraction per step, zero $\lambda$’s. Disjoint → diagonal → independent searches; shared → coupled Cov matrix; under-budget → add $s^2$.

007Lesson 7 — RCO put together~10 min

The complete Riemannian optimizer

Lessons 3 and 4 gave you the three geometric tools. Lesson 5 gave you the discrete trick. This lesson clicks them together into one loop that never needs a budget penalty knob.

Think of it like staying on a tightrope while juggling. Each step has five beats, always in this order:

  1. Gumbel + DP forward (g times). Add fresh random Gumbel noise $G_{ik}$, make noisy logits $\hat{\alpha}_{ik}=(\alpha_{ik}+G_{ik})/\tau$, and solve the exact knapsack $\mathbf{z}^* = \arg\max \sum z_{ik}\hat{\alpha}_{ik}$ subject to $\sum w_i\sum z_{ik}c_k \le B$. You get $g$ different budget-respecting assignments and their real model loss.
  2. STE gradient. Keep the hard $\mathbf{z}^*$ for the forward value, but let gradients flow through the soft $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \text{softmax}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})$. Average the $g$ gradients.
  3. Tangent projection. Subtract the budget-pushing part: $\mathbf{g}_{tan}=\mathbf{g} - (\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{n}\rangle/\|\mathbf{n}\|^2)\mathbf{n}$, where $\mathbf{n}=\nabla C$ from $w_i p_{ik}(c_k - \mathbb{E}[c])$. Now the gradient is sideways to the budget.
  4. Adam step. Update $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ with Adam using $\mathbf{g}_{tan}$. Adam will drift off the surface a little because of curvature and per-coordinate scaling.
  5. Retraction + transport. Shift $\alpha_{ik} \leftarrow \alpha_{ik}+ t^* c_k$ and binary-search $t^*$ until $C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+t^*\mathbf{c})=B$. Then slide momentum $\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \mathbf{m}-(\langle \mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}'\rangle/\|\mathbf{n}'\|^2)\mathbf{n}'$ into the new tangent plane.

Why does this work even though the STE gradient is biased? Proposition 6 in the paper is the key. Projection deletes the normal component of any bias $\mathbf{b}$: $P_T(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})=P_T(\nabla L)+\mathbf{b}_T$. What is left is only sideways bias. Then a step along $\mathbf{g}_{tan}$ violates the budget only at second order, $|C(\boldsymbol{\alpha}')-B| \le \tfrac{\eta^2}{2}\|\nabla^2 C\|\,\|P_T(\tilde{\mathbf{g}})\|^2$, not first order $\eta|\langle \mathbf{n},\tilde{\mathbf{g}}\rangle|$ like an unprojected step. And because $\langle \mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\rangle=0$ is restored every iteration, momentum can never sneak a budget drift across steps. Retraction then erases that tiny $O(\eta^2)$ leftover exactly.

No $\lambda$ for any number of budgets. A penalty needs one $\lambda$ per budget, and the paper shows one shared $\lambda$ can miss one budget by 42% while nearly hitting the other. RCO's projection is $\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{N})^{-1}\mathbf{N}^{\top}\mathbf{g}$ — one tiny $q\times q$ solve, zero coefficients.

What do you still tune? Only normal optimization knobs: how many Gumbel samples $g$, number of steps $T$, lowest temperature $\tau_{min}$, and learning rate $\text{lr}$. The ablations say $g$ matters most: going $g=1\to4$ cut FineWeb perplexity by 0.85, while $g=32$ with 50 steps matched $g=16$ with 200 steps at half the forwards. $\text{lr}=0.05$ to $0.2$ is robust, and $\tau_{min}=0.01$ is best (too low locks in too early). Initialization is free: start from uniform $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_0=\mathbf{0}$ or from a prior like expert frequency — one retraction puts you on $\mathcal{M}$ before step one. Discrete decoding cost is linear in $N$: about 270 ms for 144 groups and 1783 ms for 1,040 groups in the paper's Python DP, and $O(N\log N)$ top-$k$ for simple keep/prune. In the paper's expert-pruning Table 13 scale, RCO had 879 of 1,024 experts committed ($p>0.9$) after 200 steps, penalty had at most 5.

Go deeper: Adam's second moment $\mathbf{v}$ is per-coordinate and has no tangent structure, so it isn't transported. Its scaling $D=\text{diag}(1/(\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon))$ can leak a normal piece $\mathbf{m}^{\top}(D-\bar d I)\mathbf{n}$. RCO lets that leak happen and fixes it exactly with retraction every step — cheap because $d/dt\,C=\sum w_i\text{Var}_{p_i}[c]>0$ guarantees monotone binary search in ~34 iterations ($\le45$ observed).

008Lesson 8 — Does it actually win?~12 min

Beating search and penalties — exactly on budget

So does staying exactly on a curved budget surface actually help in the real world — or is it just pretty math? The paper tests the same geometry trick in two very different places: a clean synthetic puzzle where we know the perfect answer, and big real language models where we care about speed and quality.

First the synthetic check: the Multiple-Choice Knapsack. You have $N$ groups, each picks 1 of $K$ options with a cost $c_k$ and a value. The best discrete assignment can be found by dynamic programming (DP), but the paper instead optimizes the smooth expected cost $C(\alpha)=\sum_i w_i \langle p_i,c\rangle$ with $p_i=\text{softmax}(\alpha_i)$, and forces $C(\alpha)=B$ exactly. On the “huge” setup — $N=1000$ groups, $K=32$ options, averaged over three random instances — the result is stark. After 5000 steps:

◎

Manifold (RCO)

0.00% ± 0.00% gap to DP optimum. Mean budget miss <10-8. Reaches within 1% of optimum in 594 steps.

○

Penalties

Lagrangian: 37.56% ± 1.78% gap, miss 1.15, never reaches 1%. Augmented Lagrangian: 4.28% gap, miss 0.12, also never reaches 1% at this scale.

On the log-scale view the manifold’s violation sits near $10^{-9}$ the whole time, while penalty methods hover near $10^{-1}$. That is not a small tuning difference — it is a billion-times stricter. Penalty methods oscillate: the weight $λ$ overshoots, then undershoots, and the paper shows the same pattern on $q=16$ simultaneous budgets where Lagrangian violations blow up to ~10 and Augmented to ~0.1, while the manifold stays at ~10-13.

Now the messy real world. Take Qwen3-8B mixed-precision quantization: 252 layer groups, each chooses among 7 bitwidths (2–8 bits), target average bits $B/\sum w_i$. Here the team keeps the expected-budget trick — the manifold gives an exact $C(\alpha)=B$ every iterate — but decodes to a real model by DP rounding to an integer grid (for example 2.251 vs 2.25 bits). That tiny rounding is the only inexactness, and it is built in on purpose.

💎

Tight budget: 2.25 bits

RCO perplexity 20.47 (FineWeb-Edu). HIGGS proxy+DP: 32.07. EvoPress search: 21.40. RCO cuts HIGGS by 36% and still beats EvoPress, at 62–215 min vs 11–14 hours — about 10× faster.

⏱️

Loose budget: 3.5–4 bits

Proxies already work well here (HIGGS within 3% of RCO). RCO’s edge is no longer quality but wall-clock: same score in minutes instead of hours.

The pattern repeats everywhere they looked. OLMoE-1B-7B at 25% experts pruned: RCO reaches 0.608 average over 8 tasks in 66 minutes, vs EvoESAP 0.581 in 5.8 hours; even 50 steps (about 10 min) already beats EvoESAP at 0.597. Qwen3-30B-A3B at 25%: RCO 71.0 vs EvoESAP 70.0–70.4 vs uniform 70.6. Qwen3-Coder-Next with 512 experts per layer at 50% sparsity: letting RCO distribute pruning freely recovers 97% of HumanEval, while forcing the same count per layer (48 simultaneous budgets needed for GGUF export) recovers only 55%.

Limits — what geometry can’t fix. The paper is honest: RCO is a heuristic, not a proof of optimality. The side-to-side STE bias remains — only the budget-pushing part is removed. Your calibration data chooses what you keep (code data keeps code, general data keeps general; Table 1 shows the trade-off is sharp). And the cheap binary-search only works when costs add up; fused-operator latency needs pricier root-finding.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

Budget manifold M

A smooth surface inside a bigger space that locally looks flat. Here the surface is all logit settings where expected cost exactly equals the budget B. It has dimension NK-1.

Normal vector

The vector perpendicular to the manifold at a point. For this paper it has a closed form: (grad C)_{ik}=w_i p_{ik}(c_k - E_{p_i}[c]). It points in the budget-changing direction.

Tangent plane / tangent space

The flat plane that just touches the manifold at a point. Directions in this plane keep the budget unchanged to first order. The opposite of the normal direction.

Riemannian

Adding structure to a manifold so you can measure lengths and angles on it. Lets you define gradients on the curved surface.

Logits (alpha)

Unnormalized scores a_i for each option k in each group i. Probabilities come from p_i=softmax(alpha_i). The paper optimizes over logits alpha in R^{NK}.

Softmax

Turns logits into probabilities that sum to 1: p_{ik}=exp(alpha_{ik})/sum_j exp(alpha_{ij}). Always >0, which makes the expected-cost manifold smooth.

Expected cost C(alpha)

Smooth cost of soft choices: C(alpha)=sum_i w_i <p_i,c>=sum_i w_i sum_k p_{ik} c_k. w_i is group weight (e.g. parameter count), c_k is option cost (e.g. bits). Budget constraint is C(alpha)=B.

Retraction

Operation that brings a point that drifted off the manifold back onto it. Here: shift all logits by t*c, alpha'_ik = alpha_ik + t c_k, and binary-search t until C=B. Works because d/dt C = sum_i w_i Var_{p_i}[c] >0.

Vector transport

Moving a vector like Adam's momentum from the old tangent plane to the new one after retraction. Because the manifold has codimension 1 it is just another projection: m <- m - (<m,n'>/||n'||^2) n'.

Gumbel Straight-Through Estimator (STE)

Trick to get gradients through discrete choices. Forward: sample Gumbel noise, solve discrete assignment with DP. Backward: pretend the discrete choice was the soft softmax of the noisy logits so gradients flow. Temperature tau is annealed 1.0 -> 0.01.

Dynamic Programming (DP)

Exact algorithm for budget-constrained assignment: pick one option per group to maximize sum of scores while sum w_i c_{choice} <= B. Multiple-Choice Knapsack Problem. Runs in O(N K B') where B' is integer budget. For 2 options {0,1} it is just top-k.

Lagrangian / penalty method

Classic penalty method for constrained optimization: minimize L + lambda (C-B)^2. Needs tuning of lambda, never hits budget exactly, oscillates. Needs one lambda per budget.

Slack variable

Extra variable s added to turn inequality C <= B into equality C + s^2 = B. Lets the optimizer leave budget unused when optimum is under budget.

Adam optimizer

Standard adaptive optimizer that keeps running averages m (first moment) and v (second moment) of gradients. RCO wraps projection, retraction and transport around an Adam step with no constraint hyperparameters.
