001Lesson 1 — The Problem~4 min

Why translation was stuck waiting in line

Imagine translating a sentence on an assembly line. A worker can only handle one word after the previous worker is done. That was machine translation until 2017.

The best translators back then used RNNs — short for recurrent neural networks. Think of an RNN as a very careful reader with a notepad. For each word at position t, it writes a new note ht using two things: the note it just wrote ht-1 and the new word xt:

$$h_t = \text{function}(h_{t-1}, x_t)$$

In plain words: you cannot write today's note until yesterday's note is finished. If your sentence has n words, you must do n steps in order. Computers call this inherently sequential.

Key idea: sequential = waiting in line. Even on a super-fast GPU, you can't make step 5 start before step 4 ends. That's a traffic jam inside the computer.

That jam hurts in two ways. First, training is slow — you can't split work across the GPU within one example. The paper notes this "becomes critical at longer sequence lengths, as memory constraints limit batching across examples." Second, long sentences fade: a word at the end has to travel through n notes to reach the beginning, so long-range links are hard to learn.

Researchers knew about a helper called attention — a way to look back at other words — but almost always they bolted it onto an RNN, like adding mirrors to a car still stuck in traffic. The paper asks a bold question from its title: what if we keep only attention and throw away recurrence and convolutions entirely?

If that worked, we could go from O(n) waiting steps to O(1) — a constant number — meaning the time to move information doesn't grow with sentence length. The next lab lets you feel that difference.

Try this: Drag the sentence length n from 1 to 40. Watch the orange "sequential" machine light words one-by-one, arrow to arrow, needing n steps. Then switch to the blue "parallel" machine — it lights all words at once in 1 step. Replay the race and see how the speed-up grows as sentences get longer.

002Lesson 2 — The Idea~5 min

A spotlight that mixes meaning

Imagine you could read a sentence not left-to-right, but by shining a spotlight on every word at once — and your question decides what lights up.

That spotlight is attention. The paper defines it this way: you have a question called a Query (Q), a set of labels called Keys (K), and the actual contents called Values (V). Every Value has its own Key, like a library book has a spine label.

Here is the whole idea in one line:

Attention asks: how well does my Query match each Key? Turn those match scores into weights that add to 100%, then mix the Values using those weights. The output is a weighted sum — a blend, not a single pick.

Think of a dictionary. You look up "its" — your Query. You scan all Keys ("The", "cat", "chased", "its", "tail"). The Key for "cat" matches best, so its Value gets the biggest weight. The result is mostly the meaning of "cat", with a little of the others mixed in. Nothing is hidden, nothing is sequential — every Key is checked at the same time.

The paper then uses this lookup in three different ways. Two matter most here:

🔁

Self-attention

All Q, K, V come from the same place. In the encoder, every English word can look at every other English word to fix its own meaning — like figuring out that "its" refers to "cat". In the decoder, each German word can look at the German words before it.

🌉

Encoder-decoder attention

Q comes from the decoder (the German being written), while K and V come from the encoder (the English memory). Every German position can look at all English positions — like checking the whole source sentence before choosing the next word.

Same formula, different sources. Self-attention builds understanding inside one language. Encoder-decoder attention bridges two languages. And because it is just matching and mixing, the computer can do all positions in parallel — no waiting in line.

003Lesson 3 — The Math~6 min

One equation that looks everywhere

Imagine you ask a library question — "where is the verb for making?" — and every book on the shelf shouts back how well it matches. The loudest match gets most of your attention. The Transformer does exactly that, but with vectors and one tidy equation that lets every word look at every other word at once.

The equation

In the paper this is Equation 1. In a simplified version:

$$\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

Here's what each piece means in plain words:

  • Q = Queries — the questions each position asks ("what do I need?"). Packed into a matrix, one row per word, each row has length dk.
  • K = Keys — labels for every position ("what do I offer?"). Same shape as Queries, also dimension dk.
  • V = Values — the actual contents to mix together, dimension dv.
  • QKT = all dot products — how much each Query agrees with each Key. Big number = good match.
  • / √dk = scale — shrink the matches so they don't explode (more on this next).
  • softmax = turn the matches into weights that sum to 1, exaggerating the biggest.
  • … V = weighted sum — blend the Values by those weights. That's your output.
One lookup, everywhere: each row of the result is "what I learned by looking at all positions at once," weighted by how well they matched my Query.

Why divide by √dk?

Dot products add up dk little multiplications. If dk is big (the paper uses 64 per head, 512 total), even random vectors produce huge dots — like 20 or -30 instead of 2 or -1.

Softmax hates huge numbers. It turns [20, 2, 1] into [≈1.0, ≈0, ≈0] — a spike. When softmax spikes, its slope (gradient) collapses to near zero, so the model stops learning. The authors note this: for large dk, dot products "push the softmax into regions where it has extremely small gradients."

Dividing by √dk is the fix. If dk=64, you divide by 8; if dk=256, by 16. That pulls the dots back to a friendly range where softmax stays soft and gradients stay alive. Same idea, much faster than the older "additive" attention that needed an extra neural net to score matches — dot-product just uses optimized matrix math.

Go deeper: If each entry of Q and K is mean 0, variance 1, the variance of their dot product is dk, so its standard deviation is √dk. Scaling by 1/√dk restores variance ≈1, which is exactly where softmax learns best. The paper keeps this scaling in Figure 2 (left) as the "Scale" box.

Now try it yourself: crank up dk and watch the unscaled softmax spike and the gradient ticks vanish — then flip scaling on and see learning return.

004Lesson 4 — Many Spotlights~5 min

Why eight little spotlights beat one big one

One big spotlight can only point one place at a time. If it has to watch the next word and a far-away verb and a pronoun link, it ends up pointing in the middle and seeing nothing clearly. That is what happens with a single attention head.

The paper's fix is simple: use eight little spotlights instead. Each one gets its own learned lenses to look with, then you staple what they saw together.

The formula, in plain words:
$$\text{MultiHead}(Q,K,V)=\text{Concat}(\text{head}_1,\dots,\text{head}_h)W^{O}$$
$$\text{where head}_i = \text{Attention}(QW^{Q}_i,\, KW^{K}_i,\, VW^{V}_i)$$
Think: Query, Key and Value are the question, label and content from Lesson 2. For head i, we first project them to a smaller room with learned matrices WQi, WKi, WVi. Each head then does the same Scaled Dot-Product Attention from Lesson 3, but in its own room. Finally we glue the h results side-by-side and project once more with WO to get back to full size.

In the paper h = 8 heads, dmodel = 512, and each head uses dk = dv = 64. That is 512 ÷ 8 = 64 dimensions per head. The sizes of the lenses are WQi in ℝ512×64, WKi in ℝ512×64, WVi in ℝ512×64, and the final glue WO in ℝ512×512 (because 8·64 = 512 concatenated).

Why not just one head with all 512 dimensions? Two reasons. First, cost stays about the same: eight tiny 64-dim dot-products cost roughly as much as one big 512-dim one. Second, a single head averages everything together. Multi-head lets heads specialize to different subspaces and positions — one can watch neighbours, another can jump to a distant verb like making… more difficult in Figure 3, another can do pronoun links like its in Figure 4.

The paper checked this. In Table 3 row (A), keeping compute equal, a 1-head model got 24.9 BLEU, 4 heads got 25.5, 8 heads got 25.8, and 32 tiny heads dropped again to 25.4. Too few heads blur; too many make each head too tiny (16 dims) to be useful. Row (B) shows cutting dk to 16 or 32 also hurts, so telling if a Key matches a Query is not trivial — heads need enough room.

A bit deeper: smaller dk means dot-products to compare Query and Key have fewer numbers, so compatibility is noisier. The paper notes this hints a more clever match function than plain dot-product might help, but multi-head with 64 dims already works well.

Try it below: give each head a different job, pick a query word, and flip between eight separate heads and one averaged head to see the blur appear and the BLEU drop.

What to try in the lab: Start with the query making and all 8 heads on — notice how different rows point to different words. Now hit “only 1 head” or toggle to 1 averaged head mode and watch the heatmap turn into a flat, spread-out average. Turn heads back on one by one and see coverage and the paper’s BLEU score climb back to 25.8.

005Lesson 5 — Where Are You?~5 min

Adding waves to remember order

Take away recurrence and you take away the clock. An RNN knows “cat” came third because it processed words one, two, then three. The Transformer from Section 3 looks at all words at once. Without help, “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” look the same — a bag of words with no order.

So the paper injects order back in the simplest way possible. Before the first encoder and decoder layer, it adds a small pattern to each word’s embedding. Same size, so they can be summed: word meaning + position signal = input the model actually sees.

You could just learn a table — position 0 gets vector A, position 1 gets vector B, and so on — and the paper tried that. It worked. But the authors chose waves instead:

$$PE_{(pos,2i)} = \sin\big(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}\big) \qquad PE_{(pos,2i+1)} = \cos\big(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}\big)$$

Here $pos$ is the position in the sentence (0,1,2…). $d_{\text{model}} = 512$ in the paper is the vector size. $i$ picks which dimension pair you are in, from 0 to 255. Even dimensions get a $\sin$, odd dimensions get a $\cos$ at the same frequency.

Each pair $i$ wiggles at a different speed. The wavelength goes from $2\pi$ for $i=0$ (very fast) to $10000\cdot 2\pi$ for the largest $i$ (very slow), stepping geometrically. Fast waves tell neighbours apart; slow waves tell you if you are near the start or end.

Why sines and cosines and not a learned table? Two neat properties, both stated in Section 3.5:

  • Relative shifts are linear. For any fixed jump $k$, the vector $PE_{pos+k}$ is a fixed linear rotation of $PE_{pos}$. In plain language: if you know the fingerprint for position 8 and you want position 13, there is a simple matrix that turns one into the other, and the model can learn to use it. That makes “5 words later” easy to detect.
  • It extrapolates. The formula works for $pos=500$ even if you only trained on sentences up to length 100. A learned table has no entry for unseen positions. The paper found learned and sinusoidal encodings scored almost the same (Table 3, row E: 25.7 vs 25.8 BLEU), but picked sinusoids because they might allow the model to extrapolate to longer sequences.
🌊

Think speakers

Embeddings are the song. Positional encodings are two speakers adding a chord: high notes wiggle fast, bass notes wiggle slow. The mix still sounds like the song, but now you can hear when it plays.

📐

Why pairs?

$\sin$ and $\cos$ together act like clock hands. As $pos$ grows, the hand smoothly rotates. A shift by $k$ is just turning the clock a bit — a linear operation the model can learn.

In the lab, scrub the position and watch every dimension light up. Look for how a single offset $k$ rotates the whole fingerprint at once.

006Lesson 6 — The Tower~6 min

How the whole machine fits together

Think of the Transformer as two towers side by side. The left tower is the encoder: it reads your English sentence — all words at once — and turns it into a rich memory called z. The right tower is the decoder: it writes the German sentence one word at a time, always looking back at what it already wrote and at the encoder's memory.

The paper makes both towers from the same simple block, stacked N = 6 times. That's Figure 1.

The encoder: look everywhere, then think

One encoder layer has just two steps, repeated for every position:

1) Multi-head self-attention — every word looks at every other word and mixes meanings. 2) A tiny feed-forward network (FFN) — applied to each position separately.

A shortcut goes around each step, then a normalization cleans it up:

$$\text{output}=\text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))$$

In plain words: add the input x back to what the sub-layer did, then normalize. That residual connection is like keeping the original photo under tracing paper — even a 6-layer tower doesn't forget where it started. To make shortcuts line up, every layer and every embedding keeps the same size: dmodel = 512.

The decoder: same idea, but don't peek

A decoder layer has three steps: a) masked self-attention over words already written, b) encoder-decoder attention where its queries look at the encoder's memory z (keys and values from the encoder, queries from the decoder), and c) the same FFN.

Masking is how it doesn't cheat. Inside the attention softmax, every future position is set to -∞ before the softmax, so its weight becomes 0. Prediction for position i can then only use known outputs at positions < i. The paper also shifts the decoder's input by one position for the same reason — this keeps generation auto-regressive.

A little brain per position

The FFN looks tiny but does a lot. For each position:

$$\text{FFN}(x)=\max(0, xW_1+b_1)W_2+b_2$$

That is: multiply by W1, cut negatives to zero with ReLU (max(0,…)), multiply by W2. The same two matrices are used for every position in a layer, but different layers learn different matrices. Inside the FFN the vectors grow to dff = 2048 then shrink back to 512. You can also think of it as two convolutions with kernel size 1.

Embeddings share weights: the same learned matrix turns input tokens into 512-dim vectors, turns target tokens into 512-dim vectors, and (reversed) turns the decoder's final vector into next-word probabilities. The paper multiplies embeddings by √dmodel so they aren't tiny next to the positional waves.

007Lesson 7 — Faster & Farther~5 min

One hop to anywhere

Imagine you have to pass a secret from the first word of a sentence to the last. In a line of people, the message hops person by person. In the Transformer, everyone can shout directly to everyone else. How many hops does it take? That's the key question of this lesson.

The paper compares designs on three wants. First, work per layer — roughly how many math operations you do. Second, sequential steps — how many steps must wait for the previous one, so you can't run them in parallel. Third, path length — how many layers a signal must cross to connect two far-apart words. Shorter path means easier to learn long-distance links.

$$\text{Self-Attention: } O(n^2\cdot d)\quad \text{Recurrent: } O(n\cdot d^2)\quad \text{Convolution: } O(k\cdot n\cdot d^2)$$

Here $n$ is sentence length, $d$ is the model's width (512 in the paper), and $k$ is a convolution's kernel width. Table 1 in the paper says:

  • Self-attention does $O(n^2\cdot d)$ work, but needs only $O(1)$ sequential steps and $O(1)$ path length. One hop to anywhere.
  • Recurrent does $O(n\cdot d^2)$ work, needs $O(n)$ sequential steps, and $O(n)$ path length. Every new word waits in line.
  • Convolution with kernel $k$ does $O(k\cdot n\cdot d^2)$ work, $O(1)$ sequential steps, but $O(\log_k n)$ path length — you need a stack of layers to connect distant words.
The paper's rule of thumb: when $n < d$, self-attention is actually cheaper than a recurrent layer. With word-pieces and byte-pairs, sentences are often shorter than 512, so that happens a lot. Even separable convolutions cost about $O(k\cdot n\cdot d + n\cdot d^2)$ — still a convolution plus a feed-forward, what the Transformer already has.

For very long texts, full $n^2$ attention gets heavy. The paper notes you could restrict attention to a small neighborhood of size $r$ around each word. That drops work to $O(r\cdot n\cdot d)$, but path length grows to $O(n/r)$ hops — the trade-off of looking only nearby.

Short paths have a bonus you can see. Because every word looks everywhere, individual heads learn different jobs. The paper's appendix shows it: in layer 5, some heads track the verb in "making... more difficult" across a distance, and others do anaphora — the word "its" looking back to what "it" refers to. No recurrence needed to carry that memory.

Try it below. Move $n$ and $d$ and watch the purple self-attention bar fall below blue when $n < d$, and see how the hop-count for attention stays at 1 while the others grow. Look for the $n < d$ crossover — that's exactly when the paper says attention wins on both speed and reach.

008Lesson 8 — The Proof~5 min

Better translations in hours, not months

For years, the best translators were also the most expensive to train — weeks on racks of GPUs, and ensembles of many models voting together. The Transformer paper asked a blunt question: can you translate better while doing less total work?

The team tested on the standard WMT 2014 datasets. English-to-German used about 4.5 million sentence pairs, split with byte-pair encoding into a shared vocabulary of about 37,000 pieces. English-to-French was much larger: 36 million pairs, split into about 32,000 word-pieces. Every training batch held roughly 25,000 words on each side. For a completely different check, they also tried English parsing on the Wall Street Journal treebank — only about 40,000 sentences — plus a 17-million-sentence semi-supervised set.

Training itself was deliberately ordinary. One machine with 8 NVIDIA P100 GPUs. Base models ran 100,000 steps, about 0.4 seconds per step — roughly 12 hours. Big models ran 300,000 steps, about 1.0 second per step — 3.5 days. They used the Adam optimizer with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.98$, $\epsilon=10^{-9}$, and a learning-rate schedule that first warms up then decays:

$$\text{lrate}=d_{\text{model}}^{-0.5}\cdot \min\left(\text{step}^{-0.5},\; \text{step}\cdot \text{warmup}^{-1.5}\right)$$

Here $d_{\text{model}}$ is the width of the vectors — 512 for base, 1024 for big — $\text{step}$ is how many updates you've done, and $\text{warmup}=4000$ steps of steadily increasing the rate. They also used two regularizers: dropping out random connections with probability $P_{\text{drop}}=0.1$ (0.3 for the big En-De model) and label smoothing with $\epsilon_{ls}=0.1$, which makes the model a bit less sure of itself — dev perplexity gets slightly worse, but final BLEU goes up.

The scoreboard: On WMT 2014 English-to-German newstest2014, the big Transformer scored 28.4 BLEU — more than 2 points above the previous best ensemble at 26.36. Even the base model at 27.3 BLEU beat every previous single model and ensemble. On English-to-French it reached 41.8 BLEU (the paper text also reports 41.0 in one place) — a new single-model record — at less than one quarter the training work of the prior best. And it generalized: on parsing it got 91.3 F1 training only on WSJ, beating the classic BerkeleyParser at 90.4, and 92.7 F1 semi-supervised.
A note on cost: BLEU is just a human-designed quality score — higher is better. FLOPs is how many math operations training took. The paper estimates FLOPs as time × GPUs × each GPU's speed. Transformer base used about $3.3\times10^{18}$ FLOPs; big used $2.3\times10^{19}$ — far below many competitors at $10^{20}$ to $10^{21}$, even though its scores were higher. That's why the authors say attention alone suffices.

What matters is not just the numbers, but what they proved: the first model built entirely from attention — no waiting-in-line recurrence, no convolutions — could lead the field and finish in hours. That result turned the Transformer from a translation trick into the blueprint modern AI still builds on.

ABCWord bank

Word bank.

sequence transduction

A system that turns one sequence (like English words) into another sequence (like German words).

RNN / recurrent network

A type of neural network that reads one word at a time, remembers what it saw in a hidden state h_t, and must wait for step t-1 before doing step t. Like an assembly line that can only handle one item at a time.

Query (Q)

A vector sent out to ask: what information do I need? Like looking up a dictionary.

Key (K)

A label on each piece of memory that says what it contains. The Query is compared to all Keys.

Value (V)

The actual information stored with each Key. Once we decide which Keys match the Query, we average their Values.

softmax

A function that turns numbers into probabilities that sum to 1, emphasizing the largest numbers. Used to make attention weights.

dot product

A way to measure how well two vectors point the same way (sum of multiplied entries). Large when vectors agree.

self-attention

Replacing the heavy recurrent connections with attention that lets every position look at every other position at once. Also called intra-attention.

multi-head attention

Running several different attention lookups in parallel, each learning to watch a different kind of relationship, then combining the results.

masked attention / auto-regressive

A mask that hides future words in the decoder so it cannot cheat — prediction for position i can only use positions < i.

positional encoding

Added wiggles (sine and cosine waves) that tell the model what order words are in, since there is no recurrence. Wavelengths go from 2π to 10000·2π.

residual connection + layer norm

A shortcut that adds a layer's input to its output, then normalizes. Helps training deep stacks. Formula: LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)).

BLEU score

A score for machine translation quality invented by humans. Higher is better. The paper reports 28.4 on English-to-German and 41.8 on English-to-French.

FLOPs / training cost

A mathematical measure of work: how many floating-point operations were needed to train. Used to compare costs.
